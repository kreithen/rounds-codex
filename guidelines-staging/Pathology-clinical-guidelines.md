### 1. CAP 2025 Laboratory Accreditation Checklists: Digital Pathology and Patient Self-Collection

*College of American Pathologists · 2025 checklist edition*

**The breakthrough.** The digital pathology section was expanded with new use cases across laboratory disciplines and revised requirements addressing the factors that affect the quality of images and data used for final interpretation. Laboratories must validate or verify digital pathology systems used for clinical diagnosis, including systems used at remote sites, and the Laboratory General checklist now explicitly brings digital pathology within its scope. A new Phase II requirement covers specimens self-collected by patients, including with caregiver assistance, obliging laboratories to provide written instructions addressing the quality issues that could affect testing — vaginal self-collection for HPV being the prominent example.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Turns two practices that had outrun their rules — diagnosing from a screen and receiving a specimen the laboratory never watched being taken — into inspectable requirements.

**Practical implications.** Two concrete jobs follow. Document validation or verification for every digital system used diagnostically, including any pathologist's home or satellite setup, since remote sites are named. And write patient-facing collection instructions that address what actually degrades the specimen, because the instructions themselves are now the accredited pre-analytical control.

[CAP — 2025 laboratory accreditation checklists focus on digital pathology and self-collection](https://newsroom.cap.org/cap-in-the-news/cap-2025-laboratory-accreditation-checklists-focus-on-digital-pathology-and-self-collection/s/72ba1e2c-8c1d-41cf-8f9a-14d004710b05)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — two named requirements not confirmed.** Verified: the 2025 CAP Laboratory Accreditation Program checklist edition does expand digital pathology (new use cases, image and data quality factors, validation or verification of systems including at remote sites, digital pathology brought into Laboratory General) and does add a new Phase II requirement for patient self-collected specimens requiring written instructions. TWO CORRECTIONS. The submission lists 'AI image analysis validation' and 'color calibration' as things the checklist establishes standards for; neither was confirmed as a checklist requirement in this edition, so they are not asserted — CAP's AI validation guidance is a separate and still-forthcoming document (see the AI entry). The verified requirements are quoted instead, which are more useful anyway because they are the ones an inspector will ask about. The submitted url was retained since it resolves to the CAP newsroom item.

### 2. CAP Guidance on Validating Machine Learning in Pathology: A Concept Paper and a Guideline Still Coming

*Archives of Pathology & Laboratory Medicine · Concept paper 2024; full guideline submitted 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The CAP published 'Recommendations for Performance Evaluation of Machine Learning in Pathology' as a concept paper in Archives of Pathology & Laboratory Medicine, and the CAP Artificial Intelligence Committee is developing the full guide covering the entire machine learning life cycle — ideation, model development, validation, deployment, monitoring and reporting — for submission in 2026. In the interim the committee's stated position is that laboratories need not wait to begin using AI, but that full local validation is required even for a vendor tool carrying FDA clearance, approached with at least the rigour of a conventional laboratory assay.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Names the gap it is filling: there are currently no formal guidelines to help a pathology laboratory verify or validate a decision support system, and FDA clearance does not substitute for local validation.

**Practical implications.** Validate any AI tool against your own slides, scanners, stains and patient population before it contributes to a diagnosis, and plan for ongoing monitoring rather than a one-off validation — population drift and analytical drift are the failure modes, and they appear after go-live. Treat interpretive output (Gleason grade, HER2, Ki-67) as needing pathologist confirmation. Note that this is interim expert guidance and a concept paper, not yet a published guideline.

[CAP — recommendations for performance evaluation of machine learning in pathology](https://aplm.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/arpa/148/10/article-pe335.xml)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the guideline described has not been published yet.** The work is real but not at the stage submitted. The submission says 'The CAP Artificial Intelligence Committee introduced comprehensive clinical recommendations governing the complete machine learning lifecycle' and that it 'Mandates local site-specific validation for all commercial and FDA-approved AI diagnostic tools'. What exists is a 2024 CAP CONCEPT PAPER in Archives of Pathology & Laboratory Medicine plus a full guide that CAP TODAY reports the AI Committee will SUBMIT in 2026 — so as of this list there is no published CAP AI guideline and nothing is mandated. The reporting is explicit that no formal guidelines currently exist for verification and validation of these systems. Rewritten to say that plainly. The substance of the submission's advice — local validation even for FDA-cleared vendor tools, monitoring for drift and bias, pathologist confirmation of AI-assisted grading — is accurate as the committee's stated interim position and is kept, correctly labelled as such rather than as a mandate.

### 3. Multi-Tissue Cancer Detection Foundation Models Reach FDA Breakthrough Designation

*Regulatory milestone and vendor validation · 2024 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Paige PanCancer Detect received FDA Breakthrough Device Designation in April 2025 — the first for a multitissue AI assist — for detecting suspicious foci across multiple tissues and organs. Built on Paige's Virchow foundation model, it expanded from 17 tissue and organ types at its early-2024 introduction to over 40, and was internally validated on 21 biopsy and 25 resection tissue types, covering rare variants as well as common cancers. Separately, Ibex Prostate Detect (Galen Second Read) received FDA 510(k) clearance in February 2025.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Moves computational pathology from one-organ, one-algorithm products toward a single model that can be pointed at any slide, which is what a general surgical pathology service actually needs.

**Practical implications.** Understand what the regulatory labels mean before relying on either. Breakthrough Device Designation is a route to expedited review, NOT a clearance or approval and not a finding that the device works; Ibex's 510(k) clearance is real but is for a prostate second-read product, not a 40-tissue model. Local validation still applies. No study has yet shown that either tool reduces turnaround time or diagnostic error in practice.

[Paige — FDA grants Breakthrough Device Designation for AI detecting cancer across anatomic sites](https://www.paige.ai/press-releases/us-fda-grants-paige-breakthrough-device-designation-for-ai-application-that-detects-cancer-across-different-anatomic-sites)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — regulatory status and one product conflated with another.** The 40-plus tissue type claim is real and correctly attributed to Paige: Breakthrough Device Designation April 2025, the first for a multitissue AI assist, expanded from 17 to over 40 tissue types, internally validated on 21 biopsy and 25 resection types, built on the Virchow foundation model. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission presents 'Paige PanCancer Detect and Ibex Galen platforms' as jointly covering 40+ tissue types; Ibex's FDA-cleared product is Prostate Detect (Galen Second Read, 510(k) February 2025), a single-organ second read. (2) Breakthrough Device Designation is not clearance or approval and is not evidence of performance — the submission's 'clinical validation... expanded automated cancer detection' and 'Validates AI as an automated secondary reader' both overstate it, and the validation described is internal. (3) 'Dramatically decreases diagnostic turnaround times... and reduces diagnostic error rates in high-volume biopsy subspecialties' has not been demonstrated and is removed.

### 4. Predicting Genomic Alterations Directly from H&E Slides

*Journal of Clinical Oncology and Medical Image Analysis · 2022 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Deep learning applied to routine H&E whole-slide images can infer molecular features that were previously only detectable by sequencing. DeepHRD, trained on TCGA breast and ovarian cancers, predicted homologous recombination deficiency from held-out H&E breast slides with an area under the curve of 0.81, and was compared against four standard HRD molecular tests in independent platinum-treated cohorts using progression-free survival, complete response and overall survival endpoints. DeepSMILE uses self-supervised pre-training and variability-aware multiple instance learning to classify both HRD and microsatellite instability from H&E in colorectal and breast cancer, and attention-based models have predicted HRD across nine to ten tumour types.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Shows that DNA instability leaves a morphological signature the eye was never trained to read, and offers a triage route in settings where molecular profiling is not universally available — which is the actual clinical problem HRD testing faces.

**Practical implications.** This is pre-screening, not testing. An AUC of 0.81 is a useful enrichment tool for deciding which tumours go first for confirmatory next-generation sequencing; it is not a substitute for a molecular result and must not be used to withhold PARP inhibitor or platinum therapy. No such model is cleared for standalone use, and performance is reported on curated research cohorts.

[JCO — deep learning predicts homologous recombination deficiency and platinum response from histologic slides](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.23.02641)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — supported, with performance quantified.** Verified. Deep learning prediction of MSI and HRD from routine H&E is real and published in strong venues, including DeepHRD in the Journal of Clinical Oncology with comparison against four standard HRD molecular tests in platinum-treated clinical cohorts, and DeepSMILE for MSI and HRD in colorectal and breast cancer. ONE CORRECTION of degree: the submission says these models 'can accurately predict underlying genomic alterations'. The reported AUC for HRD from held-out breast slides is 0.81, which supports triage but not accuracy sufficient to replace sequencing — added, because the submission's 'accelerates precision oncology treatment selection by identifying candidates for targeted therapy weeks before... NGS results return' invites using the prediction as the decision. The entry now states explicitly that it must not be used to withhold PARP inhibitor or platinum therapy. The submission's targetable-mutation claim is broader than what I could verify; MSI and HRD are named instead.

### 5. Multi-Cancer Early Detection: Real-World Performance and the Sensitivity Problem

*Nature Communications and Annals of Oncology · 2021 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Real-world data from over 111,080 individuals tested with a targeted cfDNA methylation multi-cancer early detection assay reported a cancer signal detection rate of 0.91% and an empirical positive predictive value of 49.4%, with 87% accuracy in predicting the anatomical origin of the signal. In the earlier Circulating Cell-free Genome Atlas case-control validation, sensitivity across more than 50 cancer types was 51.5% with specificity 99.5% and tissue-of-origin accuracy 88.7%. In the prospective interventional PATHFINDER study of a real-world asymptomatic population, specificity was 99.1% and tissue-of-origin accuracy 85%, but sensitivity was 28.9%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes molecular pathology laboratories as the confirmatory pathway behind a positive blood signal, and establishes the honest limit of the technology in the same breath.

**Practical implications.** The number to teach is the sensitivity in the population these tests are marketed to: about 29% prospectively in asymptomatic people. A negative result therefore excludes very little, and no patient should be told it does — nor should a negative MCED result displace colonoscopy, mammography or any established screening. A positive result is worth acting on: roughly half are true, with tissue-of-origin correct around 85 to 87% of the time, which makes the diagnostic workup tractable. No guideline currently recommends MCED for population screening.

[Nature Communications — real-world data from over 100,000 multi-cancer early detection tests](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-64094-7)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the decisive number was missing and no guideline endorses these tests.** The studies are real and large: 111,080-person real-world series in Nature Communications 2025 (0.91% detection rate, 49.4% empirical positive predictive value, 87% signal-origin accuracy), the CCGA validation in Annals of Oncology (51.5% sensitivity across 50+ cancers, 99.5% specificity), and the prospective PATHFINDER study. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) THE OMISSION IS THE PROBLEM. The submission says these studies 'validated blood-based cfDNA methylation... assays for detecting early-stage cancers' without any sensitivity figure. Prospective sensitivity in an asymptomatic population was 28.9% — the single most important number for a clinician, because it determines that a negative result is nearly uninformative. Added, with an explicit statement that MCED must not displace established screening. (2) 'Refines guidelines for non-invasive cancer screening' — no guideline recommends MCED for screening; that is stated. The submission's 'spectroscopic liquid biopsy' assays are a separate and much earlier-stage research area and are not asserted as validated alongside the methylation assays.

### 6. In-House Rapid NGS versus Send-Out Molecular Profiling in Solid Tumours

*Journal of Health Economics and Outcomes Research and related series · 2023 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Send-out molecular profiling takes roughly three weeks, with reported ranges of 9 to 30 days. Fast in-house next-generation sequencing has been reported at a 3-day turnaround, including a median of three business days across 578 solid tumour samples at one cancer centre, and an average reduction of 10 days versus send-out. A hospital budget impact analysis in metastatic non-small cell lung cancer modelled a return on investment of $1,022,446 over five years with a 15-month payback period, and the proportion of patients started on targeted therapy rose by 3.38%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reframes molecular turnaround as a clinical variable rather than a logistical one. In a patient who is deteriorating, three days versus three weeks decides whether the targeted agent is ever started.

**Practical implications.** Argue for in-house profiling on time-to-treatment and retention of patients who would otherwise be lost to a second opinion, not on unit cost — the assay and bioinformatics are cheaper per sample, but the up-front investment to establish the capability is substantial and the return in the modelled analysis takes 15 months. Note that the budget impact analysis was specific to metastatic NSCLC and will not transfer unchanged to a low-volume tumour type.

[Fast in-house next-generation sequencing in metastatic non-small cell lung cancer: a hospital budget impact analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10306161/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — supported, with the investment cost restored.** Verified and quantified. Send-out turnaround of roughly three weeks (9-30 days) against 3 days in-house, a median of three business days over 578 solid tumour samples, a 10-day average reduction, a modelled $1,022,446 five-year return with a 15-month payback, and a 3.38% increase in patients on targeted therapy — all confirmed and added, since the submission asserted superiority without figures. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission says in-house testing 'significantly outperforms outsourced laboratory send-outs in clinical decision speed and overall cost-efficiency'. The speed finding is solid; on cost, the sources are explicit that investment costs of establishing in-house NGS are substantial and the saving is a longer-term modelled result — that qualifier is now in the entry, because a resident quoting cost-efficiency to an administrator will be asked about capital. (2) The budget impact analysis was specific to metastatic NSCLC rather than pan-cancer as the submission implies; noted.

### 7. Cryptic PML::RARA in Acute Promyelocytic Leukaemia: When RT-PCR Is Negative and Morphology Is Not

*Case series and systematic reviews · 2024 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Cytogenetically cryptic acute promyelocytic leukaemia occurs in fewer than 1% of cases and has been described with all three PML-RARA transcript isoforms. These rearrangements can escape routine quantitative RT-PCR, and reported cases include a submicroscopic PML::RARA fusion cryptic to both conventional chromosome analysis and qRT-PCR where complex breakpoints left the PCR assay insufficiently sensitive. Unbiased RNA sequencing, optical genome mapping and rapid next-generation sequencing have each resolved such cases.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Sets the rule for a genuine haematological emergency: in morphologically suspected APL with a negative PCR, the negative result does not close the question, and the diagnostic pathway has to escalate rather than stop.

**Practical implications.** Escalate to a method that does not depend on knowing the breakpoint. Targeted RNA fusion assays — including qRT-PCR, targeted RNA sequencing and anchored multiplex PCR — are excellent at detecting low-abundance transcripts of KNOWN fusions, but a novel or uncharacterised breakpoint can be missed by any of them, so unbiased RNA sequencing or optical genome mapping is the appropriate next step. Start ATRA on clinical and morphological suspicion; do not wait for the molecular confirmation.

[Utilization of RT-PCR and optical genome mapping in APL with cryptic PML::RARA rearrangement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11765422/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the recommended assay has the same blind spot as the one it was meant to fix.** The clinical problem is real and well documented: cryptic APL in under 1% of cases, present with all three isoforms, escaping routine qRT-PCR, with resolution by RNA sequencing, optical genome mapping or rapid NGS. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) TECHNICAL, and it matters. The submission recommends anchored multiplex PCR NGS as the answer to fusions 'missed by traditional RT-PCR and amplicon assays'. Anchored multiplex PCR is itself a TARGETED assay: the sources state that qRT-PCR, targeted RNA sequencing and anchored multiplex PCR are all highly effective for low-abundance transcripts of known fusions but that novel or uncharacterised events may be overlooked by these targeted assays. Naming AMP as the escalation would leave a resident with a false sense of coverage; the entry now names unbiased RNA sequencing and optical genome mapping. The submission's own guideline statement — broad RNA-based sequencing when suspicion is high despite negative targeted RT-PCR — was right, and is kept. (2) Journal: I could not confirm Archives of Pathology & Laboratory Medicine as the venue, so it is not named. Added the point about starting ATRA on suspicion, since the delay this entry is about is a bleeding-death delay.

### 8. Transformer-Based Direct Nuclei Detection Replaces Segmentation in Whole-Slide Analysis

*arXiv preprint with public code · 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** CellNuc-DETR adapts the Detection Transformer architecture, with deformable attention for small densely packed objects, to localise and classify cell nuclei in whole-slide images without producing segmentation masks and without the expensive post-processing that masks require. It is twice as fast as HoVer-NeXt, the fastest segmentation-based method, while being substantially more accurate, and about ten times more efficient than CellViT at inference on whole-slide images. Code is publicly available, and follow-on work (LSP-DETR) continues the approach.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Removes the computational bottleneck that kept nucleus-level quantification out of routine throughput. If counting nuclei across a whole slide stops being expensive, quantitative measures such as tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte density become available as a matter of course rather than as a research exercise.

**Practical implications.** This is a research result with open code, not a validated diagnostic product: no regulatory clearance, no clinical outcome data, and the accuracy figures are on public benchmark datasets. Its practical significance for a laboratory is in what it makes affordable — quantitative scoring at scale — and the local validation requirements for any AI tool still apply before such output informs a report.

[Cell nuclei detection and classification in whole slide images with transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06307)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — one performance figure wrong, and the 2026 duplicate of this entry removed.** Real: CellNuc-DETR (arXiv 2502.06307) adapts DETR with deformable attention to detect and classify nuclei without segmentation masks, with public code, and LSP-DETR follows it. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) FIGURE WRONG: the submission claims 'up to a 40-fold reduction in post-processing time'. The reported results are 2x faster than HoVer-NeXt with higher accuracy and roughly 10x more efficient than CellViT on whole-slide inference; the 40-fold figure is not in the work and has been replaced with the actual comparisons. (2) The submission says it 'shifts computational pathology standards' and enables quantification 'directly within routine diagnostic workflows'; this is a preprint with benchmark results and no clinical validation or clearance, which the entry now states. The ECDP presentation could not be confirmed, so the arXiv record is cited instead. (3) DEDUPLICATION: the pathology 2026 submission contains an entry ('Direct Whole-Slide Transformer Models Replace Pixel-Wise Segmentation') making the same claim about the same development. Under the rule applied to neurosurgery, obstetrics and neurology, a development appears under one year only, so that entry has been dropped and 2026 carries nine entries. Its one distinct point — that this removes the need for heavy local GPU infrastructure — is folded into the impact field here.

### 9. The Ferritin Threshold for Iron Deficiency in Anaemia Is 45 ng/mL, Not 15

*Gastroenterology (AGA clinical practice guideline) · Guideline 2020; adoption studied 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The AGA guideline on gastrointestinal evaluation of iron deficiency anaemia makes a strong recommendation, based on high-quality evidence, for a serum ferritin cut-off of 45 ng/mL rather than 15 ng/mL to diagnose iron deficiency in a patient with anaemia. At 45 ng/mL sensitivity is 85% and specificity 92%; at 15 ng/mL sensitivity falls to 59% for a specificity of 99%. Work published in 2025 has examined what adopting the higher threshold does to diagnosis rates.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A laboratory reference interval that flags iron deficiency only below 15 ng/mL misses roughly a quarter of iron-deficient anaemic patients that the recommended threshold would catch. The change is in the reporting comment and the decision-support rule, not in the assay.

**Practical implications.** Check what your laboratory's ferritin comment and any EHR alert actually say, since many still key on 15 ng/mL. Two caveats belong with the number: it applies to the patient who is anaemic, and ferritin is unreliable in chronic inflammation or chronic kidney disease, where serum iron, transferrin saturation, soluble transferrin receptor and CRP are needed to reach the same conclusion.

[AGA clinical practice guidelines on the gastrointestinal evaluation of iron deficiency anaemia](https://www.gastrojournal.org/article/S0016-5085(20)34847-2/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — real threshold, wrong attribution and wrong year.** The 45 ng/mL threshold is real and is a strong recommendation on high-quality evidence, with the sensitivity and specificity figures confirmed (85%/92% at 45, 59%/99% at 15). TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) Attribution and date: the submission credits 'Gastroenterology and Clinical Pathology joint consensus guidelines' that 'formally updated' the threshold as a 2025 development. It is the AGA clinical practice guideline published in Gastroenterology in 2020; what is recent is 2025 work on the effect of adopting it. Presenting a five-year-old recommendation as new would also mislead a laboratory into thinking it had time to comply. (2) The submission's claim that it 'Mandates updated laboratory reference ranges and clinical decision alerts' overstates a clinical practice guideline, which recommends rather than mandates — reworded to what a laboratory should check. ADDED: the inflammation and chronic kidney disease caveat, which the submission omits and which is where the threshold misleads most often.

### 10. Drone Transport of Laboratory Specimens Between Hospital Sites

*Institutional pilot programme, reported in CAP TODAY · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Northwestern Medicine Delnor Hospital, working with Valqari, ran one of the first US hospital pilots of drone delivery for inter-site transport of specimens and medical supplies. The route is a half-mile round trip walked more than 40 times a month, taking about 30 minutes on foot against roughly 4 minutes by drone. The pilot delivered 303 packages with a 100% success rate, no damage and no temperature excursions, and patients needing same-day blood product transfusion benefited. The drones carry no cameras, and flight teams have no access to patient information.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Attacks a specific and unglamorous cause of delayed results — laboratory capability being distributed across campuses so that specimens have to physically travel — with a solution whose numbers are checkable.

**Practical implications.** The relevant problem is the multi-campus health system where the analyser is not where the patient is. Note the actual scope before quoting it: this is a single-hospital pilot over a half-mile inter-site route, 303 packages, not a general capability and not intra-facility transport within one building. The privacy design — no cameras, no patient identifiers available to flight crew — is the part worth copying, because it is the objection that stops these programmes.

[CAP TODAY — privacy, cost, safety: drone use for patient samples](https://www.captodayonline.com/privacy-cost-safety-drone-use-for-patient-samples/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — scope overstated and a regulatory claim not supported.** Real and unusually well documented: Northwestern Medicine Delnor with Valqari, 303 packages at a 100% success rate with no damage or temperature fluctuation, a half-mile round trip taking about 30 minutes on foot versus 4 minutes by drone and walked over 40 times a month, benefit to same-day blood product transfusion, and no cameras on the drones. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission says 'Major academic medical systems (such as Northwestern Medicine) completed pilot programs' — it is one hospital within one system, and Delnor is described as among the first in the US, so the plural overstates adoption. (2) 'Intra-hospital' and 'intra-facility' are wrong: this is INTER-site transport between campuses over a half-mile route, which is a different logistics problem from moving a frozen section within a building — and the submission's headline benefit of 'accelerating intraoperative pathology consults' does not follow from it. (3) 'Defines cold-chain, temperature-monitoring, and biosecurity protocols for airborne medical logistics under CAP and FAA regulatory frameworks' was not supported; CAP TODAY discusses privacy, cost and safety, but no CAP protocol was defined. Removed, and the privacy design substituted since it is the concrete transferable lesson.

### 1. 2026 CAP/ADASP Guideline: Interpretive Diagnostic Error Reduction in Surgical Pathology and Cytology

*College of American Pathologists with the Association of Directors of Anatomic and Surgical Pathology · 2026 update of the 2016 guideline*

**The breakthrough.** CAP and ADASP convened an expert interdisciplinary panel to update the 2016 Interpretive Diagnostic Error Reduction guideline, asking what the most effective ways are to reduce interpretive diagnostic error in anatomic pathology, and reviewing new evidence, changed practice environments and the adoption of digital tools. The update issues two strong recommendations supported by moderate-certainty evidence: pathologists should establish formal procedures for reviewing cases to identify diagnostic disagreements and potential interpretive errors, and those reviews should be completed quickly enough to influence patient management.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Puts the emphasis on timeliness as much as on the existence of review. A discrepancy found after the patient has started treatment is a quality statistic; the same discrepancy found before is a corrected diagnosis.

**Practical implications.** Audit your own department against the second recommendation, which is the one usually failed: review that happens, but too late to change anything. Interpretive diagnostic error means the interpretation of tissue, cytopathology or autopsy material failing to produce a prompt accurate diagnosis, and the consequences named are treatment delay and inappropriate therapy. Note the strength: two strong recommendations on moderate-certainty evidence, not a large prescriptive framework.

[CAP — Interpretive Diagnostic Error Reduction guideline](https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cap-guidelines/current-cap-guidelines/interpretive-diagnostic-error-reduction)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — real guideline, the specific requirements described are not in it.** The guideline is real, jointly sponsored by CAP and ADASP, and is a 2026 update of the 2016 'Interpretive Diagnostic Error Reduction in Surgical Pathology and Cytology' guideline. CORRECTION TO CONTENT. The submission says it 'Formally requires pathology departments to establish mandatory secondary review workflows for complex cases (e.g., first-time cancer diagnoses, high-grade dysplasias) prior to sign-out, as well as systematic peer-review tracking'. The guideline's published content is two strong recommendations on moderate-certainty evidence — establish formal case-review procedures to identify diagnostic disagreements and potential interpretive errors, and complete those reviews quickly enough to positively influence patient management. It does not specify mandatory pre-sign-out secondary review of the named case categories, and a resident citing that requirement would be citing something that is not there. ### 2. 2024 McDonald Criteria: Kappa Free Light Chains Accepted Interchangeably with Oligoclonal Bands

*Lancet Neurology and Nature Reviews Neurology (implementation) · 2024 revisions, published 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The 2024 revisions of the McDonald diagnostic criteria for multiple sclerosis accept CSF kappa free light chain testing as an alternative to oligoclonal IgG bands, usable interchangeably for the CSF-positivity component. Diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of κ-FLC are equivalent to oligoclonal bands at the 95% confidence level, with a κ-FLC index of ≥6.1 performing best. Concordance between the two is around 90%, so in rare discordant cases determining both remains appropriate.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Replaces a manual, rater-dependent isoelectric focusing gel read with an automated quantitative immunoassay of equal diagnostic performance — a genuine improvement in reproducibility and turnaround for a test that gates an MS diagnosis.

**Practical implications.** A laboratory can offer κ-FLC index on an automated platform with faster turnaround and no interpreter variability, and report a numeric index against a ≥6.1 cut-off rather than a subjective band count. Two points of precision matter: the criteria treat κ-FLC as INTERCHANGEABLE with oligoclonal bands, not as a replacement that retires them, and with roughly 90% concordance the two are not identical, so keep oligoclonal band capability available for the discordant or borderline case.

[Nature Reviews Neurology — implementation of the 2024 revision of the McDonald criteria](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41582-025-01141-3)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year of the criteria, and 'replace' is too strong.** Real and correctly identified in substance: the McDonald revisions do accept CSF kappa free light chain testing alongside oligoclonal bands, with equivalent sensitivity and specificity at the 95% confidence level, a κ-FLC index cut-off of ≥6.1 performing best, and the practical advantages of an automated, cost-effective, rater-independent platform. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) These are the 2024 REVISIONS, published in 2025 and presented at ECTRIMS — not '2026 McDonald Criteria Updates' as submitted. Getting the edition right matters because clinicians look these criteria up by year. (2) The submission says κ-FLC 'formally replace' oligoclonal band detection via isoelectric focusing. The revisions make them INTERCHANGEABLE; concordance is around 90% and the sources advise determining both in rare cases. A laboratory that read 'replace' and decommissioned its isoelectric focusing capability would have acted on the wrong word, so the distinction is stated explicitly.

### 3. Pathology AI Is Regulated as Software-as-an-IVD, Not as Software-as-a-Medical-Device

*Regulatory analysis · 2026 snapshot*

**The breakthrough.** FDA's framing treats a computational pathology algorithm as a component of a diagnostic assay rather than as standalone software that happens to read a medical image — the separation that holds in radiology, where the product is software and the image comes from separate equipment, does not hold in pathology. Digital pathology tools used to generate patient results, including primary diagnosis, are in vitro diagnostic medical devices. Post-market expectations under the pathology review office accordingly focus on analytical drift, assay-level adverse events and lot-traceable corrections. Through April 2026 the FDA had authorised 51 AI/ML-flagged devices in pathology-relevant review panels, of which only seven actually analyse whole-slide images.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Determines what a developer must prove and what a laboratory must monitor. Treating the algorithm as part of an assay makes stains, fixatives, scanners and lab-specific protocols part of the device's validated conditions of use rather than incidental context.

**Practical implications.** The practical consequence is specimen-aware validation: performance has to hold across your stains, your scanner and your fixation, and drift is monitored as an analytical property the way it would be for any assay. Keep the 51-versus-7 figure in mind as a corrective to the impression that pathology AI is broadly cleared — most authorisations in these panels are not whole-slide image analysers at all.

[AI/ML in digital pathology and the Software-as-an-IVD paradigm: 2026 snapshot](https://innolitics.com/articles/ai-ml-in-digital-pathology-and-the-software-as-an-ivd-paradigm-snapshot/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — supported, with one framing precision.** Supported. Digital pathology tools supporting generation of patient results are IVD medical devices; the pathology review office's framing treats the algorithm as a component of a diagnostic assay rather than standalone image-reading software, in explicit contrast with radiology; and post-market attention falls on analytical drift, assay-level adverse events and lot-traceable corrections. The submission's substance and its implication — specimen-aware validation across stains, scanners, fixatives and lab protocols — both hold. ONE CORRECTION of framing: this is a regulatory paradigm evident in how the office reviews these products, not a formal 2026 reclassification announcement, and the entry no longer implies a discrete rulemaking event. ### 6. Rapid Whole-Genome Sequencing for Critically Ill Infants

*Pediatric Research and related series · 2023 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Rapid whole-genome sequencing can return actionable findings within 24 to 72 hours in the fastest programmes, reducing time to diagnosis by 77 to 90% against conventional workflows. Real-world turnaround is more variable: a 100-infant study reported a median of 13 days, and a pilot a mean of 169 hours.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Displaces stepwise single-gene testing in the critically ill neonate, where the diagnostic question has to be answered inside the window in which treatment decisions are being made rather than after them.

**Practical implications.** Distinguish the achievable from the typical when arguing for a programme: 24 to 72 hours is what a mature rapid pipeline delivers, while median real-world turnaround in published cohorts is often a week or more, and the gap is logistics — consent, sample transport, analyst availability — not sequencing chemistry. The yield is high enough to justify first-line use, and roughly half of diagnoses change management, which is the number that funds the service. Variant interpretation in an acute setting needs the pathologist, geneticist and laboratory director working together in real time.

### 9. Clinical Decision Support for Laboratory Utilisation Management

*Clinical Biochemistry and the ELMO cluster randomised trial · 2020 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Electronic health record-based decision support for laboratory ordering works through several mechanisms: rules on test frequency such as daily labs, restriction of tests by location or patient population, best practice advisories against unnecessary repeat testing, and removal of pre-checked laboratory tests from order sets. Predictive decision support embedded in the EHR has been developed specifically to target low-yield repetitive testing in hospitalised patients, and clinicians themselves identify culture and simple unawareness of existing repeat orders as the drivers of overuse. The ELMO cluster randomised trial showed that decision support improved the appropriateness of laboratory test ordering in primary care WITHOUT increasing diagnostic error.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The ELMO result is the important one. The standing objection to restricting test ordering is that something will be missed, and a randomised trial found appropriateness improved with no increase in diagnostic error.

**Practical implications.** The intervention with the least resistance and a real effect is removing pre-checked tests from order sets — most repeat ordering is inherited from an order set rather than chosen. Pair any programme with a dashboard tracking total volumes, tests per admission and per admission-day, cost saving and balancing measures, so that harm from under-testing would be visible. Note that published evidence is mostly quality-improvement reports plus one primary care cluster trial, not multi-year outcome studies from academic centres.
