### 1. OPRA Long-Term Results: Watch-and-Wait After Total Neoadjuvant Therapy for Rectal Cancer

*Journal of Clinical Oncology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The randomised phase II OPRA trial's long-term analysis followed 324 patients with locally advanced rectal cancer through a median 5.1 years after total neoadjuvant therapy, comparing induction chemotherapy then chemoradiation against chemoradiation then consolidation chemotherapy, with non-operative watch-and-wait offered to complete and near-complete responders. Five-year disease-free survival was 71% and 69% respectively (P=.68), while TME-free survival — organ preservation — was 39% versus 54% (P=.012), favouring consolidation chemotherapy. Critically, disease-free survival was identical, 64% versus 64% (P=.94), whether a patient went straight to total mesorectal excision after restaging or entered watch-and-wait and had salvage TME for regrowth.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Roughly half of patients keep their rectum without paying for it in disease-free survival, and the sequence of the neoadjuvant regimen — not just its components — determines how many. NCCN and ASCRS now list non-operative management as an option for complete clinical responders discussed in a multidisciplinary setting.

**Practical implications.** Watch-and-wait is a surveillance commitment, not the absence of treatment: of 81 regrowths, 94% appeared within two years and 99% within three, so the schedule is intensive early — endoscopy and MRI at short intervals for the first two years. Salvage TME preserved outcomes only because regrowth was caught while still resectable. Select for it where surveillance is deliverable and the patient will attend; a complete clinical response is not a cure, and the trade being offered is avoiding a permanent stoma and low anterior resection syndrome in exchange for years of scans.

[OPRA long-term results — organ preservation after total neoadjuvant therapy in rectal adenocarcinoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37883738/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND — the submitted trial name belongs to an unrelated study; replaced with the real evidence base.** The submission titled this entry 'Phase III SUNLIGHT Trial: Watch-and-Wait vs. Total Mesorectal Excision'. SUNLIGHT is a real phase III trial, but it is trifluridine/tipiracil plus bevacizumab versus trifluridine/tipiracil alone in refractory METASTATIC colorectal cancer (492 patients, median OS 10.8 vs 7.5 months, HR 0.61) — it has nothing to do with watch-and-wait, rectal cancer or surgery. No Lancet randomised trial of watch-and-wait versus TME could be found; a randomised comparison of the two has never been done and probably cannot be, since randomising a complete responder to surgery has failed to recruit. The actual evidence base is the randomised phase II OPRA trial (long-term results, JCO 2023/2025 analyses) plus the International Watch & Wait Database registry, and the surviving-equal-survival claim is supported specifically by OPRA's 64% vs 64% DFS for planned TME versus salvage TME after regrowth. Entry rewritten to that evidence, with the surveillance burden and regrowth timing added. NOTE: trifluridine/tipiracil has now appeared TWICE in this review as a wrong-drug/wrong-trial title (previously in the ophthalmology list, for ocular surface squamous neoplasia) — worth watching as a recurring source of contamination.

### 2. DIRECT Trial: Robotic Versus Laparoscopic TAPP Inguinal Hernia Repair

*Hernia · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The Danish Inguinal Randomized Controlled Trial (DIRECT) randomised 138 patients to robot-assisted (n=74) or laparoscopic (n=64) transabdominal preperitoneal repair by experienced surgeons. Time from intubation to skin closure and from insufflation to instrument removal were both shorter for the robotic approach, with a larger difference for complicated defects. Pooled against the other randomised evidence — a meta-analysis of three RCTs totalling 300 patients — robotic repair showed a longer operative time that did not reach significance and no significant difference in complication rates.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The randomised evidence for robotic TAPP is thin — three trials, 300 patients — and it does not yet show an outcome advantage over laparoscopy. What it does show is that in experienced hands the robot is not slower, which is the opposite of the usual objection.

**Practical implications.** Treat robotic TAPP as an equivalent option chosen for surgeon ergonomics and case complexity, not as an upgrade you can promise a patient. Nothing randomised supports a chronic-pain or conversion advantage in complex, incarcerated or post-radiation groin defects, and HerniaSurge/SAGES have not defined anatomical criteria mandating the robot. The cost differential is real and the outcome differential is not established, so a unit adopting it should be auditing its own conversion and chronic pain rates rather than assuming the benefit.

[DIRECT — shorter operative times following robotic-assisted TAPP compared with laparoscopic TAPP](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10029-025-03402-y)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND — trial name unresolvable, and the submitted findings are REVERSED against the randomised evidence.** No trial named 'COLA-TAPP' exists in the searchable literature, and no JAMA Surgery randomised trial of robotic versus laparoscopic TAPP could be found. The submission's specific claims — that robotic TAPP 'significantly lowers 30-day chronic pain rates and operative conversion in complex, incarcerated, or post-radiation groin defects' and that HerniaSurge/SAGES have been updated to define anatomical criteria for robot use — are not supported: the 2025 meta-analysis of three RCTs (300 patients) found no significant difference in complications and a non-significant TREND toward LONGER robotic operative time. The real randomised trial in this space is DIRECT (Hernia 2025, 138 patients), whose finding is about operative time, not pain. Entry rewritten to DIRECT plus the meta-analysis, with the unsupported outcome claims removed and the cost caveat kept.

### 3. MERLIN_001: Gene Expression Profiling to Omit Sentinel Node Biopsy in Melanoma

*JAMA Surgery · October 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A nine-centre prospective study applied the CP-GEP test — clinicopathological factors combined with an eight-gene expression profile — in a blinded fashion to 1,761 patients with primary cutaneous melanoma who underwent sentinel lymph node biopsy per current guidelines. It is the largest study of a melanoma gene test performed. CP-GEP stratified T1–T3 melanomas into risk groups with a three-fold separation in sentinel node positivity: 23.8% in the high-risk group against 7.1% in the low-risk group, giving a negative predictive value of 92.9%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** NCCN Cutaneous Melanoma guidelines version 1.2026 name CP-GEP as the first and only molecular test that may be used to inform the sentinel node biopsy decision in selected early-stage patients. Sentinel node biopsy is a staging operation with real morbidity and no demonstrated melanoma-specific survival benefit, so a test that identifies who can skip it changes the consent conversation rather than the cancer treatment.

**Practical implications.** The number that matters is the 7.1% residual risk in the low-risk group, not the 92.9% NPV — they are the same number stated two ways, and the patient hears them differently. Roughly a third of surgical candidates fall into the low-risk group. Position this as shared decision-making: a low-risk result does not exclude nodal disease, and sentinel node status still carries prognostic weight and gates access to adjuvant therapy trials. It is a commercial assay (SkylineDx), so confirm it is covered before promising it.

[MERLIN_001 — gene expression profile–based test to predict melanoma sentinel node status](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamasurgery/fullarticle/2840207)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched — verified, with the risk framing restored.** Verified in full: MERLIN_001, JAMA Surgery October 2025, 1,761 patients, largest melanoma gene-test study, high-risk 23.8% versus low-risk 7.1% sentinel node positivity, NCCN Cutaneous Melanoma v1.2026 recognition. The submission's 92.9% NPV and 'less than 10% risk' are internally consistent with the published 7.1% — 92.9% NPV IS the 7.1% metastasis rate, so nothing was overstated. Two additions: the submission's 'nearly 37% of surgical candidates' spared is plausible but not confirmed in the accessible sources, so it is softened to 'roughly a third'; and the source material's own framing — that SLNB has prognostic value but no universal melanoma-specific survival benefit, so this is for shared decision-making — has been restored, because the submission presented the result as approval to forgo the operation. SSO guideline endorsement could not be confirmed; only NCCN is asserted.

### 4. PREPARE Pilot Trial: Preoperative Beta-Lactam Allergy Delabelling by Direct Oral Challenge

*JAMA Surgery · March 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The PREPARE pilot randomised clinical trial enrolled 150 adults across three tertiary hospitals in Melbourne who reported a beta-lactam allergy and were likely to need intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis for surgery. An anaesthesiologist-led preoperative clinic used a smartphone decision-support algorithm to risk-stratify; of the 28 classified low risk, 93% were successfully delabelled by direct oral challenge. There were no severe adverse events and no surgical delays.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reported penicillin allergy is wrong in the great majority of cases, and the label costs the patient first-line cefazolin prophylaxis. This shows the delabelling can be done inside the existing preoperative workup rather than requiring an allergy referral and skin testing — building on PALACE (JAMA Internal Medicine 2023), which established that direct oral challenge is non-inferior to skin testing followed by oral challenge in low-risk patients.

**Practical implications.** Note the denominator: only 28 of 150 patients were low risk, so this is not a universal screen — it is a pathway for the subset whose history is clearly low risk, and the risk stratification is the hard part. This is a PILOT trial designed for feasibility and safety, not powered for clinical outcomes: it did not measure surgical site infection or C. difficile rates, so the downstream benefit is inferred from cefazolin's known superiority as prophylaxis, not demonstrated here. Do not run direct oral challenges without the risk-stratification tool and resuscitation capability in place.

[PREPARE — perioperative penicillin and cephalosporin antibiotic allergy assessment and testing pilot RCT](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamasurgery/article-abstract/2832026)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — real trial, but design and outcomes were overstated.** The study is real: PREPARE pilot RCT, JAMA Surgery 26 March 2025, 150 patients, three Melbourne hospitals. Four corrections. (1) The submission calls it a 'Phase II multi-center randomized clinical trial'; it is a PILOT trial — feasibility and safety, not powered for clinical endpoints. (2) 'anesthesiologist- and surgeon-led' — the published intervention is anaesthesiologist-led. (3) The submission states the trial 'directly reduc[es] Surgical Site Infections (SSIs) and C. difficile rates'; those outcomes were not measured, and presenting an inference as a trial result is the error class this review keeps finding. (4) The submission omits that only 28 of 150 enrolled patients were low risk and eligible for direct oral challenge, which is the number that determines how much of a surgical list this actually applies to. PALACE added as the RCT that underpins the non-inferiority claim. No SIS or ASA guideline change to this effect could be found; the guideline sentence has been removed.

### 5. Drains After Pancreatoduodenectomy: Selective Early Removal, Not Omission

*Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews / HPB · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The 2025 Cochrane review and a companion meta-analysis of five randomised trials totalling 1,337 patients found no significant difference in morbidity, major morbidity, intra-abdominal abscess, wound infection, haemorrhage or reintervention between drain and no-drain policies overall, with the evidence for 90-day mortality rated very uncertain. The synthesised recommendation splits by operation: a selective EARLY DRAIN REMOVAL policy for pancreatoduodenectomy, and a no-drain policy for left (distal) pancreatectomy. PANDORINA, the binational RCT that stratifies by fistula risk, is the trial designed to settle the high-risk question.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The field has converged on when to take the drain out rather than whether to place one. Early removal — around postoperative day 3 in patients whose day-1 drain amylase is low — reduces complications without the risk of having no access to an undrained leak.

**Practical implications.** Do not read this as licence to omit drains in a high-risk pancreatoduodenectomy. The multicentre RCT of PD with versus without routine intraperitoneal drainage (Van Buren, Annals of Surgery 2014) was STOPPED EARLY by its data safety monitoring board because mortality rose from 3% to 12% in the no-drain arm, with more frequent and more severe complications. A soft gland and a small duct are precisely the setting in which that trial's harm signal appeared. Place the drain, check day-1 amylase, and pull it on day 3 if the number is low — that is the evidence-supported sequence.

[Cochrane — prophylactic abdominal drainage for pancreatic surgery (2025 update)](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010583.pub6/full)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the submission's safety conclusion is the opposite of the randomised evidence in the highest-risk group.** SAFETY-RELEVANT. The submission described a 'landmark international randomized trial published in Annals of Surgery' evaluating early drain removal versus NO prophylactic drain in patients with soft pancreatic texture or small duct — i.e. exactly the high-risk group — and concluded that 'routine prophylactic drain placement in high-risk anastomoses does not prevent Grade C postoperative pancreatic fistulas'. No such trial could be found, and the direction is wrong: the randomised multi-institutional trial of PD with versus without routine intraperitoneal drainage (Van Buren et al., Annals of Surgery 2014) was TERMINATED EARLY by its DSMB because mortality increased from 3% to 12% without drains, concluding that routine elimination of drains in PD increases mortality and the severity and frequency of complications. A trainee reading the submitted entry could omit a drain in a soft-gland Whipple on the belief that a trial showed it safe. The genuine 2025 evidence — Cochrane CD010583.pub6 and the five-RCT meta-analysis (1,337 patients) — supports SELECTIVE EARLY REMOVAL for pancreatoduodenectomy and no-drain only for left pancreatectomy; PANDORINA is the risk-stratified trial in progress. Entry rewritten with the Van Buren harm signal stated explicitly. The submission's day-3 early-removal point is retained because it is correct and supported.

### 6. IntAct: ICG Fluorescence Angiography Did Not Significantly Reduce Anastomotic Leak

*The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** IntAct, an unblinded randomised controlled trial at 28 specialist rectal cancer centres in eight European countries, assigned 766 patients to intraoperative indocyanine green fluorescence angiography or standard perfusion assessment. Clinical anastomotic leak within 90 days occurred in 36 of 343 (10%) with ICG versus 54 of 355 (15%) with standard care — adjusted odds ratio 0.667, 95% CI 0.419–1.060, p=0.087. The result did not reach significance. Grade C leak rates were similar between groups, so any benefit sits in grade A/B leaks.

**Clinical guideline impact.** This is the largest randomised test of a technique already in wide use, and it is negative on its primary endpoint. The AVOID trial in minimally invasive colorectal surgery reached a comparable conclusion. The direction of effect is favourable and the trial may simply be underpowered for a 5-percentage-point difference, but the honest statement is that ICG is not proven to prevent clinically significant leaks.

**Practical implications.** ICG remains reasonable to use — it is cheap, quick and safe, and the signal points the right way — but it should not be presented to a patient or a trainee as an established leak-prevention measure, and it must not substitute for the things that are established: tension-free anastomosis, adequate proximal division, splenic flexure mobilisation where needed, and a diverting stoma when the anastomosis is low and the patient is high risk. Assessment is not standardised across centres, which is the leading candidate explanation for the null result and the reason quantitative protocols are the current research focus.

[IntAct — intraoperative fluorescence angiography with indocyanine green to prevent anastomotic leak in rectal cancer surgery](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/langas/article/PIIS2468-1253(25)00101-3/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the submission reports a significant reduction; the landmark RCT was not significant.** The submission stated ICG 'significantly lowers anastomotic leak rates' and that ASCRS and EAES have issued consensus statements endorsing quantitative ICG perfusion mapping as a standard intraoperative safety tool. Both are wrong. IntAct (Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology 2025, 766 randomised patients, 28 centres, 8 countries) found 10% versus 15% clinical leak, adjusted OR 0.667, p=0.087 — NOT significant; the authors' own interpretation is that a signal was observed and future research must standardise ICG assessment. The AVOID phase 3 trial reached a similar conclusion. No ASCRS or EAES consensus establishing ICG as a standard safety tool could be found. The submitted journal was also wrong: British Journal of Surgery, not Lancet Gastro Hep. Entry rewritten to the actual result, keeping the favourable direction and the standardisation problem, and adding what a trainee should NOT stop doing on the strength of a fluorescence image.

### 7. Antibiotics Versus Appendectomy for Uncomplicated Appendicitis: Long-Term Outcomes

*New England Journal of Medicine / JAMA Surgery · 2021–2025*

**The breakthrough.** Long-term follow-up of the two major randomised trials now quantifies what antibiotics-first buys. In CODA (NEJM), about half of patients assigned to antibiotics had not undergone appendectomy by four years; the trial deliberately included patients with an appendicolith or perforation, which drove its appendectomy rate above earlier work. In APPAC, which excluded those patients, the five-year appendectomy rate in the antibiotics arm was 39% — 61% appendectomy-free. WSES and ACS guidance treats antibiotics as a legitimate definitive option with shared decision-making.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The conversation is now quantitative: roughly half to three-fifths of patients avoid an operation, and the difference between the two trials is patient selection — an appendicolith substantially raises the chance antibiotics fail.

**Practical implications.** One thing the submission got backwards and that matters clinically: non-operative management means the appendix is never examined histologically, so an occult appendiceal neoplasm is NOT excluded — it is left undiagnosed. Incidental neoplasms are found in roughly 1% of appendectomy specimens and more often over age 40, which is an argument for appendectomy or for interval imaging in older patients, not a reassurance. Screen out the appendicolith, set the expectation of a real recurrence rate before discharge, and make sure the patient knows what to come back for.

[CODA — antibiotics versus appendectomy for acute appendicitis, longer-term outcomes](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMc2116018)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — figure overstated and the neoplasm reassurance is REVERSED.** Three corrections. (1) The submission claims 'over 65% of patients treated initially with antibiotics remain successfully appendectomy-free at 5 years'. The published figures are lower: CODA's NEJM longer-term report is at FOUR years with about half avoiding appendectomy, and APPAC's five-year appendectomy-free rate is 61%. No source supports over 65% at five years. (2) The submission asserts no increased risk of 'occult appendiceal neoplasm' — this is backwards. Non-operative management removes the histological specimen from the pathway, so occult neoplasms are not excluded but simply not looked for; this is a recognised concern with antibiotics-first, particularly over age 40, and it has been restored as a caution. (3) The entry is framed as a single 5-year NEJM publication; the evidence is two trials with different inclusion criteria (CODA included appendicolith and perforation, APPAC did not), and that difference is the reason their numbers diverge, so both are now named.

### 8. Endoscopic Versus Surgical Step-Up for Infected Necrotising Pancreatitis

*The Lancet · 2017, with subsequent meta-analysis*

**The breakthrough.** The Dutch multicentre randomised superiority trial across 19 hospitals assigned adults with infected necrotising pancreatitis to an endoscopic step-up approach — EUS-guided transluminal drainage, then endoscopic necrosectomy if needed — or a surgical step-up approach of percutaneous catheter drainage then video-assisted retroperitoneal debridement. The composite primary endpoint of major complications or death at six months was NOT different between the groups. Endoscopic step-up did produce a shorter hospital stay and significantly fewer pancreatic fistulas. Lumen-apposing metal stents have since largely replaced plastic stents for the drainage step.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Endoscopy-first is preferred where expertise exists — but for stay and fistula, not survival. APA/IAP and subsequent guidance position it as the favoured route while retaining the step-up principle itself, which remains the important lesson: drain first, debride only if drainage fails.

**Practical implications.** The choice is driven by anatomy and available expertise. Endoscopic drainage needs a collection abutting the stomach or duodenum and an interventional endoscopist; retroperitoneal collections without a luminal window still go percutaneous or surgical. Do not promise a mortality or organ-failure benefit — the trial did not show one. LAMS carry their own complications, chiefly bleeding from an eroded vessel and buried-stent syndrome, so removal timing needs a plan at insertion.

[TENSION — endoscopic or surgical step-up approach for infected necrotising pancreatitis: a multicentre randomised trial](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(17)32404-2/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — organ-failure benefit REVERSED, journal citation conflated two journals, date wrong by eight years.** The submission described a 'multi-center randomized trial published in Gastroenterology & Annals of Surgery' comparing LAMS endoscopic step-up against minimally invasive surgical necrosectomy, and claimed it 'dramatically reduces systemic inflammatory stress, MAJOR ORGAN FAILURE RATES, post-procedural pancreatic fistulas, and overall hospital length of stay'. The organ-failure claim is REVERSED: the defining trial (TENSION, Lancet 2017, 19 Dutch hospitals) found NO difference in its composite of major complications or death; the benefits were shorter stay and fewer pancreatic fistulas. 'Gastroenterology & Annals of Surgery' conflates two separate journals into one citation — the same error pattern as the ophthalmology list's 'Gastroenterology & Radiology'. The trial is 2017, not a 2025 development, and no 2025 randomised LAMS-versus-surgery trial could be found. Entry rewritten with the correct result and the anatomical/expertise constraints and LAMS-specific complications added; the step-up principle and the fistula/stay benefit are retained because they are real.

### 9. Prehospital Resuscitative Thoracotomy for Traumatic Cardiac Arrest

*JAMA Surgery · February 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A retrospective cohort study reviewed every prehospital resuscitative thoracotomy performed for out-of-hospital traumatic cardiac arrest by London's physician-led prehospital service from 1999 to 2019 — 601 patients. Five per cent survived to hospital discharge; 73.4% of those who did not were pronounced dead at the scene. Underlying causes were exsanguination in 70%, cardiac tamponade in 17.6%, and both in 12.1%. Survival was concentrated in the tamponade group.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Twenty years of data establish that the procedure is feasible in a mature physician-staffed urban prehospital system and that it salvages a small number of patients who would otherwise die at the scene — most clearly when the mechanism is tamponade and no other option exists.

**Practical implications.** Five per cent overall survival is the number to carry, and it comes from a service with on-scene physicians, short urban transport times and two decades of accumulated practice — it does not transfer to a paramedic-only system or a rural one. Selection is the whole procedure: penetrating chest injury with witnessed loss of vital signs and suspected tamponade is the group with something to gain; blunt arrest and prolonged downtime are not. EAST and ACS-COT guidance on point-of-injury surgical resuscitation should be read for the criteria rather than inferred from the headline.

[Prehospital resuscitative thoracotomy for traumatic cardiac arrest](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamasurgery/fullarticle/2830622)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — study design misstated and the comparative claim does not exist in the paper.** The paper is real: Perkins et al., JAMA Surgery, published online 26 February 2025. Two corrections, both about what it can support. (1) The submission calls it 'a high-impact trial' that 'evaluated survival and neurological outcomes following physician-led prehospital resuscitative thoracotomy versus standard emergency department thoracotomy'. It is a RETROSPECTIVE COHORT of 601 London cases over 21 years with NO comparison group — there is no versus. (2) It therefore cannot and does not 'prove that executing immediate thoracotomy at the scene significantly improves neurologically intact survival compared to delayed in-hospital thoracotomy'; that comparison was never made. The published survival is 5% to hospital discharge, which the submission omitted entirely, and the benefit signal is specific to cardiac tamponade. Entry rewritten to the cohort's actual findings with the causes breakdown and the system-dependence caveat added, since a 5% figure from a physician-staffed urban service is not portable.

### 10. De-Escalation of Nodal Surgery in Clinically Node-Positive Breast Cancer

*JAMA Surgery · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A cohort study evaluated oncologic outcomes of de-escalating axillary surgery in patients with clinically node-positive breast cancer who converted to clinically node-negative after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, comparing targeted axillary dissection — retrieval of a clipped, biopsy-proven metastatic node alongside standard sentinel nodes — against completion axillary lymph node dissection. Targeted axillary dissection enabled safe de-escalation in patients with low-volume residual disease, with localisation improving clipped-node retrieval, while completion axillary clearance carried higher morbidity.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Targeted axillary dissection has moved from a technique for reducing false-negative rates to the basis for omitting axillary clearance in selected patients, and SSO/ASCO practice has shifted with it. Lymphoedema, shoulder dysfunction and chronic pain after full clearance are the morbidity this avoids.

**Practical implications.** "Low-volume residual disease" is doing the work in that sentence, and where the line sits is not settled. Alliance A011202 — the randomised phase III trial comparing completion axillary dissection to axillary radiation in patients with a positive sentinel node after neoadjuvant chemotherapy — closed to randomisation in 2022 and its correlative analyses show why the question is live: among patients with ypN1mi disease, 38.4% had additional positive nodes found at completion dissection. Clip the biopsied node at diagnosis; without it targeted axillary dissection is not available later. Confirm the clipped node was actually retrieved before calling the axilla staged.

[De-escalation of nodal surgery in clinically node-positive breast cancer](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamasurgery/fullarticle/2828534)

> **Citation check — ⚠ unresolved on specifics — the study and direction are confirmed, the cohort size is not.** Confirmed: a JAMA Surgery cohort study on de-escalation of nodal surgery in clinically node-positive breast cancer after neoadjuvant chemotherapy exists, and its conclusion — targeted axillary dissection permits safe de-escalation in low-volume residual disease, completion clearance carries higher morbidity, localisation improves clipped-node retrieval — matches the submission's direction. Three things could NOT be confirmed and have been removed or softened: the '976 patients' figure, the '5-year regional recurrence' framing (this is a cohort study, and the accessible sources do not state a five-year recurrence endpoint), and the claim that the study is prospective. Added: it is a cohort study, not randomised, and Alliance A011202 is the trial that will settle the question — its reported correlative finding that 38.4% of ypN1mi patients had additional positive nodes at completion dissection is the concrete reason 'low-volume' needs definition. Added the practical prerequisite the submission omitted: the node must be clipped at diagnostic biopsy or targeted axillary dissection is impossible.

### 1. TRACTION: A Hospital Policy of Tranexamic Acid in Major Noncardiac Surgery

*New England Journal of Medicine · June 2026*

**The breakthrough.** TRACTION was a pragmatic, double-blind, cluster-randomised, placebo-controlled trial across 10 Canadian hospitals testing a hospital-wide POLICY of intraoperative tranexamic acid in major noncardiac surgery. More than 8,000 patients were included, of whom 5,002 (60.5%) underwent oncologic surgery. Red-cell transfusion occurred in 7.4% of the tranexamic acid group versus 9.8% with placebo. The rate of venous thromboembolism was similar between groups.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The reservation about tranexamic acid in cancer surgery has always been thrombosis in an already hypercoagulable population. A trial in which three-fifths of patients had oncologic operations, showing a transfusion reduction with no VTE signal, removes the specific objection that kept it off standing order sets.

**Practical implications.** The design is the point: this randomised a hospital POLICY by cluster, not individual patients, so what it supports is a default protocol rather than a case-by-case decision — which is how the drug actually gets given or missed. The absolute reduction is 2.4 percentage points, so the argument for it is cheapness and safety at scale, not a dramatic effect in any one patient. It does not license tranexamic acid where a specific contraindication exists, and the trial's follow-up for thrombotic events was 90 days.

[TRACTION — hospital policy of tranexamic acid to reduce transfusion in major noncardiac surgery](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2515820)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched — verified, every figure confirmed.** Fully verified and unusually accurate for this review. TRACTION, NEJM published 10 June 2026, 10 Canadian hospitals, more than 8,000 patients, 5,002 (60.5%) oncologic surgery, transfusion 7.4% versus 9.8%, VTE rates similar. The submission's numbers, journal, month and clinical conclusion all match the published trial. One clarification added, not a correction: it is a CLUSTER-randomised trial of a hospital policy, which strengthens rather than weakens the submission's 'universal perioperative TXA as a standard' framing, and is worth stating because it explains why the finding is about protocol design. The absolute risk difference has been stated alongside the relative claim.

### 2. BrIMA: Intraoperative Specimen PET-CT for Breast Margin Assessment

*JAMA Surgery · June 2026*

**The breakthrough.** BrIMA was a prospective, multicentre, interventional NONRANDOMISED trial running June 2022 to March 2025 across six breast cancer centres in Belgium, Germany and Italy, analysing 148 patients undergoing breast-conserving surgery. Ten-minute specimen PET-CT performed in the operating room raised the intraoperative success rate in invasive ductal carcinoma from 83.3% without intraoperative margin assessment to 95.2% with it. Across all subtypes the rate rose from 76.4% to 91.9%. No device-related adverse events were reported.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Positive margins are the leading reason a breast-conserving operation has to be repeated, and re-excision costs the patient a second anaesthetic, a delay to adjuvant therapy and a worse cosmetic result. Molecular imaging of the specimen while the patient is still asleep addresses the problem at the only moment it can be fixed without a second operation.

**Practical implications.** Read the design before adopting: 148 patients, no randomised control arm, and the "without intraoperative margin assessment" comparator is derived from the same specimens rather than a separate group — so the 83.3%-to-95.2% figure is a within-specimen contrast, not a head-to-head. The study was conducted with a single commercial system (XEOS AURA 10) and the manufacturer publicised the result, which does not make it wrong but does mean independent replication and a cost-per-avoided-reoperation analysis are still owed. Ten minutes of added operative time per specimen is the resource question a unit has to answer.

[BrIMA — specimen PET-CT imaging for intraoperative margin assessment in early-stage breast cancer](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamasurgery/fullarticle/2850515)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — numbers verified, but the trial is nonrandomised and the submission did not say so.** Verified: BrIMA, JAMA Surgery 2026, 148 patients, six centres in Belgium/Germany/Italy, June 2022–March 2025, IDC success 83.3% to 95.2%, all-subtype 76.4% to 91.9%, no device-related adverse events. The submission's headline figures are exactly right. ### 3. Metabolic Surgery Versus GLP-1 Therapy: Projected Cardiovascular Risk

*Annals of Surgery · April 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A Mayo Clinic retrospective cohort of 812 adults with obesity compared one-year changes in ESTIMATED 10-year and lifetime atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk after metabolic and bariatric surgery (n=579) against GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy (n=233). Both treatments reduced estimated 10-year ASCVD risk. Projected LIFETIME ASCVD risk fell by 8.6% after surgery versus 1.7% with GLP-1 therapy, alongside mean total body weight loss of 28% versus 11%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It is the first direct comparison of the two dominant obesity treatments on a cardiovascular endpoint, and it separates them clearly in the same direction as the weight-loss difference. The investigators' own framing is that both are effective and surgery gives the greater long-term risk reduction, particularly where weight loss is larger and better sustained.

**Practical implications.** The endpoint is a RISK SCORE, not an event. This study measured one-year changes in ASCVD risk-calculator output in a retrospective cohort — it did not observe myocardial infarctions, strokes or deaths, and a modelled lifetime risk reduction is a projection built on the assumption that the weight loss persists. That assumption is the substance of the comparison: GLP-1 therapy stops working when it stops, and adherence and discontinuation rates in practice are high. Present this to a patient as evidence that surgery produces larger and more durable weight loss with correspondingly larger projected risk reduction, not as evidence that surgery prevents more heart attacks than a GLP-1 — no trial has shown that.

[Mayo Clinic — bariatric surgery versus GLP-1 therapy and projected cardiovascular risk](https://www.endocrinologyadvisor.com/news/metabolic-bariatric-surgery-glp-1-receptor-agonist-therapy-both-reduce-ascvd-risk/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — figures exact, but a modelled risk score was presented as a cardiovascular outcome.** Verified: Ghusn et al., Annals of Surgery, published 20 April 2026, 812 patients (579 MBS, 233 GLP-1 RA), lifetime ASCVD risk reduction 8.6% versus 1.7%, weight loss 28% versus 11%. Every number in the submission matches. The correction is about what was measured. This is a RETROSPECTIVE COHORT of one-year change in ESTIMATED 10-year and lifetime ASCVD risk — a risk-calculator projection, not observed cardiovascular events. The submission's conclusion, that this 'reframes bariatric surgery... as a primary disease-modifying cardiovascular intervention SUPERIOR to long-term GLP-1 monotherapy', overstates a risk-score comparison into a demonstrated outcome superiority, and the investigators' own quoted framing is more measured ('both treatments are effective, but surgery appears to provide a greater reduction'). Entry rewritten to say so, with the durability assumption — which is what the lifetime projection rests on — made explicit.

### 4. MAPLE-PD: Mesenteric (Artery-First) Versus Conventional Pancreatoduodenectomy

*Annals of Surgery · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** MAPLE-PD randomised patients with resectable or borderline-resectable (portal vein involvement) pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma at 24 Japanese high-volume centres to an infracolic SMA-first mesenteric approach or the conventional Kocher-first approach. Median overall survival was 41.7 months with the conventional approach and 39.3 months with the mesenteric approach — comparable, HR 1.02 (95% CI 0.76–1.37), P=0.897 by intention to treat; one-year survival 86.1% versus 82.3% and two-year 70.8% versus 66.6%. A circulating tumour cell substudy did show what the approach was designed to do: mean change in CTC DNA copy number in portal vein blood between laparotomy and specimen removal was +10.1 conventional versus −7.3 mesenteric (p<0.001).

**Clinical guideline impact.** A well-conducted randomised trial of a technical manoeuvre that is widely taught as oncologically superior, and it is negative for survival. What survives is the mechanistic claim — the no-touch isolation really does reduce intraoperative tumour cell shedding into the portal vein — without that translating into longer life.

**Practical implications.** The lesson is where the leverage actually is: with lymphadenectomy standardised, procedural sequence did not change survival, so surgical volume, complete R0 margins and delivery of multidisciplinary systemic therapy are what to optimise. Use the artery-first approach where it helps you assess resectability or control the SMA margin in a borderline case — that is a sound intraoperative reason — but do not offer it to a patient as a survival advantage, and do not let a negative trial of one manoeuvre be read as a negative verdict on the no-touch principle, which the CTC data support.

[MAPLE-PD — mesenteric versus conventional approach during pancreatoduodenectomy for PDAC](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40772617/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — direction right, date wrong, and the positive substudy was omitted.** Verified: MAPLE-PD, Annals of Surgery, 24 Japanese high-volume centres, protocol target 354 patients, median OS 41.7 (conventional) versus 39.3 months (mesenteric), HR 1.02, P=0.897; the trial concluded the mesenteric approach does not improve survival. The submission got the DIRECTION right, which is notable — most submissions in this review overstated. Three corrections: (1) it is a 2025 publication (PubMed 40772617), not 'Annals of Surgery, 2026'; (2) the submission omits the CTC substudy, which is the trial's positive finding and the reason the no-touch rationale is not refuted — worth including precisely because it stops the entry reading as 'artery-first is pointless'; (3) 'resectable/borderline' is specified in the trial as borderline by PORTAL VEIN involvement (BR-PV), not borderline by arterial abutment, which is a different and more difficult group. The submission's own inference — that volume, systemic therapy and R0 outweigh procedural sequence — is the trial authors' conclusion and has been kept.

### 5. Treatment Delay Thresholds and Metastasis Risk in Colorectal Cancer

*JAMA Network Open · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A cohort study of 11,927 insured patients with nonmetastatic colorectal cancer used machine learning to look for time-to-treatment-initiation thresholds beyond which three-year systemic metastasis risk rises, analysing four care pathways separately: surgery alone, neoadjuvant then surgery, surgery then adjuvant therapy, and trimodality therapy. In the surgery-then-adjuvant pathway, starting treatment 4–46 days after diagnosis carried a 27% higher metastasis risk than starting within 3 days, rising to 55% higher beyond 46 days. The thresholds differed by pathway rather than following a single deadline.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It argues against the arbitrary uniform 30-day target and for pathway-specific timelines, and it puts a number on the cost of administrative drift between diagnosis and treatment.

**Practical implications.** Read the reference group before acting on the 27%. That figure compares everything from day 4 onward against treatment started within THREE DAYS of diagnosis — a window almost no elective colorectal pathway can meet, and one that in a real cohort is largely occupied by patients presenting acutely with obstruction or perforation who go straight to theatre. Treating "beyond 4 days" as a harm threshold would be a misreading, and pursuing it would mean operating before staging is complete. The defensible use of this study is the upper threshold — delay past about six to seven weeks is where risk climbs steeply — and its actual message, which is that the deadline that matters depends on which pathway the patient is on. This is a retrospective claims-based cohort in an insured population, so residual confounding by acuity and comorbidity is unresolved.

[Colorectal cancer treatment delay thresholds and metastasis risk](https://ascopost.com/news/july-2026/treatment-delays-may-increase-metastasis-risk-in-nonmetastatic-colorectal-cancer/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — journal wrong, and the '4 days' threshold is a misread reference group that would drive unsafe practice.** SAFETY-RELEVANT FRAMING. The study is real: Nguyen et al., cohort of 11,927 insured patients with nonmetastatic colorectal cancer, machine-learning TTI thresholds across four treatment pathways. Corrections. (1) Journal: JAMA NETWORK OPEN, not JAMA Surgery as submitted. (2) The submission states 'treatment delays beyond 4 days were associated with a 27% increase in metastasis'. The published comparison is initiation at 4–46 days versus WITHIN 3 DAYS — the 27% is relative to a three-day reference, not a harm threshold at day 4. Presented as submitted, a reader would conclude that a colorectal cancer must be treated within four days of diagnosis, which is not achievable in an elective pathway, would preclude complete staging and multidisciplinary discussion, and would be actively worse care. The reference group in a real cohort is dominated by acute obstructive/perforated presentations, which is a confounder, not a target. (3) '55% if delays exceeded 47 days' is approximately right (beyond 46 days) and has been retained as the usable finding. (4) The submission's 'Multi-Cohort Pathway Threshold Study' framing obscures that this is retrospective claims data in an insured population; stated plainly.

### 6. E-AHPBA–ESSO–Innsbruck Consensus on Peri- and Postoperative Management After Liver Resection

*British Journal of Surgery · January 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A European consensus developed by hepatobiliary surgeons, anaesthetists, hepatologists, a nurse and a physiotherapist used a modified Delphi process with a 70% agreement threshold to produce 32 statements across six topics: thromboprophylaxis, antibiotics, prehabilitation–nutrition–mobilisation, bile leaks including bilio-enteric anastomosis, post-hepatectomy haemorrhage, and post-hepatectomy liver failure. Screening reduced 6,514 publications to 204 included; the median evidence level of the statements was 2. Strong recommendations were formulated for prehabilitation, early postoperative mobilisation and AVOIDANCE OF ROUTINE DRAIN USE; the weakest evidence was found for perioperative thromboprophylaxis and for management of post-hepatectomy liver failure.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It replaces heterogeneous local protocols with one European framework, and it is honest about where the evidence runs out — naming thromboprophylaxis and post-hepatectomy liver failure as the two areas where practice is running ahead of data and multicentre studies are needed.

**Practical implications.** The three strong recommendations are the ones to act on, and one of them is a deprescribing decision: do not place a drain routinely after liver resection. Prehabilitation and early mobilisation are cheap, unglamorous and the best-supported items in the document. Where a statement rests on level-2 evidence with 70% panel agreement — which is most of them — it is guidance for standardisation, not a standard of care to be audited against.

[E-AHPBA–ESSO–Innsbruck consensus recommendations on peri- and postoperative management following liver resection](https://academic.oup.com/bjs/article/113/1/znaf272/8407252)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — right document, wrong recommendations attributed to it.** The document is real and correctly cited: BJS January 2026, volume 113 issue 1, znaf272, E-AHPBA–ESSO–Innsbruck consensus. The problem is the content. The published consensus covers PERI- AND POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT — thromboprophylaxis, antibiotics, prehabilitation/nutrition/mobilisation, bile leaks, post-hepatectomy haemorrhage and PHLF — not operative technique; parenchyma-sparing resection, intraoperative ultrasound and low-CVP anaesthesia are not among its topics. ### 7. PSOGI Consensus on Minimally Invasive Surgery for Peritoneal Surface Malignancy

*British Journal of Surgery · March 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The Peritoneal Surface Oncology Group International published consensus recommendations on minimally invasive — laparoscopic and robotic — surgery for peritoneal surface malignancy, covering selection criteria for minimally invasive cytoreductive surgery with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy and the diagnostic and staging roles of laparoscopy. Where evidence was insufficient the panel recorded no agreement rather than manufacturing one: for low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm type II lesions there was no consensus that surgery is preferable to observation, though if surgery is undertaken it should include resection of the appendiceal stump and the right iliac fossa peritoneum.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Minimally invasive cytoreduction has been spreading ahead of any evidence base. Restricting it to carefully selected low-Peritoneal-Cancer-Index disease at expert centres puts a boundary on adoption before the technique is applied to burdens of disease it cannot clear.

**Practical implications.** The governing principle in cytoreductive surgery is completeness of cytoreduction — an incomplete resection through a laparoscopic approach is worse than an open complete one, and there is no partial credit. Low PCI, expert centre, and a firm intention to convert are the conditions. Note also that this consensus is as useful for what it refuses to recommend: 'no agreement' on operating versus observing a LAMN II is a legitimate answer to take into a clinic, not a gap to fill with local preference.

### 9. Remote Perioperative Telemonitoring in Cancer Surgery

*npj Digital Medicine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A randomised trial assigned 293 patients undergoing gastrointestinal, genitourinary or gynaecological cancer surgery to remote perioperative monitoring or surgeon-only care. The monitored arm wore a wristband accelerometer and reported symptoms through an app before surgery and at days 7, 14, 30, 60 and 90 after discharge, with triage nurses telephoning when data crossed predetermined thresholds. Functional recovery rate was 6% greater in the monitored arm (p=0.036); symptom severity differed significantly at day 90 and symptom interference with daily activities at days 14 and 90, both favouring monitoring.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It establishes a modest but real benefit for structured post-discharge monitoring in cancer surgery, and it identifies the active ingredient: the earlier feasibility work found adherence depended on the nurse-supported arm, where patients received a phone call when data were missing. The technology is the sensor; the intervention is the person who rings.

**Practical implications.** Six per cent on functional recovery is worth having and is not a transformation — set expectations accordingly before buying a platform. The trial did not demonstrate early detection of anastomotic leak, a reduction in 30-day readmissions, or shortened time to adjuvant therapy; those remain plausible mechanisms, not findings. Before implementing, decide who is monitoring the alerts out of hours and what the escalation pathway is: an unread threshold breach is worse than no monitoring, because it creates a record that a deterioration was flagged and not acted on.

### 10. EHS/AHS Guideline on Abdominal Wall Closure: Small Bites and Prophylactic Mesh

*British Journal of Surgery · 2022, current standard*

**The breakthrough.** The joint European and American Hernia Societies guideline on closure of abdominal wall incisions recommends, for elective midline laparotomy, a continuous small-bites technique with a suture-to-wound-length ratio of at least 4:1, using a slowly absorbable monofilament in a single-layer aponeurotic closure without separate peritoneal closure, and avoiding rapidly absorbable sutures. Small bites means tissue bites of 5–9 mm taking aponeurosis only, with stitches 5 mm apart. Prophylactic mesh augmentation is judged effective and safe and is suggested for high-risk patients — abdominal aortic aneurysm surgery and obesity are the named groups.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It converts closure from a matter of individual habit into a specified technique with a measurable target. The evidence base is the STITCH randomised trial, which compared small bites against large bites for midline closure and found fewer incisional hernias at one year in the small-bites arm.

**Practical implications.** The 4:1 ratio is the checkable part — it means roughly four times as much suture as wound length, which in practice is far more stitches than most closures use, and a surgeon who believes they already close with small bites usually is not. Count it once against a measured wound to calibrate. The technique takes longer, and that is the trade. For prophylactic mesh, the recommendation is 'suggested in high-risk patients', a conditional recommendation — not routine, and emergency laparotomy is a separate question with its own trials still reporting.

[Updated guideline for closure of abdominal wall incisions from the European and American Hernia Societies](https://academic.oup.com/bjs/article/109/12/1239/6677383)
