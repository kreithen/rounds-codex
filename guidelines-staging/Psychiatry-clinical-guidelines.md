### 1. Esketamine Nasal Spray as Monotherapy for Treatment-Resistant Depression

*Pivotal trial NCT04599855; FDA approval January 2025 · 21 January 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial randomised 378 adults with treatment-resistant depression to esketamine 56 mg, 84 mg or placebo nasal spray twice weekly for four weeks, with no newly initiated oral antidepressant. Both doses beat placebo on the primary endpoint — least-squares mean differences of −5.1 and −6.8 points, each P < 0.001 — with separation as early as 24 hours. Remission at week 4 was 22.5% on esketamine against 7.6% on placebo. The FDA approved Spravato as the first and only monotherapy for adults with major depressive disorder who have had an inadequate response to at least two oral antidepressants.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Removes the requirement to start a new oral antidepressant alongside esketamine, which had forced a fresh trial of a drug class the patient had already failed twice by definition.

**Practical implications.** Monotherapy does not mean simpler. Esketamine remains a REMS-restricted drug given in a certified healthcare setting with at least two hours of post-dose observation for sedation, dissociation and blood pressure elevation — so the visit burden that deters patients is unchanged. Set expectations from the absolute figures: about one patient in four to five reaches remission at four weeks, against roughly one in thirteen on placebo. The benefit of dropping the oral antidepressant is avoiding its weight gain, sexual dysfunction and GI effects, not avoiding monitoring.

[Johnson & Johnson — Spravato approved in the US as the first and only monotherapy for adults with treatment-resistant depression](https://www.jnj.com/media-center/press-releases/spravato-esketamine-approved-in-the-u-s-as-the-first-and-only-monotherapy-for-adults-with-treatment-resistant-depression)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — journal unconfirmed, and the monitoring burden was omitted.** Verified: the FDA approved Spravato as the first and only monotherapy for TRD on 21 January 2025, on the basis of NCT04599855 — 378 adults, esketamine 56 mg or 84 mg versus placebo twice weekly for four weeks, LS mean differences −5.1 and −6.8 (P<.001), separation by 24 hours, remission 22.5% versus 7.6%. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) JOURNAL NOT CONFIRMED: the submission cites JAMA Psychiatry; I could not verify the publication venue, so the trial registration and the FDA approval are cited instead. (2) The effect sizes and remission rates were absent — 22.5% remission is a meaningful but modest absolute figure and belongs in any patient conversation. (3) The submission says monotherapy 'reduces polypharmacy burdens' and lists the oral-antidepressant side effects avoided, without noting that esketamine is REMS-restricted, given in a certified setting with two hours of post-dose observation for sedation, dissociation and blood pressure rise. A clinician told only the first half would misjudge what they are offering. Note also that TRD3005 — a trial name adjacent to this programme — studied esketamine WITH an oral antidepressant in patients 65 and over, and is not the monotherapy evidence.

### 2. Lithium versus Quetiapine Augmentation for Treatment-Resistant Depression (LQD)

*The Lancet Psychiatry · April 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A pragmatic, open-label, parallel-group randomised superiority trial at six NHS trusts in England enrolled 212 adults with treatment-resistant depression (mean age 42.4, 46% men, 89% White) to quetiapine (n=107) or lithium (n=105) augmentation, followed for 52 weeks. Quetiapine augmentation reduced depressive symptoms more than lithium, was more cost-effective, and had comparable safety. Discontinuation was high in both arms: about 50% of those starting lithium and 39% of those starting quetiapine stopped within 12 months, usually because of side effects.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first long-term head-to-head comparison of the two standard augmentation strategies, run in ordinary NHS practice rather than a specialist research setting — which is what makes it applicable.

**Practical implications.** Quetiapine is a defensible first choice for augmentation on this evidence. Two things temper it. The trial was open-label, so patients and clinicians knew the allocation, and the outcome is self-reported symptom severity. And the monitoring argument only half holds: quetiapine avoids lithium's renal and thyroid surveillance but brings metabolic monitoring of its own, plus sedation and weight gain — which is why nearly two in five stopped it. Expect roughly half of patients to be off whichever drug you choose within a year, and plan the review accordingly.

[The Lancet Psychiatry — clinical and cost-effectiveness of lithium versus quetiapine augmentation for treatment-resistant depression](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366(25)00028-8/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the trial acronym is invented, and the design was open-label.** The trial is real and the direction is right: Cleare et al., The Lancet Psychiatry, April 2025, doi 10.1016/S2215-0366(25)00028-8, PMID 40350798, with quetiapine more clinically and cost-effective than lithium over 52 weeks. FOUR CORRECTIONS. (1) NAME: the submission calls it the 'LITH-QUE Trial'. It is the LQD trial — an invented acronym sends a reader looking for a study that does not exist under that name. (2) DESIGN: submitted as giving 'high-level, long-term comparative data'; it is pragmatic and OPEN-LABEL, not blinded, with 212 participants at six sites, 89% White. (3) The discontinuation figures — roughly 50% on lithium and 39% on quetiapine stopping within a year, usually for side effects — were missing and are the most practically important result. (4) The claim that quetiapine avoids 'the intensive laboratory monitoring (renal/thyroid) required for lithium' is only half the picture, since quetiapine carries metabolic monitoring, sedation and weight gain. The submitted assertion that this 'elevates atypical antipsychotic augmentation... in major depressive disorder practice guidelines' could not be confirmed and is not asserted.

### 3. Serial Ketamine Infusions Added to Inpatient Care for Depression (KARMA-Dep 2)

*JAMA Psychiatry · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** An investigator-led, double-blind, midazolam-controlled pragmatic trial at an academic centre in Ireland, running September 2021 to August 2024, randomised adults hospitalised with a DSM-5 major depressive episode — unipolar or bipolar — and a baseline MADRS of 20 or more, 1:1 to up to eight infusions of ketamine or midazolam twice weekly over four weeks. There was no statistically significant difference in end-of-treatment MADRS, and no differences on secondary efficacy, cognitive, economic or quality-of-life outcomes.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A well-designed negative trial in the setting where enthusiasm for ketamine runs highest. Midazolam as comparator matters: it produces enough psychoactive effect to preserve blinding, which unblinded saline-controlled trials cannot do — and much of ketamine's apparent advantage elsewhere may live in that gap.

**Practical implications.** Do not build an inpatient depression pathway around serial ketamine infusions expecting them to outperform the rest of the admission. This does not speak to single-dose ketamine for acute suicidality, to esketamine in the outpatient TRD setting, or to ECT. It is one academic centre, and it included bipolar as well as unipolar episodes, so read it as evidence about adjunctive serial infusions in hospital rather than about ketamine generally.

[JAMA Psychiatry — serial ketamine infusions as adjunctive therapy to inpatient care for depression: the KARMA-Dep 2 randomized clinical trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2840552)

> **Citation check — matched.** Verified and accurately summarised, including the negative primary outcome and the use of midazolam as an active comparator: JAMA Psychiatry, PMID 41123905, investigator-led double-blind midazolam-controlled pragmatic trial at an academic centre in Ireland, September 2021 to August 2024, up to eight infusions twice weekly over four weeks, baseline MADRS ≥20, no significant difference in end-of-treatment MADRS or in secondary efficacy, cognitive, economic or quality-of-life outcomes. Details added rather than corrected: the single-centre design, the inclusion of bipolar as well as unipolar episodes, and the infusion schedule. The submission's framing that the trial highlights 'the significant role of unblinding and expectancy effects in IV ketamine trials' is a fair inference — midazolam was chosen to preserve blinding — but is presented here as the interpretation it is rather than as a finding. Also bounded what the trial does not address (single-dose ketamine for suicidality, outpatient esketamine, ECT), since the submission's practical advice reads more broadly than the evidence.

### 4. Maintenance Pharmacotherapy in Bipolar Disorder: Swedish Nationwide Cohort

*Nationwide register-based cohort study · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A within-individual analysis of a nationwide Swedish cohort of people with bipolar disorder examined which maintenance treatments were associated with reduced hospitalisation. Lithium monotherapy was associated with lower risk of depression-related, mania-related and somatic hospitalisation compared with periods of non-use, while antidepressant monotherapy was associated with increased risk.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Within-individual designs compare a person with themselves on and off treatment, which removes the confounding by severity that cripples between-person comparisons in psychiatry — patients on lithium differ systematically from those who are not. That makes this the strongest observational support available for lithium's maintenance role.

**Practical implications.** Two actions follow. Offer lithium maintenance rather than deferring it, including earlier in the illness course than habit suggests. And do not leave a patient with bipolar depression on an unopposed SSRI or SNRI — the signal here is towards harm, and it aligns with existing guidance against antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar disorder. Note this is observational: within-individual analysis handles stable confounders, not time-varying ones such as a clinician switching drugs because the illness is worsening.

[Real-world effectiveness of pharmacological maintenance treatment in bipolar disorder](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=lithium+bipolar+maintenance+within-individual+nationwide+Swedish+cohort+hospitalisation)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — direction consistent with established evidence.** I did not verify this specific study, including the cohort size of over 105,000, the journal (submitted as The Lancet Psychiatry) or the individual hospitalisation outcomes, so none of those is asserted. The findings as described are consistent with a substantial existing literature from Swedish and Finnish national registers using within-individual designs, and with guideline positions on lithium as first-line maintenance and against antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar disorder — so the direction is credible even though the citation is unconfirmed. Rewritten to attribute the design and direction without the unverified specifics, and the limits of a within-individual observational design added, since the submission presents it as settling the question. Flagged for the physician to confirm the primary citation.

### 5. Xanomeline-Trospium (Cobenfy) in Schizophrenia: Implementation Guidance

*Expert consensus and post-approval implementation literature · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Xanomeline-trospium is the first antipsychotic approved for schizophrenia that does not work by blocking dopamine D2 receptors. Xanomeline is an M1/M4-preferring muscarinic agonist acting centrally; trospium is a peripherally restricted muscarinic ANTAGONIST added specifically to blunt the cholinergic side effects xanomeline causes outside the brain. Consensus and implementation work has followed the approval to guide practical use.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first genuinely new mechanism in antipsychotic pharmacology in decades, which matters most for patients in whom D2 blockade is intolerable rather than ineffective.

**Practical implications.** Get the pharmacology right, because the combination is an agonist plus an antagonist, not a dual agonist — trospium exists to make xanomeline tolerable. And weigh the whole side-effect profile: avoiding extrapyramidal effects, tardive dyskinesia and hyperprolactinaemia is real, but the drug has a substantial gastrointestinal burden — nausea, vomiting, dyspepsia and constipation are among the commonest reasons for discontinuation — and hepatic safety warrants attention. Positioning it as an early-line option is a reasonable clinical argument but is not yet a guideline recommendation.

[Cobenfy (xanomeline and trospium chloride) prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm?event=overview.process&ApplNo=217704)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the mechanism was misdescribed and the side-effect claim is one-sided.** The drug is real and the significance is genuine: xanomeline-trospium (Cobenfy) is the first schizophrenia treatment approved that does not act through dopamine D2 blockade. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) MECHANISM: the submission calls it 'the first dual M1/M4 muscarinic receptor agonist'. That describes xanomeline alone. Trospium is a peripherally restricted muscarinic ANTAGONIST, included to reduce peripheral cholinergic adverse effects — so the product is an agonist-plus-antagonist combination, and describing it as a dual agonist misrepresents why the second molecule is there. (2) The submission says it treats positive, negative and cognitive symptoms 'without exposing patients to extrapyramidal side effects, tardive dyskinesia, hyperprolactinemia, or metabolic weight gain' — listing only the harms avoided. The gastrointestinal burden is substantial and is a leading cause of discontinuation, and hepatic safety requires attention; both are now stated. The specific consensus panel report in The Journal of Clinical Psychiatry could not be verified, so the entry cites the prescribing information and describes the implementation literature generically. Its claim that guidelines now position muscarinic agonists as early-line is not asserted.

### 6. Transdiagnostic Prediction of Psychosis and Bipolar Disorder in Young People

*Clinical prediction modelling literature · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Work in this area develops machine-learning prediction models on routinely collected electronic health record data to estimate an individual young person's risk of developing a psychotic disorder and bipolar disorder together, rather than running separate single-disorder risk instruments.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Matches the way early psychosis and bipolar disorder actually present. Prodromal states are not diagnostically clean, and a young person referred for concerning symptoms does not arrive pre-sorted into the pathway that will eventually fit them.

**Practical implications.** Treat this as a research direction, not a tool to adopt. Prediction models built on one health system's records characteristically lose accuracy elsewhere, and a risk score that identifies a young person as high-risk is only useful where a preventive intervention and a service to deliver it exist. There is also a real harm to weigh — telling an adolescent they are at high risk of psychosis carries stigma and iatrogenic anxiety, which is why prodromal risk labelling remains contested. I could not confirm the specific validated model the submission describes, nor that early-intervention guidelines have moved to transdiagnostic screening.

[Transdiagnostic risk prediction in youth mental health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=transdiagnostic+clinical+prediction+model+psychosis+bipolar+electronic+health+records+youth)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — plausible field, specific model and guideline shift not confirmed.** Transdiagnostic clinical prediction modelling in youth mental health is a real and active field, and the rationale the submission gives is sound. But I did not verify the specific validated model described, nor its publication in The Lancet Psychiatry, nor the claim that this 'transitions early-intervention guidelines away from single-disorder assessment tools toward transdiagnostic risk-screening frameworks' — that is a substantial claim about guideline change and no supporting document was found. Rewritten to describe the approach without asserting a specific validated instrument. Two limitations added that the submission omits and that determine whether such a model helps anyone: external validity across health systems, and the recognised harms of risk labelling in adolescents, which is why prodromal designation remains contested. ### 7. Psilocybin with Psychological Support in Treatment-Resistant Depression: Longer-Term Follow-Up

*Follow-up of phase 2b trial cohorts · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Follow-up of participants from psilocybin phase 2b trials in treatment-resistant depression reports on how many maintain response or remission over an extended period after one or two 25 mg doses given with psychological support.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Durability is the question that decides whether psychedelic-assisted therapy is a treatment or an experience. Acute antidepressant effects were established some time ago; whether they persist without redosing is what regulators and guideline committees need.

**Practical implications.** Read naturalistic follow-up carefully. Participants who remain in contact are not a random subset of those originally randomised, other treatment continues during follow-up, and there is no control group beyond the trial window — so a maintained-response figure is not the same as a demonstrated 12-month drug effect. Psilocybin is not approved for depression, and outside a trial this cannot be offered. The submission's inference that structured preparation and integration sessions are essential to durability is plausible and is how the trials were designed, but it was not tested against psilocybin given without them.

[Psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression: phase 2b programme and follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=psilocybin+25+mg+treatment-resistant+depression+phase+2b+long-term+follow-up)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — journal name appears garbled.** The phase 2b psilocybin programme in treatment-resistant depression using 25 mg with psychological support is real and well known, and extended follow-up reports exist. I did not verify the specific 12-month follow-up publication or its findings, so no response or remission figure is quoted. The submitted journal, 'Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics', appears to be a garbled rendering of Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics — the citation could not be resolved either way, so it is not named. Rewritten to describe the design and to set out why naturalistic follow-up overstates durability: differential retention, continued concurrent treatment, and absence of a control group beyond the randomised window. Also stated that psilocybin is not approved for depression, which the submission's practical field does not mention. Its claim about preparation and integration sessions being 'essential' is flagged as untested rather than removed.

### 8. CT-155, a Prescription Digital Therapeutic for Negative Symptoms of Schizophrenia (CONVOKE)

*Trial programme; protocol published in JMIR Research Protocols · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** CT-155 is a mobile prescription digital therapeutic developed to target experiential negative symptoms of schizophrenia — avolition and asociality — as an adjunct to antipsychotic treatment. CONVOKE is the trial evaluating it.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Experiential negative symptoms are the domain antipsychotics treat least well and the one that most determines whether someone works, studies or maintains relationships. A treatment aimed specifically at that gap is worth following.

**Practical implications.** Nothing to prescribe yet, and the citation matters here: the submission's source is JMIR Research Protocols, which publishes trial PROTOCOLS — a protocol describes what a trial will do, not what it found. Do not present CONVOKE as having established efficacy until the results publication exists. When appraising any digital therapeutic for negative symptoms, ask what the comparator was: a sham app controls for engagement, a waitlist does not, and avolition makes daily app use exactly the behaviour the illness impairs.

[JMIR Research Protocols — CT-155 for experiential negative symptoms of schizophrenia](https://www.researchprotocols.org/)

### 9. Long-Acting Injectable Antipsychotics in First-Episode and Early-Phase Schizophrenia

*Expert consensus and comparative trial synthesis · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Consensus and synthesis work argues that starting a long-acting injectable antipsychotic during the first episode or early phase of schizophrenia gives better relapse prevention than oral formulations, and that the traditional practice of reserving injectables for chronic non-adherent patients should be abandoned.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reframes the injectable from a response to failure into a first-line choice. The framing itself is the intervention: offering an injectable only after relapse communicates to the patient that it is a sanction.

**Practical implications.** Offer an injectable early and through genuine shared decision-making — the rationale is fewer relapses in the period when relapse does most damage, not distrust of the patient. Practical points the submission passes over: establish tolerability on the oral form of the same molecule first, and be clear that a long-acting formulation cannot be withdrawn quickly if a serious adverse effect appears, which is the real trade-off. The claim that early relapse causes cumulative neurostructural change is a contested hypothesis rather than settled fact, and should not be used as a persuasion tactic.

[Long-acting injectable antipsychotics in early-phase schizophrenia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=long-acting+injectable+antipsychotics+first-episode+early-phase+schizophrenia+consensus+relapse+prevention)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — direction well supported, one claim contested.** The clinical direction is well supported in the schizophrenia literature: long-acting injectables reduce relapse and rehospitalisation relative to oral antipsychotics, particularly in early-phase illness, and the position that they should not be reserved for chronic non-adherence is widely argued. I did not verify the specific consensus document the submission cites — the link given is 'PubMed Central' generally rather than an article — so no particular statement is attributed. TWO ADDITIONS/CORRECTIONS. The submission's claim that early relapse causes 'cumulative neurostructural changes' is a contested hypothesis, not established, and the entry says so, because it is exactly the kind of claim that gets used to press a patient toward a decision. And two practical points are added that the submission omits: establishing tolerability on the oral form of the same molecule first, and the irreversibility of a depot if a serious adverse effect emerges.

### 10. Micronutrient Testing and Supplementation in Depression: What the Trials Actually Show

*Randomised trials and meta-analyses · 2010 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The evidence for correcting micronutrient deficiency to improve antidepressant response is mixed and mostly modest. A large randomised trial found no clear potentiation of antidepressant effect by folic acid plus vitamin B12, with a signal in only one of four follow-up periods, and meta-analysis indicates short-term folate and B12 supplementation does not improve depressive symptoms in adults with major depression already taking antidepressants. One randomised trial did find benefit from B12 specifically in patients with low-normal B12 levels. For vitamin D, a dose-response meta-analysis of 31 randomised trials found each 1,000 IU per day produced a slight reduction in depressive symptoms, in people with and without depression.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The honest summary is a small effect at best, concentrated in people who are actually deficient. That is a narrower proposition than routine supplementation, and much narrower than routine panel testing.

**Practical implications.** Check B12 and folate where there is clinical reason — restricted diet, malabsorption, metformin, alcohol, macrocytosis — and correct what is low, which is good medicine independent of mood. Do not order broad nutritional, inflammatory and metabolic panels on every patient starting an antidepressant: no guideline recommends it, the results rarely change management, and abnormal incidental findings generate their own workups. Nutraceutical augmentation is not a substitute for adequate antidepressant dosing, an evidence-based augmentation strategy, or psychotherapy.

[Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised placebo-controlled trials of folate and vitamin B12 for depression](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1041610224005532)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the evidence does not support the claim, and no guideline recommends the screening.** The submission states that 'large-scale meta-analyses and clinical trials confirm[] that correcting specific micronutrient deficiencies (such as folate/B9, B12, and high-dose Vitamin D3) significantly enhances standard antidepressant treatment response and improves cardiometabolic markers', and that this 'updates major depressive disorder evaluation guidelines to recommend baseline screening for nutritional, inflammatory, and metabolic biomarkers prior to or alongside psychotropic initiation'. THE EVIDENCE RUNS THE OTHER WAY. A large randomised trial found no clear potentiation of antidepressant effect by folic acid plus B12 (effect in one of four follow-up periods only); meta-analysis found short-term folate and B12 supplementation does not improve depressive symptoms in adults already on antidepressants; benefit was found in one trial specifically in patients with low-normal B12. For vitamin D, a 31-trial dose-response meta-analysis found a SLIGHT reduction per 1,000 IU/day, in people with and without depression. No guideline recommending baseline nutritional, inflammatory and metabolic biomarker panels before psychotropic initiation was found, and the cardiometabolic claim is not an established augmentation finding. Rewritten to give the actual trial results and a defensible clinical rule — test where there is a reason, correct what is low — because the submitted version would license selling supplement panels to every patient starting an antidepressant.

### 1. Dose-Effect Network Meta-Analysis of ADHD Pharmacotherapy

*The Lancet Psychiatry · June 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The first comprehensive dose-effect network meta-analysis of ADHD medication, pooling 113 double-blind randomised trials — 14,138 children and adolescents and 11,016 adults — using a Bayesian hierarchical model with restricted cubic splines to map the shape of the dose-response curve rather than compare fixed doses. It covers oral stimulant and non-stimulant monotherapy from age 5 upwards, with efficacy on validated clinical scales as the primary outcome and discontinuation due to adverse events as the secondary.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Answers the question prescribers actually face — not whether a drug works, but at what dose it stops adding benefit. Modelling the whole curve is what makes that answerable, and 25,000 patients is enough for the shape to be stable.

**Practical implications.** Two errors it lets you avoid: leaving a patient on a sub-therapeutic dose and assuming non-response, and pushing past the licensed maximum in the hope of more effect. Read efficacy and tolerability curves together — they do not peak at the same dose, which is the whole point of a dose-effect analysis, and discontinuation for adverse events is the endpoint that decides whether a dose is usable.

[The Lancet Psychiatry — pharmacological interventions for ADHD: a systematic review and dose-effect network meta-analysis](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366(26)00091-X/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — accurate, with the guideline claim softened.** Verified and accurately described: The Lancet Psychiatry, June 2026, 113 double-blind randomised trials, 14,138 children/adolescents and 11,016 adults (the submission's '25,000+ patients' is correct), Bayesian hierarchical network model with restricted cubic splines, oral stimulant and non-stimulant monotherapy from age 5, efficacy on validated scales with tolerability as discontinuation due to adverse events. The best-sourced entry in this list. TWO CHANGES. The age breakdown, design and tolerability endpoint were added, since a dose-effect analysis is only interpretable if you know efficacy and tolerability curves are separate. And the claim that it 'updates international ADHD prescribing guidelines' is softened — the analysis informs guidelines rather than constituting one, and no guideline revision was verified.

### 2. Interleukin-6 Receptor Blockade with Tocilizumab in Difficult-to-Treat Depression

*JAMA Psychiatry · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A proof-of-concept randomised controlled trial tested whether blocking the IL-6 receptor with tocilizumab improves depressive symptoms in people with difficult-to-treat depression and low-grade inflammation. A 54% clinical remission rate was reported, with greater stepwise improvement over time in depression severity, somatic distress, anxiety and fatigue. Improvement correlated better with baseline high-sensitivity CRP than with IL-6 itself.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first targeted clinical signal that immune modulation might treat a biologically defined subgroup of depression rather than the diagnosis as a whole — and the finding that hs-CRP predicts better than IL-6 is practically useful, since hs-CRP is a routine test.

**Practical implications.** Read this as proof of concept, not as a treatment pathway. The trial is a pilot, remission and response rates are described as numerically favouring tocilizumab, and an earlier study in medically ill patients found tocilizumab associated with WORSE depressive symptoms — so the direction is not settled across populations. Tocilizumab is not approved for depression and carries serious immunosuppression risk: serious infection, tuberculosis reactivation, hepatotoxicity and gastrointestinal perforation. Measuring hs-CRP in treatment-resistant depression is defensible as research stratification; prescribing a biologic on the result is not.

[JAMA Psychiatry — interleukin 6 as a treatment target for depression: a proof-of-concept randomized clinical trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2848978)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — real, but the risk profile and a contrary trial were omitted.** Verified: JAMA Psychiatry proof-of-concept randomised trial of IL-6 receptor inhibition with tocilizumab in difficult-to-treat depression linked to low-grade inflammation, 54% remission, with improvement correlating with baseline hs-CRP better than IL-6. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) The trial is a PILOT proof-of-concept and its remission and response rates are described as NUMERICALLY favouring tocilizumab — the submission presents 54% as an established treatment effect. (2) OMITTED CONTRARY EVIDENCE: an earlier study found tocilizumab associated with worse depression and related symptoms in medically ill patients, so the effect is not consistent across populations. (3) SAFETY OMITTED ENTIRELY: the submission recommends clinicians 'order routine inflammatory panels... to evaluate candidates for adjunctive biologic anti-inflammatory interventions' without mentioning that tocilizumab is an immunosuppressant carrying risk of serious infection, TB reactivation, hepatotoxicity and GI perforation, and is not approved for depression. A psychiatrist acting on the submitted version could expose a patient to those risks on the strength of one pilot trial.

### 3. Cannabinoids for Psychiatric Disorders: No Evidence of Efficacy

*Systematic review and meta-analysis · 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A large systematic review evaluating medicinal cannabis and cannabinoid products across major psychiatric indications found no evidence of efficacy for anxiety disorders, major depression or PTSD.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A negative synthesis in a field where patient demand and commercial supply have both run far ahead of the evidence. Its clinical value is in giving a clinician something concrete to say.

**Practical implications.** Use it in the conversation with a patient already self-medicating: there is no demonstrated benefit for anxiety, depression or PTSD, and there is demonstrated harm — psychosis risk, cannabis use disorder, and the opportunity cost of delaying treatment that does work. Distinguish products carefully: this concerns cannabinoids for psychiatric indications, not the separate and narrow evidence for cannabidiol in specific epilepsy syndromes. I could not confirm the specific review the submission cites or that guidelines have issued a strong recommendation against prescribing, so the direction here rests on the wider evidence base rather than on a named document.

[Cannabinoids for psychiatric disorders: systematic review evidence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=cannabinoids+medicinal+cannabis+anxiety+depression+PTSD+systematic+review+efficacy)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — direction consistent with the evidence base.** The direction is well supported: systematic reviews of cannabinoids for anxiety, depression and PTSD have consistently failed to demonstrate efficacy, and the harms cited (psychosis risk, cannabis use disorder, delayed evidence-based care) are established. But I did not verify the specific review the submission describes as 'the largest multi-center analysis to date' in The Lancet, and I could not confirm that clinical practice guidelines now issue a strong recommendation against prescribing for mood and anxiety disorders — so neither is asserted. ### 4. Switching from Oral Atypical Antipsychotics to Xanomeline-Trospium (Cobenfy): Open-Label Outpatient Switch Study
