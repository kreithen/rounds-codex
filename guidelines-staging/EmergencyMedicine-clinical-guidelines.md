### 1. 2025 American Heart Association Guidelines for Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiovascular Care

*Circulation · 22 October 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A full update to the 2020 guidelines, published as a multi-part document covering neonates, children and adults, for lay rescuers and clinicians. Organised around the Utstein Formula for Survival, with new recommendations in areas that previously had none.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Emphasises high-quality chest compressions, early defibrillation, and integration of advanced resuscitation techniques. Part 4 on Systems of Care is where the substantive shift sits: coordination across the Chain of Survival including emergency telecommunicators and untrained lay rescuers, elements common to resuscitating different populations, and how to improve team and system performance.

**Practical implications.** The framing is that survival is a property of the system, not of the individual resuscitation — telecommunicator-assisted CPR and bystander action are treated as links to be engineered rather than hoped for. On temperature: current guidance is to actively prevent fever (above roughly 37.7°C); a mandated 32-36°C target is no longer the standard framing, and entry 6 in this list covers the trial evidence behind that.

[Circulation - 2025 AHA Guidelines for CPR and ECC, Executive Summary](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001372)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — several specifics unconfirmed, and one internally contradictory.** The guidelines are real: published 22 October 2025 in Circulation across multiple parts, Executive Summary DOI 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001372, PMID 41122893. FOUR SPECIFIC CLAIMS COULD NOT BE CONFIRMED and have been removed rather than asserted: consolidation of four Chains of Survival into a single 6-link chain; early double-sequential external defibrillation protocols for refractory VF; formalised eCPR referral criteria; and strict neuroprognostication timelines. Most importantly, the original's 'rapid targeted temperature management (32-36°C)' CONTRADICTS entry 6 in the same submitted list, which correctly describes the shift to fever prevention — and contradicts current guidance to avoid fever above about 37.7°C. Verify the removed specifics against the document before restoring them.

### 2. Endovascular Thrombectomy for Acute Ischemic Stroke with Medium-Vessel Occlusion (ESCAPE-MeVO and DISTAL)

*NEJM · February 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Two negative trials, and they matter. ESCAPE-MeVO randomised 530 patients with medium-vessel occlusion within 12 hours of last seen normal to endovascular thrombectomy plus best medical care (n=257) or best medical care alone (n=272). EVT did NOT improve 90-day functional outcome. DISTAL and DISCOUNT reached the same conclusion independently.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The ESCAPE-MeVO investigators state plainly that their results 'do not support routine endovascular treatment of acute ischaemic stroke due to medium-vessel occlusion.' The accompanying NEJM editorial was titled 'Medium- and Distal-Vessel Occlusion — The Limit of Thrombectomy?' No guideline has expanded thrombectomy candidacy to MeVO on the basis of these trials, because they argue against it.

**Practical implications.** Do NOT rebuild ED imaging or activation pathways to capture medium-vessel occlusions for thrombectomy. Proximal large-vessel occlusion remains the indication. The technical ability to reach a distal vessel turned out not to translate into patient benefit — smaller vessels, smaller territory at risk, and more procedural risk per unit of tissue saved.

[NEJM - Medium- and Distal-Vessel Occlusion: The Limit of Thrombectomy?](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMe2500492)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — original stated the opposite of the published result.** The trials are real, correctly journaled and correctly dated, and the original had the finding exactly backwards. It said EVT for MeVO 'significantly improves 90-day functional independence' and 'directly updated acute ischemic stroke guidelines, expanding interventional candidate selection'. ESCAPE-MeVO (530 patients), DISTAL and DISCOUNT all found NO clinical benefit. This is the most clinically consequential error in any list submitted so far: acting on the original would have EDs screening for and activating thrombectomy on MeVOs that three randomised trials say not to treat.

### 3. Zalunfiban at First Medical Contact for ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (CELEBRATE)

*NEJM Evidence · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A phase 3 double-blind placebo-controlled trial of a single subcutaneous injection of zalunfiban, a glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor, given at first medical contact in STEMI patients headed for PCI. 2,467 patients across three arms (0.11 mg/kg n=853, 0.13 mg/kg n=818, placebo n=796). Zalunfiban improved pre-intervention infarct-related artery patency and reduced the likelihood of a worse 30-day hierarchical clinical endpoint.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Revives glycoprotein IIb/IIIa blockade, which had largely fallen out of use, by moving it to the earliest possible moment and making it a single subcutaneous injection. The primary endpoint was a hierarchical proportional odds model ranking death, stroke, reinfarction, acute stent thrombosis, heart failure, larger infarct size and no event.

**Practical implications.** A paramedic or triage nurse can give one subcutaneous injection at first contact. Bleeding: severe or life-threatening bleeding was NOT increased, but mild to moderate bleeding WAS — which is the trade-off to weigh, and the original entry omitted it.

[NEJM Evidence - Zalunfiban at first medical contact for STEMI](https://evidence.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/EVIDoa2500268)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** Real and positive. NEJM EVIDENCE (not NEJM), DOI 10.1056/EVIDoa2500268, PMID 41211981, 2,467 patients. Three additions: the trial size and dose arms; the increase in mild-to-moderate bleeding, which the original omitted entirely; and the actual endpoints. 'Refines prehospital acute coronary syndrome protocols' softened — no guideline revision has been identified, and an EHJ journal scan was headlined 'what should we CELEBRATE and what remains uncertain', so this is promising rather than settled.

### 4. Resuscitative Transesophageal Echocardiography in Cardiac Arrest and Refractory Shock

*Annals of Emergency Medicine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Multicentre registry data on point-of-care resuscitative TEE during active CPR, reporting shorter hands-off compression pauses, better discrimination of pseudo-PEA from fine ventricular fibrillation, and guidance of mechanical CPR positioning in real time.

**Clinical guideline impact.** TEE has a genuine mechanical advantage over transthoracic imaging in arrest: the probe stays in place during compressions, so it does not force a pause to acquire an image. Registry data can describe that association; it cannot establish that TEE improves ROSC.

**Practical implications.** Where TEE is available and someone is trained, it answers questions transthoracic imaging cannot during ongoing CPR. It does not replace transthoracic ultrasound — the barrier is training and equipment, and most EDs have neither for TEE.

[Annals of Emergency Medicine - resuscitative TEE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=resuscitative+transesophageal+echocardiography+cardiac+arrest+registry+emergency)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, and evidence level overstated.** No specific registry publication matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search. Two corrections regardless of the citation: registry data cannot 'prove' improved ROSC — that is an observational association, and the original's 'proved' has been removed. And the claim that TEE should be 'replacing standard transthoracic ultrasound during resuscitation' is not a position any consensus body has taken; it has been softened to where TEE adds something TTE cannot.

### 5. Single-Bolus Tenecteplase versus Alteplase for Acute Ischemic Stroke

*The Lancet / NEJM (multiple trials) · AcT 2022; ATTEST-2 and TASTE subsequently*

**The breakthrough.** Tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg as a single intravenous bolus is non-inferior to a one-hour alteplase infusion for 90-day functional outcome in acute ischemic stroke, established across several randomised trials — AcT in Canada being the pragmatic one that moved practice.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Tenecteplase has become the preferred thrombolytic across many stroke systems, on the strength of equivalent outcomes plus a dosing form that is far easier to deliver. Note that this is a body of non-inferiority evidence rather than a single superiority trial.

**Practical implications.** The operational gain is the real one: a single bolus removes infusion pump setup and removes the problem of transporting a patient mid-infusion, which shortens door-in-door-out time for transfer to a thrombectomy centre. See also BRIDGE-TNK, which showed tenecteplase before thrombectomy beats going straight to thrombectomy.

[Tenecteplase versus alteplase for acute ischaemic stroke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=tenecteplase+versus+alteplase+acute+ischaemic+stroke+non-inferiority+randomised)

> **Citation check — ⚠ reframed — no single 2025 NEJM trial identified.** The clinical conclusion is well supported, but not by a single 2025 NEJM trial as submitted — no such publication was located. The evidence is a set of non-inferiority trials (AcT, Lancet 2022; ATTEST-2; TASTE). Reframed accordingly, and the original's 'superior early reperfusion' softened, since the trials were designed and reported as non-inferiority for functional outcome. 'Formally establishes tenecteplase as the preferred first-line agent in updated stroke guidelines, replacing alteplase' overstates a transition that has happened by adoption more than by formal replacement.

### 6. Targeted Temperature Management at 33°C versus Normothermia after Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest (TTM2)

*NEJM · 2021*

**The breakthrough.** 1,850 comatose survivors of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest randomised to 33°C or targeted normothermia (below 37.5°C). Survival and neurological outcome at 6 months were not clinically or statistically different — and the hypothermia arm had MORE hemodynamically compromising arrhythmias, 24% versus 17%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Shifted post-arrest care from induced hypothermia toward active fever prevention. Current guidance is to avoid fever, defined as core temperature above roughly 37.7°C, rather than to cool to a fixed target.

**Practical implications.** The important exception runs OPPOSITE to the original entry: HYPERION found that targeting 33°C DID improve 90-day survival with favourable neurological outcome specifically in comatose patients after NON-SHOCKABLE arrest, compared with 37°C. So non-shockable rhythm is the one setting where cooling retains the strongest support, not the setting to abandon it in. Continuous core temperature monitoring and antipyretic control are the baseline in all comers.

[Targeted temperature management after cardiac arrest](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.122.026539)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — wrong trial, wrong year, and the non-shockable claim reversed.** The described trial is TTM2, NEJM 2021 (1,850 patients), not a 2025 Lancet trial — no such 2025 publication was located. The equivalence finding is correct, and the arrhythmia signal (24% vs 17%) has been added. THE NON-SHOCKABLE CLAIM WAS BACKWARDS: the original said guidelines shifted toward fever prevention 'rather than induced therapeutic hypothermia FOR NON-SHOCKABLE cardiac arrest'. HYPERION found 33°C BENEFICIAL in non-shockable arrest versus 37°C, making that the subgroup where cooling is best supported. Note also that this entry contradicted entry 1 as submitted.

### 7. High-Sensitivity Cardiac Troponin 0/1-Hour Accelerated Diagnostic Protocols for Rule-Out of Myocardial Infarction

*Multiple trials; incorporated into ESC and ACEP guidance · Established pathway*

**The breakthrough.** A 0/1-hour high-sensitivity troponin algorithm combined with clinical risk scoring allows a large proportion of low-risk chest pain patients to be ruled out and discharged from the ED within a few hours, with very high negative predictive value for 30-day major adverse cardiac events.

**Clinical guideline impact.** 0/1-hour hs-cTn pathways are established in ESC guidance and in ACEP clinical policy on emergency chest pain evaluation, and have largely displaced serial 3-6 hour troponin sampling.

**Practical implications.** The clinical value is in what it removes: fewer observation admissions, fewer downstream stress tests in patients whose pretest probability was always low, and less ED crowding. The algorithm depends on a validated assay-specific cut-off — the thresholds are NOT interchangeable between manufacturers' assays, which is the commonest implementation error.

[High-sensitivity troponin 0/1-hour rule-out pathways](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=high-sensitivity+troponin+0%2F1-hour+algorithm+rule-out+myocardial+infarction+emergency+department)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** The approach is well established and correctly described, but no specific JAMA 2025 study matching the submitted description was located, so the two precise figures — discharge of 'over 60%' within 2 hours and a '99.8% 30-day NPV for MACE' — are not asserted here. They are plausible for this literature but should be tied to a specific paper before being taught as numbers. Added the assay-specificity caveat, which is the practical trap and was absent.

### 8. Balanced Crystalloids versus Saline in Noncritically Ill Adults (SALT-ED)

*NEJM · 2018*

**The breakthrough.** A single-centre pragmatic multiple-crossover trial in adults given intravenous crystalloid in the ED and then admitted outside the ICU. The PRIMARY outcome — hospital-free days — showed NO difference (median 25 days in both arms; adjusted OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.92-1.04, P = 0.41). Major adverse kidney events within 30 days, a SECONDARY outcome, were lower with balanced crystalloids: 4.7% versus 5.6% (adjusted OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.70-0.95, P = 0.01).

**Clinical guideline impact.** The renal signal is real but secondary, and the effect is small in absolute terms — under one percentage point. Large subsequent ICU trials (BaSICS and PLUS) were neutral, so the field is not unanimous that balanced fluids improve outcomes.

**Practical implications.** Reasonable to default to lactated Ringer's rather than 0.9% saline, since it costs nothing to do and the renal signal points one way. But this is a low-stakes preference supported by a secondary endpoint, not a practice mandate — and hyperchloremic acidosis is a physiological observation rather than a demonstrated harm in this trial.

[NEJM - Balanced Crystalloids versus Saline in Noncritically Ill Adults](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1711586)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — wrong year, and the primary outcome was null.** The trial is SALT-ED, NEJM 2018 (PMID 29485926), not a 2025 trial. Three corrections. It was SINGLE-CENTRE, not 'massive'. Its PRIMARY outcome — hospital-free days — was NULL; MAKE30 was a secondary outcome. And 'Rewrote emergency fluid resuscitation guidelines, designating balanced crystalloids as the primary first-line crystalloid' overstates: the large ICU trials BaSICS and PLUS were neutral, which the entry now says.

### 9. Gepotidacin for Uncomplicated Urinary Tract Infection and Uncomplicated Urogenital Gonorrhea (EAGLE-1, EAGLE-2, EAGLE-3)

*The Lancet · EAGLE-1 published April 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Gepotidacin, a first-in-class oral triazaacenaphthylene antibiotic. EAGLE-1 in uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea (about 600 patients) compared two oral 3,000 mg doses against intramuscular ceftriaxone plus oral azithromycin: 92.6% versus 91.2% urogenital success, with no failures from bacterial persistence in either arm. EAGLE-2 and EAGLE-3 (about 3,000 patients) showed non-inferiority and superiority to nitrofurantoin in uncomplicated UTI.

**Clinical guideline impact.** FDA approved in March 2025 for uncomplicated UTI in females aged 12 and over (at least 40 kg), and subsequently for uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea in patients 12 and over (at least 45 kg). A genuinely new oral mechanism, which is rare.

**Practical implications.** An entirely oral option for gonorrhea, where the standard has been an intramuscular injection. Note the indication precisely: UNCOMPLICATED UTI and UNCOMPLICATED urogenital gonorrhea — not complicated UTI, and not established multi-drug-resistant infection. Gastrointestinal side effects are common: diarrhea in 16%, nausea in 9%, mostly mild.

[The Lancet - EAGLE-1 gepotidacin in uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhoea](https://us.gsk.com/en-us/media/press-releases/blujepa-gepotidacin-approved-by-us-fda-as-oral-option-for-treatment-of-uncomplicated-urogenital-gonorrhea/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** Real and well supported. Two clinically material corrections. The original said gepotidacin showed high cure rates in organisms 'resistant to conventional fluoroquinolones and cephalosporins' and positioned it for 'suspected multi-drug resistant gonorrhea or COMPLEX UTIs'. EAGLE-1 compared it head-to-head against ceftriaxone plus azithromycin in uncomplicated gonorrhea generally, and the approved indications are UNCOMPLICATED UTI and UNCOMPLICATED urogenital gonorrhea. Recommending it for complicated UTI would be an off-label error. Added the trial numbers, the FDA approvals and the GI adverse event rates.

### 10. Viscoelastic Assay-Guided versus Conventional Coagulation-Directed Transfusion in Major Trauma Haemorrhage (ITACTIC)

*Intensive Care Medicine · 2021*

**The breakthrough.** A negative trial. 396 patients randomised to major haemorrhage protocols augmented by viscoelastic assay (n=201) or conventional coagulation testing (n=195). At 24 hours there was NO difference in the proportion alive and free of massive transfusion: 67% versus 64% (OR 1.15, 95% CI 0.76-1.73). 28-day mortality was also no different: 25% versus 28% (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.54-1.31).

**Clinical guideline impact.** Does not support routine ROTEM/TEG-guided transfusion over conventional coagulation-guided protocols in major trauma haemorrhage. A pre-specified subgroup of 74 patients with severe traumatic brain injury showed a signal favouring viscoelastic guidance (64% vs 46% alive and free of massive transfusion, OR 2.12) — but the confidence interval crossed 1 (95% CI 0.84-5.34), so this is a hypothesis, not a finding.

**Practical implications.** Viscoelastic testing identifies specific coagulopathies — hyperfibrinolysis, hypofibrinogenemia — faster than conventional assays, and that remains its rationale. What ITACTIC shows is that having that information faster did not translate into better outcomes than a conventional-test-guided protocol. Balanced empiric transfusion plus early tranexamic acid remains the backbone.

[ITACTIC - viscoelastic haemostatic assay augmented protocols for major trauma haemorrhage](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7550843/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — original stated the opposite of the published result.** There is no 2025 JAMA Surgery trial matching the submission. The randomised evidence is ITACTIC (2021, 396 patients) and it was NEGATIVE on both the primary outcome and mortality. The original claimed viscoelastic guidance 'significantly reduces 24-hour mortality and total blood component usage compared to fixed 1:1:1 empirical transfusion ratios' and that guidelines now recommend early ROTEM/TEG in massive transfusion protocols. Worth noting: this is the SECOND submitted entry across these lists to claim a positive result for viscoelastic-guided transfusion where the trial was negative — the other was the postpartum haemorrhage entry in the Anesthesiology 2025 list (OBS2).

### 1. Azithromycin for Preschoolers with Wheezing in the Emergency Department (AZ-SWED)

*NEJM · 18 May 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A negative trial, stopped for futility. 840 children aged 18 to 59 months at eight pediatric emergency departments, randomised to five days of azithromycin or placebo after presenting with moderate-to-severe acute wheezing. Azithromycin did not reduce wheezing symptom severity, measured by the Asthma Flare-up Diary for Young Children score.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The precision-medicine element is what makes it decisive: the absence of benefit held REGARDLESS of whether Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis or Streptococcus pneumoniae were isolated from the nasopharynx at the time of the illness. So the usual justification — that these children may have a bacterial component — does not rescue the practice. The NHLBI stopped the trial for futility on 10 December 2024 on DSMB recommendation.

**Practical implications.** Do not give empiric azithromycin for acute preschool wheezing. Bronchodilators, systemic corticosteroids and supportive care for what is almost always a viral trigger. Reserve antibiotics for clear evidence of bacterial pneumonia.

[NEJM - AZ-SWED azithromycin in preschool wheezing](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2516505)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** Real, and the original correctly reported a negative trial as negative. NEJM 18 May 2026, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2516505, 840 children, eight pediatric EDs, presented at ATS simultaneously. Two corrections: the 'over 60% tested positive for bacterial presence' figure could not be confirmed and has been replaced with the actual and stronger finding — no benefit regardless of which of three organisms was isolated. And 'Directly updates pediatric emergency airway guidelines' could not be confirmed; no guideline revision was identified. Added that the trial was stopped for futility, which strengthens the conclusion.

### 2. Ketamine versus Etomidate for Tracheal Intubation of Critically Ill Adults (RSI Trial)

*NEJM · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A pragmatic trial in adults with non-traumatic critical illness undergoing emergency tracheal intubation in the ED or ICU, comparing ketamine 1-2 mg/kg with etomidate 0.2-0.3 mg/kg for induction. Mortality at 28 days did not differ: 28% versus 29% in hospital, and 32.2% versus 32.4% counting deaths anywhere.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Settles the long-running concern that etomidate's adrenal suppression costs lives — on mortality, the two agents are equivalent. But the drugs are NOT hemodynamically equivalent, which cuts against the common assumption.

**Practical implications.** Cardiovascular collapse was MORE common with ketamine, 22% versus 17%, and a systolic drop over 30 mmHg after induction occurred in 24% of ketamine patients versus 15% with etomidate. That is the opposite of the usual reason ketamine is chosen in a shocked patient, and it is the single most useful thing to take from this trial. Either agent is defensible on survival; etomidate looks steadier on induction hemodynamics.

[NEJM - RSI trial, ketamine versus etomidate](https://rebelem.com/the-rsi-trial-ketamine-vs-etomidate-in-rapid-sequence-intubation/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — hemodynamic claim reversed.** Real (Casey et al., NEJM 2025 — a 2025 publication placed in the 2026 list). The mortality equivalence is correct. THE HEMODYNAMIC CLAIM WAS BACKWARDS: the original said the trial demonstrated 'comparable post-intubation hemodynamic stability' and classified the agents as 'clinically equivalent'. Cardiovascular collapse was significantly more common with ketamine (22% vs 17%) and large systolic drops nearly twice as frequent (24% vs 15%). Since ketamine is often selected specifically to protect blood pressure, teaching equivalence here would reinforce exactly the wrong instinct.

### 3. Head-Flat versus Head-Elevated Position While Awaiting Endovascular Thrombectomy in Large-Vessel Occlusion Stroke

*The Lancet · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Compared a 0° flat head position against a 30° head-elevated position in patients with acute large-vessel occlusion stroke during the interval before endovascular thrombectomy, on the rationale that a flat position preserves collateral cerebral perfusion pressure.

**Clinical guideline impact.** If it holds, it is the rare intervention with no cost, no equipment and no drug — a bed angle. The mechanism is plausible: collateral flow to the ischemic penumbra is pressure-dependent, and head elevation reduces cerebral perfusion pressure.

**Practical implications.** Weigh against the reason heads are elevated in the first place — aspiration risk in a patient who may have impaired airway protection, and raised intracranial pressure if there is significant established infarct or hemorrhage. A blanket flat-bed rule for every stroke patient is not the same recommendation as flat positioning in a selected LVO patient awaiting thrombectomy.

[Head positioning before endovascular thrombectomy in large-vessel occlusion stroke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=head+position+flat+versus+elevated+large+vessel+occlusion+stroke+endovascular+thrombectomy+randomised)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific publication matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search. Two claims softened regardless of the citation: 'significantly preserves collateral cerebral blood flow and improves early neurological scores' is stated as the hypothesis rather than a confirmed result, and the original's 'MANDATING a flat bed position during prehospital transport and ED holding' is not a mandate any guideline has issued. The aspiration and ICP counterweights were added — they are the reason this is not already standard.

### 4. Personalized Hemodynamic Resuscitation Targeting Capillary Refill Time in Early Septic Shock (ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2)

*JAMA · 29 October 2025*

**The breakthrough.** 1,467 patients across 86 ICUs in 19 countries, randomised to a capillary-refill-time-targeted personalized hemodynamic resuscitation protocol (n=720) or usual care (n=747) over a 6-hour intervention window. The protocol was superior on a hierarchical composite of mortality, duration of vital support and hospital length of stay, with less need for organ support at 28 days.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A positive trial in septic shock resuscitation, which is uncommon. Note what the intervention actually was: a phenotype-based protocol using pulse pressure, diastolic arterial pressure, fluid responsiveness and bedside echocardiography to tailor fluids, vasopressors and inotropes — with capillary refill time as the resuscitation target, not as the sole input.

**Practical implications.** Capillary refill time is free, instant and repeatable, and it does not wait for a lactate to come back. But the trial tested it inside a structured protocol that included echocardiography, so implementing it means adopting the protocol, not just checking a nail bed. The comparator was usual care, not lactate-guided resuscitation specifically.

[JAMA - ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 randomized clinical trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06062303)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** Real and positive. JAMA, 29 October 2025 (a 2025 publication placed in the 2026 list), NCT06062303, 86 ICUs in 19 countries, 1,467 patients. Two corrections. The comparator was USUAL CARE, not 'standard lactate clearance' as submitted. And the intervention was a personalized hemodynamic phenotype protocol including bedside echocardiography — the original's framing as 'a rapid, non-invasive physical exam tool' materially understates what was tested. 'Formally integrates CRT into international sepsis guidelines as a primary bedside target' could not be confirmed.

### 5. Higher versus Standard Mean Arterial Pressure Targets in Older Patients with Septic Shock (OPTRESS)

*Intensive Care Medicine · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Tested whether targeting a higher mean arterial pressure (above 85 mmHg) in older adults with septic shock improves outcomes compared with a standard target of 65-70 mmHg. It did not reduce mortality or need for renal replacement therapy, and was associated with more vasopressor-related arrhythmia.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Consistent with the existing evidence base — SEPSISPAM and 65 trial — that pushing MAP above the standard target does not help and does cost something. Reinforces MAP 65 as the default rather than a floor to be exceeded.

**Practical implications.** In an older patient with chronic hypertension the instinct is that they 'need' a higher pressure. The consistent finding across trials is that acting on that instinct buys vasopressor exposure, tachyarrhythmia and digital ischemia without buying organ protection.

[OPTRESS - higher versus standard MAP target in older patients with septic shock](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=OPTRESS+mean+arterial+pressure+target+older+septic+shock)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific publication named OPTRESS was located; the url is a PubMed title search. The conclusion is consistent with well-established trials (SEPSISPAM, the 65 trial), which is why the entry has been kept and framed against them rather than deleted — but the specific trial, its numbers and the arrhythmia finding should be confirmed before being taught as this trial's result.

### 6. Prehospital Whole Blood versus Component Therapy in Traumatic Haemorrhage (SWiFT)

*NEJM · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A negative superiority trial. 616 patients with major traumatic haemorrhage attended by 10 air ambulance services covering 19 hospitals, randomised to up to 2 units of prehospital whole blood or standard component therapy (up to 2 units each of red cells and plasma). Whole blood was NOT superior for death or massive transfusion at 24 hours.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Whole blood has strong logistical and conceptual appeal in the prehospital setting — one product, one line, physiological ratios. This trial found no outcome advantage over components at the doses given. Safety profiles were similar, with slightly more adverse events in the standard-care arm.

**Practical implications.** An important limitation the investigators flag: up to two units may simply be too small a dose to reveal a difference in patients with major haemorrhage. So this is evidence against whole blood being clearly better at that dose, rather than evidence that whole blood does not work. Prehospital blood logistics decisions should rest on what a given system can actually deliver quickly.

[NEJM - SWiFT prehospital whole blood trial](https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/news/article/2026/03/25/swift-trial-finds-no-advantage-to-prehospital-whole-blood-in-patients-with-life-threatening-trauma)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** Real, and the original correctly conveyed a non-positive result. NEJM 2026, phase III superiority trial, 616 patients, 10 air ambulances serving 19 hospitals, December 2022 to September 2024. The original's framing — that the trial showed 'overall protocol speed and total balanced volume take clinical precedence over early isolated whole blood units' — is an interpretation, not a finding, and has been replaced with the actual result (non-superiority for death or massive transfusion at 24 hours). Added the investigators' own dose limitation, which materially changes how the result should be read.

### 7. 2026 Core Content of Emergency Medical Services Medicine (ABEM)

*Prehospital Emergency Care · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The American Board of Emergency Medicine's revised core content for EMS medicine, defining the knowledge base for the subspecialty across prehospital care, field triage, out-of-hospital airway management and physician medical oversight.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A core content document defines what an EMS physician is expected to know and what the certifying examination covers. It shapes fellowship curricula and medical direction expectations — it is not a clinical practice guideline and does not itself set treatment protocols.

**Practical implications.** Relevant to anyone doing EMS medical direction or sitting the subspecialty examination. Field protocols for analgesia, non-invasive ventilation and stroke or trauma centre bypass are set locally by medical directors; a core content revision influences that indirectly, through what directors are trained in.

[Prehospital Emergency Care - 2026 core content of EMS medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=core+content+emergency+medical+services+medicine+ABEM+prehospital+emergency+care)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, and category corrected.** No specific 2026 document was located; the url is a PubMed title search. A category correction applies regardless: a core content document is a certification and curriculum specification, not a 'prehospital protocol overhaul' that 'establishes updated prehospital standards' and 'standardizes EMS field protocols nationwide' as the original claimed. US field protocols are set by state and local EMS medical direction. Reframed to what a core content document does.

### 8. De-implementation of Routine Corticosteroid Premedication for Contrast Media Hypersensitivity

*Joint allergy and radiology practice guidance · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Evidence synthesis concluding that empiric oral or intravenous corticosteroid premedication before contrast-enhanced CT in low and moderate risk patients offers negligible protection against severe reactions, while delaying imaging and disposition and causing hyperglycemia.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Advises against routine steroid premedication in the ED unless there is a documented history of severe, life-threatening anaphylaxis to iodinated contrast. Reflects a broader recognition that modern low-osmolar and iso-osmolar agents carry far lower reaction rates than the agents the premedication protocols were built around.

**Practical implications.** Removes the 4 to 13 hour premedication delay for patients whose 'contrast allergy' history is vague, mild, or actually a shellfish-allergy misconception. For a patient in the ED with a possible dissection or PE, that delay is the harm. A prior mild reaction is not a reason to premedicate; a prior anaphylaxis is a reason to involve allergy and consider alternatives.

[Practice guidance on contrast media hypersensitivity premedication](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=corticosteroid+premedication+contrast+media+hypersensitivity+guidance+de-implementation)

### 9. Emergency Department-Initiated Extended-Release versus Sublingual Buprenorphine for Opioid Use Disorder (ED-INNOVATION)

*NIDA Clinical Trials Network CTN-0099 · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A negative trial on its primary question. Across 29 emergency departments, patients with untreated opioid use disorder were randomised to a single dose of 7-DAY extended-release injectable buprenorphine or to sublingual buprenorphine taken daily. There was NO difference in treatment engagement at day 7 — the primary outcome.

**Clinical guideline impact.** What it does establish is feasibility and safety: both formulations were successfully and safely initiated across a large, geographically diverse set of US EDs, both were well tolerated, and precipitated withdrawal was rare despite a high prevalence of fentanyl. That last point is genuinely reassuring for anyone hesitant to start buprenorphine in the fentanyl era.

**Practical implications.** The injectable studied covers SEVEN DAYS, not thirty. Either formulation is a reasonable ED initiation strategy; the injectable does not buy better short-term engagement, so choose on logistics, cost and patient preference. The important finding for practice is that starting buprenorphine in the ED is safe and workable, not that one route wins.

[ED-INNOVATION - CTN-0099 emergency department buprenorphine trial](https://ctnlibrary.org/protocol/ctn0099/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — original stated the opposite of the published result.** Real (NIDA CTN-0099) but the submission was wrong on four counts. SITES: 29 emergency departments, not 33. FORMULATION: the injectable was the 7-DAY extended-release preparation, not a 30-day depot — the original's 'ensuring immediate and sustained 30-day coverage' would have a clinician believe one injection covers a month. ENDPOINT: the primary outcome was treatment engagement at DAY 7, not 30-day retention. RESULT: there was NO difference — the original claimed 'significantly higher 30-day treatment retention' and that this 'elevates single-dose injectable depot buprenorphine to a first-line recommended option'.

### 10. Intravenous Vernakalant versus Procainamide for Pharmacological Cardioversion of Acute Atrial Fibrillation

*Annals of Emergency Medicine · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A randomised comparison of intravenous vernakalant against intravenous procainamide for cardioversion of recent-onset, hemodynamically stable atrial fibrillation in the emergency department, reporting faster conversion to sinus rhythm with vernakalant and fewer hypotensive episodes.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Would position vernakalant as a fast-acting option for chemical cardioversion in acute-onset AF. Note a significant practical constraint: vernakalant is approved in Europe and Canada but is NOT FDA-approved in the United States, so this is not an option available to US emergency physicians.

**Practical implications.** Where it is available, rapid chemical cardioversion can avoid procedural sedation and electrical cardioversion and shorten ED length of stay. Candidate selection is the safety-critical part — recent-onset AF, hemodynamically stable, and an appropriate stroke-risk assessment before cardioversion regardless of the agent used.

[Vernakalant versus procainamide for acute atrial fibrillation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=vernakalant+versus+procainamide+acute+atrial+fibrillation+randomized+emergency)
