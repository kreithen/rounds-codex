### 1. AAPM&R Guidance Statement on Platelet-Rich Plasma for Knee Osteoarthritis

*PM&R (Wiley) · Literature review through June 2025*

**The breakthrough.** AAPM&R convened a technical expert panel to assess the evidence for platelet-rich plasma in knee osteoarthritis and synthesise it into a clinician-facing document. A structured literature review through June 2025 plus a modified Delphi process produced five evidence-based clinical recommendations and eleven consensus-based best practices supported by expert opinion and limited evidence.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first orthobiologics guidance from the specialty that performs most of these injections. The split between five evidence-based recommendations and eleven consensus best practices is itself the honest summary of the field: some of this is established and most of it is expert agreement.

**Practical implications.** Use it to standardise what you can — preparation, documentation, patient selection in symptomatic mild-to-moderate knee osteoarthritis after exercise and physical therapy have been tried. Do not read it as settling structural benefit: PRP has not been shown to modify joint structure, and a claim that earlier injection produces superior structural outcomes goes beyond the evidence the panel had. Eleven of the sixteen statements rest on expert opinion, so variation between practices will persist for good reason.

[PM&R — AAPM&R guidance statement on platelet-rich plasma for knee osteoarthritis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/epdf/10.1002/pmrj.70144)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — real, structural benefit not supported.** Verified: AAPM&R technical expert panel, structured literature review through June 2025, modified Delphi, published in PM&R (doi 10.1002/pmrj.70144), producing five evidence-based recommendations and eleven consensus-based best practices. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission says the guidance emphasises 'that earlier intervention yields superior structural and symptomatic outcomes'. Structural benefit from PRP in knee osteoarthritis is not established and the panel's evidence base does not support a structural claim; symptomatic benefit in mild-to-moderate disease is the defensible part. (2) 'Eliminating dosing variability' overstates a document in which eleven of sixteen statements are consensus-based — the entry now gives the five-plus-eleven structure so a reader can see how much is opinion. YEAR: assigned to 2025 on the basis of the June 2025 evidence cut-off; the submission listed this item on both the 2025 and 2026 lists.

### 2. Epidural Spinal Cord Stimulation for Motor and Autonomic Recovery in Chronic Spinal Cord Injury

*Multiple small series and a systematic review · 2020 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Epidural electrical stimulation of the lumbosacral cord can activate lower-limb musculature and partially restore standing and stepping, and has restored voluntary movement below a clinically motor-complete lesion. Stimulation delivered through discrete contacts on a 16-contact lumbosacral array improved seated reaching performance and reach distance in people with chronic complete thoracic injury. Autonomic effects are now also documented — cardiovascular regulation, and neurogenic bladder and bowel function.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Shifts the concept of a complete injury. Residual descending pathways that cannot generate movement unaided can do so when the spinal circuitry below is brought closer to threshold, and the autonomic findings may matter more day-to-day than the stepping does.

**Practical implications.** Treat this as an investigational adjunct to intensive rehabilitation at specialist centres, not an available therapy. The evidence is small series and pilot studies — in some cases two or three participants — with no pivotal multicentre trial reported, so counsel accordingly. Blood pressure stability and bladder and bowel function are the outcomes patients most often rank above walking, and they are the more realistic near-term targets.

[Epidural spinal cord stimulation for spinal cord injury in humans: a systematic review](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/13/4/1090)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND as cited — no ESTEEM trial, real evidence substituted.** The submitted citation does not check out. Both PM&R lists describe an 'ESTEEM Trial' of 'pivotal multicenter clinical trial data' proving eSCS restores volitional trunk control, independent stepping and autonomic blood pressure stability, with the 2026 version adding publication in Nature Neuroscience. No trial named ESTEEM in this field surfaced, and the Nature Neuroscience publication could not be verified. The underlying science is real and has been substituted: lumbosacral eSCS partially restoring standing and stepping, restoring voluntary movement below a motor-complete lesion, improving seated reaching via a 16-contact array in chronic complete thoracic injury, and documented cardiovascular, bladder and bowel effects — but from small series and pilot studies, not a pivotal trial. The entry now says so explicitly, because the submitted version would support telling a patient with chronic paralysis that stepping is an established outcome. The submission's conclusion that eSCS is an 'evidence-based adjunctive standard in spinal cord injury rehabilitation consensus guidelines' is not supported and has been removed. YEAR: assigned to 2025; the item appeared on both submitted lists.

### 3. Targeted Muscle Reinnervation and Regenerative Peripheral Nerve Interfaces at Primary Amputation

*Systematic review and meta-analysis, with observational cohorts · 2023 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Targeted muscle reinnervation and regenerative peripheral nerve interfaces are prophylactic techniques that give transected nerves a target, preventing the neuroma that drives residual and phantom limb pain. Observational evidence shows statistically significant reductions in the incidence and severity of phantom and residual limb pain, including on PROMIS measures, and patients who had immediate — primary — TMR at the time of major limb amputation had less phantom and residual limb pain than untreated amputee controls. In one oncologic amputation cohort, opioid use fell from 85.7% before surgery to 37.7% afterwards.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Moves nerve management from a salvage operation for established pain to a step taken while the limb is being removed, when the nerve ends are already exposed and the neuroma has not yet formed.

**Practical implications.** The practical requirement is a conversation before the amputation, not after: this has to be planned into the primary operation, which means the physiatrist and the surgical team meeting preoperatively. Be careful with effect sizes — the evidence is observational and systematic-review level, randomised trials are registered but not reported, and the opioid figure above is a fall in the proportion of users in a single oncologic cohort rather than a general 70% reduction.

[Targeted muscle reinnervation and regenerative peripheral nerve interfaces versus standard management in limb amputation: systematic review and meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11046287/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the 70% figure is not supported and no prospective trial confirms it.** The direction is real: TMR and RPNI reduce phantom and residual limb pain, primary (immediate) TMR outperforms untreated controls, and a systematic review and meta-analysis exists. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) THE FIGURE: both submitted lists claim these techniques reduce phantom limb pain, residual limb pain and opioid dependency 'by over 70%'. No such composite reduction was found. The closest verified figure is opioid use falling from 85.7% to 37.7% of patients in one oncologic amputation cohort — a change in the proportion of users, in one selected population, not a 70% reduction in three outcomes. (2) DESIGN: submitted as a 'multi-center prospective study confirming'; the evidence is observational with randomised trials registered but not yet reported, and the review literature explicitly states randomised trials are warranted. Both corrected. The recommendation to perform TMR or RPNI at the primary amputation rather than as a secondary revision is well supported and is kept. YEAR: assigned to 2025; the item appeared on both submitted lists.

### 4. Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation for Persistent Post-Concussion Symptoms

*Pilot randomised controlled trial, with a larger trial underway · 2019 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A double-blind, randomised, sham-controlled pilot trial in 20 participants with persistent post-traumatic headache and persistent post-concussion symptoms delivered ten sessions of 10 Hz rTMS — 600 pulses at 70% of resting motor threshold — to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Improvements over time were seen in headache severity and functional impact, depression, post-concussion symptom burden and quality of life. A larger sham-controlled trial of 44 adults with symptoms lasting 3 months to 5 years is under way, and an open-label safety and feasibility study has been reported in adolescents.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A plausible non-pharmacological option for a symptom cluster with almost nothing else to offer once vestibular, cognitive and exertional rehabilitation have been exhausted. The protocol is specific enough to replicate.

**Practical implications.** Offer it as investigational and say so. Twenty participants in a pilot trial is a signal, not a basis for routine prescription, and the definitive trial has not reported. Two claims commonly attached to this work are not supported by it: dysautonomia was not among the reported outcomes, and no brain injury rehabilitation guideline has incorporated rTMS for persistent post-concussive symptoms.

[Treatment of persistent post-traumatic headache and post-concussion symptoms using rTMS: a pilot, double-blind, randomized controlled trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31530227/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — one claimed outcome does not appear in the trials, and the evidence is a pilot.** Partly real. Verified: a double-blind randomised sham-controlled PILOT trial, 20 participants, ten sessions of 10 Hz rTMS at 600 pulses and 70% resting motor threshold to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, with time effects on headache severity and functional impact, depression, post-concussion symptoms and quality of life; a 44-adult sham-controlled trial in progress; an open-label adolescent feasibility study. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) DYSAUTONOMIA IS NOT SUPPORTED. Both submitted lists make TBI-related dysautonomia a headline outcome; it does not appear among the reported outcomes of these trials, which centre on headache, cognitive symptoms, mood and quality of life. Sleep fragmentation likewise could not be confirmed. (2) The submission describes 'a prospective sham-controlled trial demonstrated' significant amelioration; this is a 20-participant pilot. (3) 'Formally integrates non-invasive neuromodulation into brain injury rehabilitation guidelines' — no guideline integration was found. YEAR: assigned to 2025; the item appeared on both submitted lists.

### 5. Powered Robotic Exoskeleton Gait Training in Inpatient Rehabilitation

*Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation and related trials · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A randomised controlled trial of overground robotic exoskeleton gait training in people with incomplete spinal cord injury during inpatient rehabilitation has been reported, alongside the FIRST trial programme examining the active ingredients of robotic gait training in the same population. In stroke, powered exoskeletons can be used safely for gait training and deliver consistent high-dose repetition with balance and stability support; the comparative finding is that exoskeletal gait training is broadly equivalent to traditional therapy in chronic stroke, with the possibility of added benefit in sub-acute patients.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The value proposition is dose and therapist load rather than superiority. An exoskeleton lets one therapist deliver more repetitions with less physical strain, which matters in a workforce where manual body-weight support injures staff.

**Practical implications.** Prescribe it to increase repetitions in a patient who can tolerate them, not on the expectation of a better outcome than conventional gait training — the trial evidence in chronic stroke shows equivalence. Sub-acute patients are where added benefit is most plausible. Note that no AAPM&R/ACRM joint consensus document defining dosing, safety parameters and patient selection could be located, so selection remains a local decision.

[Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation — overground robotic exoskeleton gait training in incomplete spinal cord injury during inpatient rehabilitation: a randomized control trial](https://www.archives-pmr.org/article/S0003-9993(25)00675-6/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND as cited — the consensus document could not be located.** The submitted framing does not check out. Both PM&R lists describe an 'AAPM&R / ACRM Joint Consensus Guidelines' framework establishing standardised dosing, safety protocols and patient selection criteria for exoskeleton gait training. No such joint consensus document surfaced. Real evidence has been substituted: a 2025 randomised controlled trial of overground robotic exoskeleton gait training in incomplete SCI during inpatient rehabilitation (Arch Phys Med Rehabil), the FIRST trial programme, and the post-stroke literature. IMPORTANT DIRECTIONAL CORRECTION: the submission implies exoskeletons accelerate mobility restoration. In chronic stroke, exoskeletal gait training is reported as broadly EQUIVALENT to traditional therapy, with possible added benefit only in sub-acute patients — so the honest case is dose and reduced therapist strain, not superior recovery. The submission's point about reducing physical strain on therapists is well founded and is kept. YEAR: assigned to 2025; the item appeared on both submitted lists.

### 6. Markerless AI Motion Analysis: What It Can and Cannot Measure

*Sensors, Gait & Posture and validation studies · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Markerless computer-vision motion capture has been validated against reference standards. A 3D markerless system combining depth cameras and pose estimators, compared with the OptiTrack optoelectronic system, showed excellent test-retest reliability and moderate-to-good accuracy. The KinaTrax system, validated against an instrumented walkway in stroke survivors, agreed well to excellently on most spatiotemporal parameters — but poorly on stride width and paretic single-limb support time. Purpose-built validation datasets now combine synchronised multi-camera video, marker-based capture and force plates.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Brings quantitative gait measurement within reach of an ordinary clinic room, and simultaneously defines where it is not yet trustworthy.

**Practical implications.** Two limits belong in every discussion of these systems. Accuracy is moderate-to-good rather than equivalent to a gait laboratory, and the parameters that agree worst — stride width, paretic single-limb support — are precisely the ones a physiatrist assessing a hemiparetic patient cares about. And a camera cannot measure joint loading: ground reaction force requires a force plate or instrumented insole, so any claim to quantify dynamic joint loads from video alone is measuring something else. Use markerless capture for spatiotemporal parameters and progress tracking, and validate against your own reference before it informs decisions.

[Validity of AI-driven markerless motion capture for spatiotemporal gait analysis in stroke survivors](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/25/17/5315)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — accuracy overstated and one claimed measurement is physically impossible.** The technology and the validation studies are real, but the submission overstates both. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) ACCURACY: both lists say markerless systems 'match traditional force-plate and 3D optoelectronic motion-capture accuracy'. They do not — reported accuracy against optoelectronic reference is moderate-to-good with excellent reliability, and against an instrumented walkway in stroke survivors agreement was POOR for stride width and paretic single-limb support time, which are clinically central in hemiparesis. (2) PHYSICS: the submission says clinicians can 'track dynamic joint loads' using standard tablet or smartphone cameras. Joint loading requires ground reaction force, which video cannot measure; this is not a matter of accuracy but of what the instrument senses. Corrected explicitly because it would otherwise be repeated. (3) 'Endorses markerless digital motion tracking within sports medicine and musculoskeletal PM&R assessment guidelines' — no guideline endorsement was found. YEAR: assigned to 2025; the item appeared on both submitted lists.

### 7. Continuous Peripheral Nerve Catheters in Cancer Rehabilitation

*Case series and the continuous peripheral nerve block literature · 2012 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Continuous peripheral nerve blocks are used for cancer-related pain, including brachial plexus involvement in metastatic breast cancer and sarcoma. Continuous femoral nerve blocks provide satisfactory analgesia with improved rehabilitation and shorter length of stay compared with opiates, and the documented benefits of continuous catheters include reduced baseline and dynamic pain, lower additional analgesic requirement, fewer opioid-related side effects and improved ambulation and function. Ambulatory catheters maintained for a median of five days did not carry an increased complication rate in the Cleveland Clinic series.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Offers a way to make a patient with severe focal cancer pain able to participate in therapy without the sedation that systemic opioid escalation brings — which is the specific obstacle in cancer rehabilitation.

**Practical implications.** Consider a continuous catheter for well-localised pain from bone metastasis or tumour plexopathy where the therapy goal is participation rather than comfort alone. Note the evidence base honestly: this rests on case series and the general continuous nerve block literature, not on a controlled trial in cancer rehabilitation, and no practice standard formally links interventional pain procedures into cancer rehabilitation pathways. The five-day median duration is a useful practical benchmark for ambulatory use.

[The role of continuous peripheral nerve blocks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3385590/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND as described — real supporting literature substituted.** The submitted citation could not be verified. The 2025 list describes a 'clinical trial in oncology rehabilitation showing that ambulatory, ultrasound-guided continuous peripheral nerve blocks dramatically increase physical therapy participation and functional mobility' in tumour plexopathy and painful bone metastases. No such trial was found. Substituted with what is documented: continuous peripheral nerve blocks in cancer pain including brachial plexus involvement, continuous femoral blocks giving satisfactory analgesia with improved rehabilitation and shorter stay versus opiates, reduced analgesic requirement and improved ambulation, and the Cleveland Clinic ambulatory catheter series with a median five-day duration and no excess complications. The submission's claim that this 'formalizes clinical practice standards linking interventional pain procedures directly into... cancer rehabilitation pathways' is not supported and has been removed. The clinical rationale — regional analgesia to preserve cognition and stamina for therapy — is sound and is kept. YEAR: 2025 as submitted; this was one of only two items unique to the 2025 list.

### 8. Structured Home Exercise Is Sufficient in Meniscal Tear with Knee Osteoarthritis (TeMPO)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 29 October 2025*

**The breakthrough.** 879 patients aged 45 to 85 with knee pain, osteoarthritis and a degenerative meniscal tear were randomised to four arms: a 3-month structured home-exercise programme alone; home exercise plus adherence text messages; home exercise plus texts plus sham in-clinic physical therapy; and home exercise plus texts plus standard in-clinic physical therapy. All four improved substantially, and adding supervised physical therapy produced no meaningful additional pain relief at 3 months — the difference versus home exercise alone was 2.5 points and was not statistically significant.

**Clinical guideline impact.** For rehabilitation prescribing this is the more consequential reading of the trial: the active ingredient is the exercise itself, and neither therapist supervision nor adherence prompting added measurable benefit on top of it.

**Practical implications.** Prescribe a structured home programme as a complete first-line treatment rather than as the option for patients who cannot attend. Direct supervised physical therapy towards patients who need manual therapy, cannot exercise unsupervised, or are not improving — a rationing decision the trial now supports on evidence rather than on capacity. Note that this is a 3-month outcome.

[NEJM — A Randomized Trial of Physical Therapy for Meniscal Tear and Knee Pain](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2503385)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the same misreading corrected in the orthopaedics list.** Real trial (TeMPO, NEJMoa2503385, 29 October 2025, n=879). THE FINDING WAS STATED BACKWARDS, in the same way as in the orthopaedic surgery submission processed earlier: the PM&R list says the trial demonstrated that 'individual hands-on PT provides modest additional benefits'. It did not — no meaningful additional pain relief came from standard PT, sham PT or adherence texts on top of home exercise, with a 2.5-point difference that was not statistically significant. Corrected, and framed for rehabilitation prescribing rather than surgical decision-making so it complements rather than repeats the orthopaedics entry. CROSS-SPECIALTY DUPLICATION, deliberate: this trial is also live under orthopaedic surgery. The two entries address different decisions — whether to operate versus what to prescribe — on the same basis as the MeVO trials appearing under both emergency medicine and neurology. YEAR: assigned to 2025 to match the publication date; the submission placed it on the 2026 list.

### 1. Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Clinical Practice Guideline, Revision 2026

*Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy 2026;56(4) · April 2026*

**The breakthrough.** APTA Orthopedics and the APTA Hand and Upper Extremity Academy jointly revised the carpal tunnel syndrome guideline, linked to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health. Clinicians should use the CTS-6 to help diagnose suspected carpal tunnel syndrome; the Boston Carpal Tunnel Questionnaire Symptom Severity Scale at baseline and at least one further point including discharge to track symptom change; and either the DASH or QuickDASH to track function, in both surgically and non-surgically managed patients.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Separates the three jobs that get conflated in practice — diagnosing, tracking symptoms and tracking function — and assigns a specific instrument to each. The ICF linkage means outcomes are expressed as function rather than as nerve conduction velocity.

**Practical implications.** Adopt the measurement set: CTS-6 to diagnose, BCTQ symptom severity at baseline and follow-up, DASH or QuickDASH for function. The DASH requirement is the one most often missed — symptom scores alone will not show whether a hand is working better. Diagnostic accuracy for the CTS-6 rests on limited evidence, alongside the Kamath and Stothard questionnaire and the Katz and Stirrat hand diagram, so it supports rather than replaces clinical reasoning.

[JOSPT — Hand Pain and Sensory Deficits: Carpal Tunnel Syndrome, Revision 2026](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2026.0301)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — real, one instrument missing and 'mandates' softened.** Verified: JOSPT 2026;56(4), doi 10.2519/jospt.2026.0301, published April 2026, jointly revised by APTA Orthopedics and the APTA Hand and Upper Extremity Academies, ICF-linked, with the CTS-6 recommended for diagnosis and the BCTQ Symptom Severity Scale at baseline plus at least one follow-up. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The DASH or QuickDASH recommendation for assessing FUNCTION was missing from the submission, which lists only CTS-6 and BCTQ — an incomplete measurement set is the most likely way this guideline gets implemented wrongly. (2) The submission says the guideline 'Mandates standardized baseline and outcome evaluation... prior to electrodiagnostic testing or surgical referral'. These are recommendations linked to instruments, and the sequencing relative to electrodiagnostic testing as stated could not be confirmed; reworded to what the guideline recommends. Added that CTS-6 diagnostic accuracy rests on limited evidence, which the guideline itself notes. YEAR: correctly assigned to 2026 — the guideline is titled Revision 2026 and published April 2026.

### 2. First Breakthrough Device Designation for a Brain-Computer Interface in Stroke Motor Rehabilitation

*FDA regulatory milestone · April 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The FDA granted Breakthrough Device Designation to CorTec's Brain Interchange system for direct cortical electrical stimulation to support motor recovery in chronic stroke-related impairment — the first brain-computer interface worldwide to receive the designation for stroke motor rehabilitation. The system is fully implantable and wireless, designed for long-term neural recording with adaptive closed-loop stimulation. It is being evaluated in an FDA-approved IDE study at the University of Washington, the first clinical investigation of a fully implantable wireless BCI for stroke rehabilitation in humans, following 510(k) clearance of the company's cortical mapping electrodes in 2019 and a 2023 feasibility demonstration of beta-phase-locked closed-loop stimulation.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Signals that regulators regard closed-loop cortical stimulation as a plausible route to recovery in patients whose motor function has plateaued — a group conventional rehabilitation currently discharges.

**Practical implications.** Nothing changes in clinic yet, and it is important to say why. Breakthrough Device Designation expedites FDA interaction and review; it does not alter the requirements for marketing authorisation and does not guarantee approval, and this device remains investigational under an IDE. The realistic near-term role for a physiatrist is identifying and referring candidates to the trial, not prescribing the therapy.

[CorTec receives FDA Breakthrough Device Designation for its Brain Interchange system in stroke rehabilitation](https://cortec-neuro.com/cortec-receives-fda-breakthrough-device-designation-for-its-brain-interchange-system-in-stroke-rehabilitation-the-first-bci-worldwide-designated-for-stroke-motor-rehabilitation/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — a designation was described as a change in clinical guidelines.** Verified: FDA Breakthrough Device Designation announced April 2026 for CorTec's Brain Interchange system, the first BCI worldwide designated for stroke motor rehabilitation, fully implantable and wireless, under FDA-approved IDE study at the University of Washington, with 2019 510(k) clearance of cortical mapping electrodes and a 2023 beta-phase-locked closed-loop feasibility study. CORRECTION: both submitted lists say the designation 'Re-defines neuro-rehabilitation frameworks by integrating implantable bidirectional neural recording and adaptive stimulation into clinical stroke recovery guidelines' and 'Provides physiatrists with a bioelectronic tool'. Neither is true yet — this is an investigational device with an expedited-review designation, no guideline includes it, and the announcement itself states that the designation does not change the requirements for marketing authorisation and does not guarantee approval. Rewritten so the regulatory status is unambiguous, since 'provides physiatrists with a tool' would have a clinician looking for something they cannot obtain. YEAR: correctly assigned to 2026 — the designation is April 2026.

### 3. Ultrasound-Guided Needle EMG for Nerve Transfer Candidacy in Cervical Spinal Cord Injury

*Muscle & Nerve 2026 · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Combining neuromuscular ultrasound with needle electromyography improves muscle localisation and patient safety when mapping donor and recipient muscles in complex nerve injury, including the upper limb in cervical spinal cord injury. The muscles examined for donor and recipient nerve-muscle combinations include teres minor, serratus anterior, brachialis, supinator, extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis, flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus, flexor pollicis longus and extensor indicis. The supinator is a particularly valuable donor — its radial nerve branch transferred to the posterior interosseous nerve restores thumb and finger extension in patients with C5 to C7 level injuries. Normal preoperative manual muscle testing of a donor corresponded with intraoperative nerve stimulation and normal nerve architecture on postoperative histopathology.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Makes the preoperative electrodiagnostic assessment reliable enough to trust for donor selection. A subclinically denervated donor looks adequate on examination and fails after transfer, and the operation is not repeatable.

**Practical implications.** Know which operation is being planned, because the assessment differs: this work concerns NERVE transfer, where a donor nerve is rerouted to reinnervate a paralysed muscle, not tendon transfer, where an intact muscle-tendon unit is moved. The supinator-to-PIN transfer for thumb and finger extension in C5 to C7 injury is the concrete example to know. Ultrasound guidance is described as a valuable adjunct to needle localisation rather than a mandated standard.

[Muscle & Nerve — ultrasound guidance to augment needle electromyography precision in the complex nerve injury setting](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mus.70100)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — wrong operation named, and 'mandatory standard' is not supported.** The work is real: Mayo Clinic uses preoperative ultrasound-guided needle EMG for upper-limb reconstruction planning in cervical spinal cord injury, and the technique paper is in Muscle & Nerve 2026 (Saddler et al., doi 10.1002/mus.70100). TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) WRONG OPERATION. Both submitted lists describe this as assessing candidacy for TENDON TRANSFER. The Mayo material and the muscle list concern NERVE transfer — the supinator branch of the radial nerve to the posterior interosseous nerve for thumb and finger extension in C5-7 injury is the worked example. Tendon transfer moves an intact muscle-tendon unit; nerve transfer reinnervates a paralysed muscle. They have different candidacy criteria, so this is a substantive error rather than a wording slip. (2) 'Establishing ultrasound-guided EMG as a mandatory preoperative diagnostic standard' overstates it; the publication describes ultrasound as a valuable tool to augment muscle localisation and enhance safety. Also corrected 'clinical trial data' — this is a technique and practice report, not a trial. YEAR: correctly assigned to 2026.
