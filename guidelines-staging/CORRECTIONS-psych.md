## Findings stated backwards — a reader would learn the opposite of the published result

### Psychiatry 2025 #10 — Micronutrient Testing and Supplementation in Depression: What the Trials Actually Show

**REVERSED — the evidence does not support the claim, and no guideline recommends the screening**

The submission states that 'large-scale meta-analyses and clinical trials confirm[] that correcting specific micronutrient deficiencies (such as folate/B9, B12, and high-dose Vitamin D3) significantly enhances standard antidepressant treatment response and improves cardiometabolic markers', and that this 'updates major depressive disorder evaluation guidelines to recommend baseline screening for nutritional, inflammatory, and metabolic biomarkers prior to or alongside psychotropic initiation'. THE EVIDENCE RUNS THE OTHER WAY. A large randomised trial found no clear potentiation of antidepressant effect by folic acid plus B12 (effect in one of four follow-up periods only); meta-analysis found short-term folate and B12 supplementation does not improve depressive symptoms in adults already on antidepressants; benefit was found in one trial specifically in patients with low-normal B12. For vitamin D, a 31-trial dose-response meta-analysis found a SLIGHT reduction per 1,000 IU/day, in people with and without depression. No guideline recommending baseline nutritional, inflammatory and metabolic biomarker panels before psychotropic initiation was found, and the cardiometabolic claim is not an established augmentation finding. Rewritten to give the actual trial results and a defensible clinical rule — test where there is a reason, correct what is low — because the submitted version would license selling supplement panels to every patient starting an antidepressant.

## Citation not independently verified

### Psychiatry 2025 #4 — Maintenance Pharmacotherapy in Bipolar Disorder: Swedish Nationwide Cohort

**citation not independently verified — direction consistent with established evidence**

I did not verify this specific study, including the cohort size of over 105,000, the journal (submitted as The Lancet Psychiatry) or the individual hospitalisation outcomes, so none of those is asserted. The findings as described are consistent with a substantial existing literature from Swedish and Finnish national registers using within-individual designs, and with guideline positions on lithium as first-line maintenance and against antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar disorder — so the direction is credible even though the citation is unconfirmed. Rewritten to attribute the design and direction without the unverified specifics, and the limits of a within-individual observational design added, since the submission presents it as settling the question. Flagged for the physician to confirm the primary citation.

### Psychiatry 2025 #6 — Transdiagnostic Prediction of Psychosis and Bipolar Disorder in Young People

**citation not independently verified — plausible field, specific model and guideline shift not confirmed**

Transdiagnostic clinical prediction modelling in youth mental health is a real and active field, and the rationale the submission gives is sound. But I did not verify the specific validated model described, nor its publication in The Lancet Psychiatry, nor the claim that this 'transitions early-intervention guidelines away from single-disorder assessment tools toward transdiagnostic risk-screening frameworks' — that is a substantial claim about guideline change and no supporting document was found. Rewritten to describe the approach without asserting a specific validated instrument. Two limitations added that the submission omits and that determine whether such a model helps anyone: external validity across health systems, and the recognised harms of risk labelling in adolescents, which is why prodromal designation remains contested. Flagged for the physician to supply the primary citation.

### Psychiatry 2025 #7 — Psilocybin with Psychological Support in Treatment-Resistant Depression: Longer-Term Follow-Up

**citation not independently verified — journal name appears garbled**

The phase 2b psilocybin programme in treatment-resistant depression using 25 mg with psychological support is real and well known, and extended follow-up reports exist. I did not verify the specific 12-month follow-up publication or its findings, so no response or remission figure is quoted. The submitted journal, 'Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics', appears to be a garbled rendering of Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics — the citation could not be resolved either way, so it is not named. Rewritten to describe the design and to set out why naturalistic follow-up overstates durability: differential retention, continued concurrent treatment, and absence of a control group beyond the randomised window. Also stated that psilocybin is not approved for depression, which the submission's practical field does not mention. Its claim about preparation and integration sessions being 'essential' is flagged as untested rather than removed.

### Psychiatry 2025 #9 — Long-Acting Injectable Antipsychotics in First-Episode and Early-Phase Schizophrenia

**citation not independently verified — direction well supported, one claim contested**

The clinical direction is well supported in the schizophrenia literature: long-acting injectables reduce relapse and rehospitalisation relative to oral antipsychotics, particularly in early-phase illness, and the position that they should not be reserved for chronic non-adherence is widely argued. I did not verify the specific consensus document the submission cites — the link given is 'PubMed Central' generally rather than an article — so no particular statement is attributed. TWO ADDITIONS/CORRECTIONS. The submission's claim that early relapse causes 'cumulative neurostructural changes' is a contested hypothesis, not established, and the entry says so, because it is exactly the kind of claim that gets used to press a patient toward a decision. And two practical points are added that the submission omits: establishing tolerability on the oral form of the same molecule first, and the irreversibility of a depot if a serious adverse effect emerges.

## Corrected — dates, journals, numbers, strength of claim

### Psychiatry 2025 #1 — Esketamine Nasal Spray as Monotherapy for Treatment-Resistant Depression

**matched with corrections — journal unconfirmed, and the monitoring burden was omitted**

Verified: the FDA approved Spravato as the first and only monotherapy for TRD on 21 January 2025, on the basis of NCT04599855 — 378 adults, esketamine 56 mg or 84 mg versus placebo twice weekly for four weeks, LS mean differences −5.1 and −6.8 (P<.001), separation by 24 hours, remission 22.5% versus 7.6%. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) JOURNAL NOT CONFIRMED: the submission cites JAMA Psychiatry; I could not verify the publication venue, so the trial registration and the FDA approval are cited instead. (2) The effect sizes and remission rates were absent — 22.5% remission is a meaningful but modest absolute figure and belongs in any patient conversation. (3) The submission says monotherapy 'reduces polypharmacy burdens' and lists the oral-antidepressant side effects avoided, without noting that esketamine is REMS-restricted, given in a certified setting with two hours of post-dose observation for sedation, dissociation and blood pressure rise. A clinician told only the first half would misjudge what they are offering. Note also that TRD3005 — a trial name adjacent to this programme — studied esketamine WITH an oral antidepressant in patients 65 and over, and is not the monotherapy evidence.

### Psychiatry 2025 #2 — Lithium versus Quetiapine Augmentation for Treatment-Resistant Depression (LQD)

**corrected — the trial acronym is invented, and the design was open-label**

The trial is real and the direction is right: Cleare et al., The Lancet Psychiatry, April 2025, doi 10.1016/S2215-0366(25)00028-8, PMID 40350798, with quetiapine more clinically and cost-effective than lithium over 52 weeks. FOUR CORRECTIONS. (1) NAME: the submission calls it the 'LITH-QUE Trial'. It is the LQD trial — an invented acronym sends a reader looking for a study that does not exist under that name. (2) DESIGN: submitted as giving 'high-level, long-term comparative data'; it is pragmatic and OPEN-LABEL, not blinded, with 212 participants at six sites, 89% White. (3) The discontinuation figures — roughly 50% on lithium and 39% on quetiapine stopping within a year, usually for side effects — were missing and are the most practically important result. (4) The claim that quetiapine avoids 'the intensive laboratory monitoring (renal/thyroid) required for lithium' is only half the picture, since quetiapine carries metabolic monitoring, sedation and weight gain. The submitted assertion that this 'elevates atypical antipsychotic augmentation... in major depressive disorder practice guidelines' could not be confirmed and is not asserted.

### Psychiatry 2025 #5 — Xanomeline-Trospium (Cobenfy) in Schizophrenia: Implementation Guidance

**corrected — the mechanism was misdescribed and the side-effect claim is one-sided**

The drug is real and the significance is genuine: xanomeline-trospium (Cobenfy) is the first schizophrenia treatment approved that does not act through dopamine D2 blockade. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) MECHANISM: the submission calls it 'the first dual M1/M4 muscarinic receptor agonist'. That describes xanomeline alone. Trospium is a peripherally restricted muscarinic ANTAGONIST, included to reduce peripheral cholinergic adverse effects — so the product is an agonist-plus-antagonist combination, and describing it as a dual agonist misrepresents why the second molecule is there. (2) The submission says it treats positive, negative and cognitive symptoms 'without exposing patients to extrapyramidal side effects, tardive dyskinesia, hyperprolactinemia, or metabolic weight gain' — listing only the harms avoided. The gastrointestinal burden is substantial and is a leading cause of discontinuation, and hepatic safety requires attention; both are now stated. The specific consensus panel report in The Journal of Clinical Psychiatry could not be verified, so the entry cites the prescribing information and describes the implementation literature generically. Its claim that guidelines now position muscarinic agonists as early-line is not asserted.

### Psychiatry 2025 #8 — CT-155, a Prescription Digital Therapeutic for Negative Symptoms of Schizophrenia (CONVOKE)

**corrected — a protocol was cited as results**

CT-155 is a real investigational prescription digital therapeutic for experiential negative symptoms of schizophrenia and CONVOKE is its trial programme. THE CORRECTION IS THE EVIDENCE STATUS. The submission describes 'results from the landmark CONVOKE Phase 3 trial' and says the findings 'incorporate prescription digital therapeutics into evidence-based guidelines as standard adjunctive care' — while citing JMIR Research Protocols, a journal that publishes study protocols. A protocol is a plan, not a result, and no results publication or guideline incorporation was verified. Rewritten so the entry conveys what the intervention targets and what to ask of the eventual evidence, without asserting efficacy. Added the comparator point — sham app versus waitlist — because engagement-dependent interventions in a condition defined partly by avolition are especially vulnerable to it. The specific url could not be resolved to an article, so the journal is cited; flagged for the physician to supply the results paper if it exists.

## Matched — no correction needed

### Psychiatry 2025 #3 — Serial Ketamine Infusions Added to Inpatient Care for Depression (KARMA-Dep 2)

**matched**

Verified and accurately summarised, including the negative primary outcome and the use of midazolam as an active comparator: JAMA Psychiatry, PMID 41123905, investigator-led double-blind midazolam-controlled pragmatic trial at an academic centre in Ireland, September 2021 to August 2024, up to eight infusions twice weekly over four weeks, baseline MADRS ≥20, no significant difference in end-of-treatment MADRS or in secondary efficacy, cognitive, economic or quality-of-life outcomes. Details added rather than corrected: the single-centre design, the inclusion of bipolar as well as unipolar episodes, and the infusion schedule. The submission's framing that the trial highlights 'the significant role of unblinding and expectancy effects in IV ketamine trials' is a fair inference — midazolam was chosen to preserve blinding — but is presented here as the interpretation it is rather than as a finding. Also bounded what the trial does not address (single-dose ketamine for suicidality, outpatient esketamine, ECT), since the submission's practical advice reads more broadly than the evidence.
