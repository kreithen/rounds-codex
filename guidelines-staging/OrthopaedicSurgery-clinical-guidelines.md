### 1. Preoperative Gait Speed as a Predictor of Patient-Reported Outcomes After Total Hip Arthroplasty

*The Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery · March 2026*

**The breakthrough.** In 274 patients followed after primary THA for osteoarthritis, preoperative 10-metre free gait speed was a significant independent predictor of midterm patient-reported outcomes. A preoperative speed of 1.0 m/s separated those who reached the Patient Acceptable Symptom State (Oxford Hip Score ≥42, Forgotten Joint Score-12 ≥50) from those who did not, and the threshold held regardless of age.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Supports adding a timed walk to the standard preoperative workup alongside the questionnaires. Gait speed is a two-minute measurement that captures function a PROM asks the patient to estimate.

**Practical implications.** Use it to flag the patient likely to be dissatisfied and to justify prehabilitation before an elective hip. Read the design before quoting it: this is a retrospective cohort, Level III prognostic evidence, in a single Asian population. It shows that slow walkers do worse, not that making them faster changes the outcome — no prehabilitation trial has tested the threshold.

[JBJS — preoperative gait speed and PROMs after THA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41296839/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — study design, publication year and the strength of the claim.** Real study, correctly identified. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) Design: submitted as a 'prospective clinical study'; it is a retrospective cohort of 274 patients operated 2012-2018, Level III prognostic. (2) Date: published in JBJS on 4 March 2026, not 2025. (3) The submitted phrase 'the single most reliable predictor of long-term postoperative satisfaction' overstates it — the paper reports gait speed as a significant predictor among several preoperative variables (symptom duration, pain intensity, hip range of motion, muscle strength), and the outcome window is midterm, not long-term. Added the PASS cut-offs and the point that a prognostic association is not evidence prehabilitation works, which is how this result will be misused.

### 2. Physical Therapy Adds Nothing to Structured Home Exercise in Meniscal Tear with Knee Osteoarthritis (TeMPO)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 29 October 2025*

**The breakthrough.** 879 patients aged 45 to 85 with knee pain, osteoarthritis and a degenerative meniscal tear were randomised across four arms: a 3-month structured home-exercise programme alone, home exercise plus adherence text messages, home exercise plus texts plus sham in-clinic physical therapy, and home exercise plus texts plus standard in-clinic physical therapy. All four groups improved substantially. Adding supervised physical therapy produced no meaningful additional pain relief at 3 months — the difference versus home exercise alone was 2.5 points and was not statistically significant.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reinforces non-operative management as first line over early arthroscopic debridement or meniscectomy, and goes further: the effective ingredient is the exercise, not the supervision. Adherence texting and in-clinic attendance both failed to add benefit.

**Practical implications.** A structured home programme is a complete first-line treatment, not a compromise for patients who cannot get to therapy. Reserve in-person PT for patients who need manual therapy, cannot exercise unsupervised, or are not improving. Note this is a 3-month outcome in a 4-arm trial run at Brigham and Women's, Buffalo, Cleveland Clinic and Pittsburgh.

[NEJM — A Randomized Trial of Physical Therapy for Meniscal Tear and Knee Pain](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2503385)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the result was submitted in the opposite direction.** Real trial (TeMPO, NEJMoa2503385, 29 Oct 2025, n=879). THE FINDING WAS STATED BACKWARDS. The submission says supervised physical therapy offered 'modest additional benefit over self-directed home exercise programs alone'. The trial found the opposite: no meaningful additional pain relief from standard PT, sham PT or adherence texts on top of home exercise, difference 2.5 points and not significant. This is a negative trial and its clinical value is precisely that it is negative — rewritten to say so, with the four arms named so the reader can see what was actually compared.

### 3. 2025 AAOS Clinical Practice Guideline: Management of Rotator Cuff Injuries

*American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons · Approved August 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The AAOS replaced its 2019 rotator cuff guideline with 21 recommendations and eight options. The emphasis of the update is the healing environment rather than the indication for surgery: a strong recommendation now supports bioinductive implants during repair to reduce retear and improve patient-reported outcomes; routine acromioplasty is not suggested alongside arthroscopic repair of small-to-medium full-thickness tears; and MRI, MR arthrography, CT and ultrasound are all endorsed as adjuncts to examination and radiographs for identifying tears.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Moves the guideline conversation from whether to repair towards how to make a repair hold. The acromioplasty statement withdraws support for a step still performed reflexively in many repairs.

**Practical implications.** Two changes alter what you do this week: consider a bioinductive implant in a repair at risk of retear, and stop adding acromioplasty to a small-to-medium full-thickness repair for therapeutic benefit. Imaging choice is a matter of availability rather than accuracy.

[AAOS — updated CPG for the management of rotator cuff injuries](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/aaos-updates-clinical-practice-guideline-for-the-management-of-rotator-cuff-injuries/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the guideline is real, the described content was not its emphasis.** The guideline exists and the date is right: AAOS Board approved it in August 2025, replacing the 2019 version, with 21 recommendations and 8 options. CORRECTION TO CONTENT. The submission describes it as clarifying conservative care versus early repair and establishing 'treatment pathways based on tear etiology, patient age, tissue quality, and symptom duration'. That is not what the 2025 update is about — AAOS's own summary frames it around optimising the healing environment: biologics, bioinductive implants, retear reduction, acromioplasty and imaging. Rewritten to the recommendations that were actually published, since the submitted version would have a resident quoting decision criteria the guideline does not contain.

### 4. Perioperative Opioid Counseling Before ACL Reconstruction (Randomized Controlled Trial)

*The Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery · July 2025*

**The breakthrough.** 121 patients undergoing ACL reconstruction were randomised 1:1 to opioid-limiting perioperative pain education — take opioids only as a last resort — or to conventional counselling to take opioids as needed for severe pain and 'stay ahead of the pain'. Within 3 months the last-resort group used a median of 15 mg of oxycodone versus 53 mg in the control group (mean total morphine equivalents 46.0 versus 63.6 mg, p < 0.001). Mean pain on the numeric rating scale over the first 14 days was 2.5 versus 2.4 (p = 0.82), with similar sleep quality and satisfaction.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Direct randomised evidence that how the prescription is framed, not just how large it is, drives consumption. Supports standardised preoperative pain education as a formal step in outpatient sports medicine and arthroscopy.

**Practical implications.** The intervention is a script, not a drug or a device, and it costs nothing. Counsel opioids as a rescue rather than a schedule, and expect roughly a two-thirds reduction in what the patient takes with no change in reported pain. Single-centre, 121 patients — large enough for the consumption endpoint, not powered for rare harms.

[JBJS — perioperative opioid counseling for patients undergoing ACL reconstruction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40440513/)

> **Citation check — matched.** Verified and accurate as submitted, including the 15 mg versus 53 mg median oxycodone figures and the absence of any pain or satisfaction penalty. Randomised trial of 121 patients at the University of Maryland Rehabilitation & Orthopaedic Institute, JBJS 16 July 2025. Two small precisions added rather than corrected: the trial is labelled perioperative (not purely preoperative) counselling, and the mean total morphine equivalents (46.0 versus 63.6 mg) are quoted alongside the medians because the medians make the effect look larger than the means do. Sample size stated so the entry does not read as definitive on safety.

### 5. 2025 AAOS Clinical Practice Guideline Update: Management of Acute Compartment Syndrome

*American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons · Published 18 November 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The five-yearly review of the AAOS acute compartment syndrome guideline was released. This is a minor update: one recommendation changed — the strength of evidence for Associated Fracture moved from Consensus (one star) to Limited (two stars) — with new supporting evidence added and the remaining recommendations unchanged.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Confirms rather than revises the existing framework for diagnosing and treating acute compartment syndrome in traumatised extremities. The value of reading it is knowing which of your practices rest on consensus and which now have limited evidence behind them.

**Practical implications.** Applies to adults with extremity trauma. It cannot be extrapolated to children or adolescents, and it does not address chronic exertional compartment syndrome. Do not expect new pressure thresholds or fasciotomy timing rules from this edition — the underlying evidence base has not changed enough to produce them, which is itself the message.

[AAOS — acute compartment syndrome CPG 2025](https://www.aaos.org/acscpg2025)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — real guideline, the scope of the update was overstated.** The 2025 update is real and the date is right (published 18 November 2025). CORRECTION. The submission claims it 'offers refined diagnostic parameters and surgical thresholds' and 'standardizes diagnostic protocols, continuous intracompartmental pressure monitoring criteria, and urgency timelines for surgical fasciotomy'. AAOS describes the opposite: a routine five-year review that 'received minor adjustments, primarily in the form of a single updated recommendation and the addition of supporting evidence', the single change being the Associated Fracture recommendation moving from Consensus to Limited. Rewritten to what changed, and the scope limits (adults only, not chronic exertional) added because both are stated explicitly in the guideline.

### 6. The "Quiet Knee" Rehabilitation Protocol After Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty

*The Journal of Arthroplasty / AAHKS Annual Meeting · 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A retrospective cohort from the Hospital for Special Surgery compared a supervised 10-day 'quiet knee' protocol — limited passive range of motion, restricted ambulation, aggressive cryotherapy and oedema control with telerehabilitation oversight — against conventional hands-on aggressive rehabilitation after primary TKA. Opioid exposure was lower with the quiet knee: 52.5 versus 67.5 inpatient morphine milligram equivalents and 265.5 versus 332.1 MME over 90 days after discharge, a reduction of more than 25%. In adjusted models conventional rehabilitation used 15 MME more as an inpatient and 58 MME more after discharge. Length of stay was shorter, with no detriment to pain, function or major complications.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Challenges the assumption that immediate aggressive range of motion is required after knee replacement. Rest and swelling control early did not cost motion later in this cohort.

**Practical implications.** Worth trialling in patients who struggle with early therapy pain, provided motion is tracked so a genuinely stiffening knee is still caught. Read this as an association: it is a retrospective cohort, not a randomised comparison, so the protocol travelled with a whole care pathway and the opioid difference cannot be attributed to the rehabilitation alone.

[Quiet knee rehabilitation after primary TKA — retrospective cohort](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41932408/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — design and one outcome that was not measured.** Real: HSS, presented at the AAHKS annual meeting and published as 'Quiet Knee Rehabilitation Protocol After Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty Is Associated With Lower Opioid Exposure and No Added Risks: A Retrospective Cohort Study'. The 25% opioid reduction is accurate and the MME figures are quoted from the paper. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) Design: submitted as a 'clinical evaluation... demonstrated that shifting... significantly reduced'; it is retrospective and the paper's own title says 'associated with'. (2) The submission claims it 'significantly reduced surgical site inflammation' — oedema control is part of the protocol, not a measured outcome; the reported outcomes were opioid exposure, length of stay, pain, function and complications. That claim removed.

### 7. Surgery vs Functional Bracing for Closed Displaced Humeral Shaft Fractures (FISH)

*JAMA · May 2020, with prespecified secondary analyses since*

**The breakthrough.** 82 adults with closed, displaced humeral shaft fractures were randomised at two Finnish university trauma centres to open reduction and plate fixation (n = 38) or functional bracing (n = 44). Function measured by DASH did not differ meaningfully between groups. The real separation was healing: 14 of the 44 braced patients developed healing problems requiring secondary surgery, and those who crossed over late had inferior outcomes out to two years compared with patients fixed at the outset.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reframes the humeral shaft decision. It is not a choice between a better and a worse functional result; it is a choice between accepting a roughly one-in-three chance of needing an operation later and having that operation now, with the knowledge that the delayed operation does worse.

**Practical implications.** Counsel bracing as a reasonable first choice with an explicit non-union rate, and set a follow-up schedule that catches failure early rather than late. In a patient in whom a second anaesthetic or a delayed recovery would be especially costly, the crossover data are the argument for primary fixation — not any claimed early functional advantage.

[JAMA — the FISH randomized clinical trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2765728)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — journal, year and the direction of the functional result.** The trial is real (FISH, Finnish Shaft of the Humerus, enrolled Nov 2012 to Jan 2018, 82 randomised). THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) Journal: published in JAMA, not The Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery. (2) Year: 2020, not 2025 — it does not belong to a 2025 review except as background. (3) DIRECTION: the submission claims surgery gave 'significantly superior short-term functional outcomes and faster return to activity at 3 months'. FISH did not show a clinically important functional advantage for surgery; its substantive finding is the 14 of 44 braced patients who needed secondary surgery and the worse outcomes in that crossover group. Rewritten around the healing and crossover data, which is the honest argument for early fixation. Also corrected 'multicenter study' to a two-centre randomised trial, and 82 patients stated so the reader knows how small it is.

### 8. An AI Care Aid for Perioperative Patient Questions in Joint Arthroplasty (HSS and Aidify)

*The Journal of Arthroplasty / AAHKS Annual Meeting · 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** HSS surgeons trained a custom-tailored AI agent on their own surgical protocols so that hip and knee replacement patients received answers reflecting their surgeon's actual instructions rather than generic content. A prospective observational pilot used a two-phase design — a 10-patient a priori cohort followed by 30 further patients undergoing primary THA or TKA — with access during the preoperative period and early recovery. Patients rated the responses highly for usefulness, understandability and completeness, and accessed the tool easily.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A demonstration that protocol-specific tuning is what makes a perioperative chatbot acceptable to patients, and an early template for how such a tool would be evaluated before deployment.

**Practical implications.** Treat this as feasibility, not effectiveness. 40 patients, and the endpoints were acceptability and perceived quality of answers. It did not measure emergency department visits, clinic call volume, protocol adherence or any clinical outcome, and it did not test the tool as a triage instrument — so it cannot yet be offered as a way to reduce unnecessary contacts.

[Journal of Arthroplasty — AI-powered care aid for perioperative inquiries in joint arthroplasty](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(26)00384-0/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — real pilot, the claimed benefits were not measured.** Real: HSS partnered with Aidify on a custom-tailored AI agent, presented at AAHKS and published in The Journal of Arthroplasty as 'Evaluating the Utility and Acceptability of an Artificial Intelligence-Powered Care Aid for Perioperative Patient Inquiries in Joint Arthroplasty Surgery'. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) Scale and design: a prospective observational pilot of 40 patients (10 a priori plus 30), which the submission does not convey when it says the platform was 'validated'. (2) The submission's practical claim — 'reduces unnecessary emergency room visits and clinic calls while improving patient compliance' — was not an outcome of this study; the endpoints were usefulness, understandability, completeness and ease of access. Removed and replaced with what was measured. 'Triage' also removed: the study did not test triage.

### 9. Platelet-Rich Plasma as an Adjunct in Osteochondral Lesions of the Talus

*Systematic review, December 2025 · December 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A systematic review of 35 studies — 8 clinical and 27 preclinical — examined platelet-rich plasma in osteochondral lesions of the talus. PRP was most often studied as an adjunct to bone marrow stimulation, and 4 of the 5 studies making that comparison reported statistically significant improvements in pain and function over bone marrow stimulation alone. The cartilage-quality rationale comes from the preclinical work, where PRP and bone marrow aspirate concentrate increase chondrocyte proliferation and synthetic capacity and blunt the catabolic effect of an inflamed joint.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Gives foot and ankle surgeons a defensible reason to add point-of-care biologics at the time of bone marrow stimulation, while being explicit that the reason is mechanistic and the clinical evidence is thin.

**Practical implications.** Reasonable as an adjunct when PRP is already available in theatre; not a substitute for adequate lesion preparation or for addressing malalignment and instability. Weigh the evidence honestly with the patient: 8 clinical studies, mostly small and not randomised, is a signal, not a standard of care, and there is no agreed PRP preparation — leucocyte-rich and leucocyte-poor formulations behave differently.

[Systematic review — PRP in osteochondral lesions of the talus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41379984/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — journal not confirmed, and the level of evidence was overstated.** A December 2025 systematic review does exist — 'The Role of Platelet-Rich Plasma in Treating Osteochondral Lesions of the Talus: A Systematic Review of Clinical and Preclinical Evidence', 35 studies (8 clinical, 27 preclinical), with 4 of 5 studies of PRP added to bone marrow stimulation reporting significant pain and function gains. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) Journal: I could not confirm JBJS Reviews as the venue, so the journal field states the study type instead of naming a journal that may be wrong. (2) The submission says it 'establishes high-level evidence supporting biologic augmentation' and 'enhanced hyaline-like cartilage repair'. Eight mostly small non-randomised clinical studies is not high-level evidence, and the cartilage-quality claim rests on preclinical and proof-of-concept work. Rewritten to separate what the clinical studies showed from what the bench work suggests, and the leucocyte-rich versus leucocyte-poor variability added since it is a live confounder in this literature.

### 10. Prior Authorization Delays Total Hip Arthroplasty Without Reducing Cost

*AAOS Annual Meeting 2025 · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The first study to quantify the time and cost of obtaining prior authorization for THA examined patients having unilateral primary THA for end-stage hip osteoarthritis under a single commercial payor from January 2020 to December 2022, with HOOS-JR and SF-12 physical component scores recorded preoperatively and at 6 months. Prior authorization was associated with significantly longer waits before surgery and lower preoperative functional scores, and it did not function as a cost-saving measure. A parallel AAHKS membership survey found members reporting high administrative burden (93%), negative clinical consequences (87%) and delays in timely access (96%), most often triggered by a requirement that non-operative treatment had not been tried for long enough.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Gives orthopaedic advocacy an outcome measure rather than an anecdote: the patient reaches the operating room in worse condition, and the payer does not save money.

**Practical implications.** Refer early and document non-operative treatment explicitly and with dates, since the commonest denial reason is that conservative care was not tried long enough. Note the limits of the data — one commercial payor, one institution, 6-month follow-up — and note what it does not contain: this study did not report opioid use, so a claim that prior authorization increases preoperative opioid consumption cannot be supported from it.

[AAOS 2025 — prior authorization does not reduce costs for total hip arthroplasty](https://aaos-annualmeeting-presskit.org/2025/research-news/new-study-shows-prior-authorization-does-not-reduce-costs-for-total-hip-arthroplasty-delays-patient-care/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — one claimed finding is not in the study.** Real, presented at the 2025 AAOS Annual Meeting; the longer wait times and lower preoperative functional scores are accurate, and the headline finding — that prior authorization was not an effective cost-saving measure — was missing from the submission and has been added. CORRECTION: the submission asserts 'increased preoperative opioid use'. No opioid endpoint appears in this study; its patient-reported measures were HOOS-JR and SF-12 PCS. That claim has been removed and the entry says explicitly that it cannot be supported, because it is the kind of plausible detail that gets repeated once written down. Also corrected 'multi-institutional': the study describes a single commercial payor cohort.

### 1. ACL Reconstruction in Patients Over 50: Age Alone Is Not a Contraindication

*AAOS Annual Meeting 2026 · March 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A cohort of patients aged 50 and over undergoing ACL reconstruction, followed for a mean of just over six years, reported 83.9% very good or excellent outcomes, 91.5% saying surgery met expectations and 88.2% reaching an acceptable symptom state. Graft failure was 7.1% and conversion to total knee arthroplasty was low.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Argues that chronological age alone should not exclude a patient from ACL reconstruction, and shifts the indication towards activity demand, instability and functional goals.

**Practical implications.** Offer reconstruction to the active 55-year-old with an unstable knee rather than defaulting to bracing and activity restriction. Read the caveat with the result: this is a single cohort with no younger comparison group, so it shows good outcomes in appropriately selected — effectively physiologically young — patients over 50, not equivalence with younger patients. Six-year follow-up leaves late graft survival and progression to arthroplasty unanswered.

[AAOS Now — patients over 50 have high satisfaction and low failure rates in ACL reconstruction](https://www.aaos.org/aaosnow/2026/mar/research/research03/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — no comparison group, so the equivalence claim is not supported.** Real, presented at the 2026 AAOS Annual Meeting in New Orleans, and the numbers are accurate (83.9% very good/excellent, 91.5% expectations met, 88.2% acceptable symptom state, 7.1% graft failure, mean follow-up just over six years). CORRECTION: the submission says over-50s 'experience identical functional gains and graft survival rates from ACL reconstruction as younger demographics'. This was a single-cohort series with no younger control arm — 'identical to younger patients' is not a finding it can produce. The AAOS report itself cautions that the results likely reflect appropriately selected, physiologically young patients and that longer follow-up is needed. Rewritten to the outcomes actually reported, with the selection caveat kept in the practical field where a reader will see it.

### 2. 2026 AAOS Clinical Practice Guideline: Management of Ankle Osteoarthritis

*American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons · Published 2 July 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The first AAOS guideline to cover both non-operative and operative treatment of ankle osteoarthritis. It contains only two recommendations — both on injections, intra-articular hyaluronic acid and intra-articular platelet-rich plasma — together with twelve option statements covering home exercise programmes, opioids, NSAIDs and acetaminophen and others. The guideline states plainly that gaps in the evidence prevented stronger guidance and identifies where research is needed.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The finding is the absence of evidence. Ankle osteoarthritis disproportionately affects working-age adults, with 70 to 80% of cases post-traumatic after injury or chronic instability, and the specialty cannot yet grade its own principal operations for it.

**Practical implications.** Do not expect this guideline to settle total ankle arthroplasty versus tibiotalar arthrodesis — it does not address that choice with a recommendation, and any decision aid claiming AAOS backing for one over the other is not citing this document. Use it for what it does cover: where injections sit, and which non-operative measures have option-level support.

[AAOS — clinical practice guideline for management of ankle osteoarthritis](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/aaos-releases-clinical-practice-guideline-for-management-of-ankle-osteoarthritis/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the guideline is real, its central claimed content is not in it.** The guideline is real and the date is right (published 2 July 2026; the first AAOS CPG covering non-operative and operative ankle OA). MAJOR CORRECTION. The submission's substance is that it 'delivers clear evidence-based decision trees contrasting total ankle arthroplasty (TAA) against tibiotalar arthrodesis (fusion), giving TAA stronger recommendation grades for patient-reported mobility'. AAOS's own release says the guideline contains only two recommendations — intra-articular hyaluronic acid and intra-articular PRP — plus twelve options, and that research gaps prevented clear treatment guidance. There is no TAA-versus-fusion recommendation to cite, and the submitted 'standardizes surgical referral criteria and post-operative weight-bearing protocols' is likewise absent. Rewritten to what the guideline says, including the 70-80% post-traumatic figure, and the practical field warns against exactly the misattribution the submission would have created.

### 3. Subvastus Muscle-Sparing Approach in Robotic Total Knee Arthroplasty

*Journal of Clinical Orthopaedics and Trauma / meta-analysis of randomised trials · 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Elevating the vastus medialis instead of dividing the quadriceps tendon preserves the extensor mechanism, and the approach is increasingly paired with robotic assistance. In a single-surgeon robot-assisted subvastus series of 81 patients (120 knees), 85% could perform an active straight leg raise by the second postoperative day, rising to 95% by the third, and mean length of stay was 3.4 days. A meta-analysis of randomised trials comparing subvastus and quadriceps-sparing approaches with the standard parapatellar approach favoured the muscle-sparing approaches on pain scores, range of motion, straight leg raise and the need for lateral retinacular release.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Supports the extensor mechanism, rather than incision length or navigation accuracy, as the variable that governs early recovery after knee replacement.

**Practical implications.** Early active quadriceps control is the reproducible benefit and it is what accelerates therapy. Be careful with the discharge claim: the robotic subvastus series reported a mean stay of 3.4 days, so faster quadriceps recovery is not the same as same-day discharge, which depends on the whole pathway, anaesthesia and patient selection. The approach has a learning curve and exposure is more demanding in a stiff or obese knee.

[Robot-assisted total knee arthroplasty using a modified subvastus approach](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0976566225001766)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — adoption and the discharge claim overstated.** The mechanism and the comparative data are real: a randomised-trial meta-analysis favours subvastus and quadriceps-sparing approaches over standard parapatellar on pain, range of motion, straight leg raise and lateral retinacular release, and there is a robot-assisted subvastus series of 81 patients / 120 knees with 85% active straight leg raise by day 2. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) 'Widespread adoption... has reshaped primary total knee replacement' is not supported — the robotic subvastus evidence is single-surgeon series, not registry-scale practice change. (2) The submission claims 'higher rates of same-day discharge'; the series it would rest on reported a mean length of stay of 3.4 days. Rewritten to the measured quadriceps outcomes, with the length-of-stay figure stated so the discharge claim cannot be repeated from this entry. Learning curve added — it is the reason the approach is not universal.

### 4. GLP-1 Receptor Agonists in Preoperative Arthroplasty Optimisation

*Arthroplasty Today / The Journal of Arthroplasty · 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Systematic review and meta-analysis data on perioperative GLP-1 receptor agonist use in hip and knee arthroplasty found lower 90-day periprosthetic joint infection (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.89), lower 90-day all-cause revision (OR 0.88, 0.82 to 0.95) and lower 90-day readmission (OR 0.79, 0.70 to 0.89), without an increase in short-term medical or aspiration complications. In patients with morbid obesity the effect is larger: 90-day periprosthetic joint infection 1.6% versus 3.2% (p = 0.03), readmission 6.9% versus 9.7%, any medical complication 10.5% versus 14.1%. Separate perioperative protocols that continued metformin, titrated insulin and added a preoperative GLP-1 agonist reported a 43% reduction in periprosthetic joint infection.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes metabolic optimisation as part of the arthroplasty pathway rather than a hurdle before it, alongside anaesthetic protocols for holding weekly doses to limit aspiration risk under general anaesthesia.

**Practical implications.** Three counterweights belong in the same conversation. A one-year increase in periprosthetic fracture risk has been reported (OR 1.49, 1.08 to 2.07), predominantly after TKA. Malnutrition is common in patients taking GLP-1 agonists before joint replacement, so check nutritional status rather than assuming weight loss is benign. And a JBJS analysis found no mid-term benefit after total joint arthroplasty — the signal is concentrated in early infection, revision and readmission, not in long-term function.

[Arthroplasty Today — GLP-1 receptor agonists in hip and knee arthroplasty: systematic review and meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352344126000920)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — direction confirmed, three counter-findings added.** Verified and, if anything, better supported than submitted: the perioperative infection, revision and readmission benefits are real with the effect sizes quoted, the morbid obesity subgroup figures check out, and the perioperative holding protocol for aspiration risk is accurate. Nothing was reversed. THREE ADDITIONS the submission omitted, all of which change the counselling: the reported one-year increase in periprosthetic fracture risk (OR 1.49, mainly TKA), the high prevalence of malnutrition in GLP-1 users presenting for arthroplasty, and the JBJS finding of no mid-term benefit. An entry that gives only the benefits would have a resident recommending these drugs preoperatively without mentioning the fracture signal.

### 5. Sensor-Enabled "Smart" Knee Implants and Remote Therapeutic Monitoring

*The Journal of Arthroplasty and related series · FDA authorisation August 2021, outcome data 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The Persona IQ knee with the canturio tibial extension carries an inertial measurement unit — a gyroscope and accelerometer — reporting through the Canary Health Implanted Reporting Processor to surgeon and patient. It received FDA market release authorisation on 30 August 2021. Outcome data have now followed: manipulation under anaesthesia was performed in 0.97% of smart implant recipients versus 3.10% of conventional implant recipients, with objective gait data directing earlier intervention in patients heading for stiffness.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Converts postoperative range of motion from something assessed at a clinic visit into a continuously observed variable, and the manipulation rate difference is the first concrete clinical endpoint attached to it.

**Practical implications.** What it measures is kinematics: step count, cadence, stride length, walking speed and range of motion. It does not measure temperature and it is not an infection detector, so it cannot flag a developing periprosthetic infection. The device is labelled an adjunct to other measurements, not a diagnostic. The realistic use is catching the knee that is not gaining motion in time to intervene before arthrofibrosis is established.

[Smart implantable device-enabled remote monitoring and manipulation under anaesthesia after TKA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12645511/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — two capabilities described do not exist in the device.** The technology and the outcome are real: Persona IQ with the canturio tibial extension (Canary Medical), FDA market release authorisation 30 August 2021, reporting through the CHIRP system, with manipulation under anaesthesia at 0.97% versus 3.10% for conventional implants. TWO CORRECTIONS, both clinically material. (1) The submission lists 'localized thermal changes' among the tracked metrics — the implant contains an inertial measurement unit (gyroscope and accelerometer) and measures kinematics; there is no temperature sensor. (2) It follows that 'clinicians can remotely identify... infection weeks before symptoms manifest' is not a capability of this device; the manufacturer's indication is objective kinematic data as an adjunct. Both removed, with the stiffness/manipulation use case — which is what the data actually show — put in their place. ### 6. Bioresorbable Magnesium Alloy Implants in Orthopaedic Trauma

*Biomaterials Research and related reviews · 2024 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Magnesium alloy hardware provides fixation and then resorbs. Its mechanical case is a density of 1.7 to 2.0 g/cm³ and an elastic modulus around 45 GPa, both close to cortical bone, which limits stress shielding in a way steel and titanium cannot. Magnesium ions released during degradation also appear to be osteoinductive, and most published clinical series report good outcomes with no second operation for implant removal.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Offers a route out of the removal operation and the long-term consequences of retained metal — stress shielding, cold sensitivity and metallic debris — in selected fractures and soft-tissue fixation.

**Practical implications.** Read the barriers before adopting. In vivo degradation kinetics remain unpredictable and corrosion can be too rapid; radiolucent zones appear around degrading implants and are not fully understood, which makes follow-up radiographs hard to interpret; long-term safety data are limited, testing protocols are not standardised and regulatory status is unsettled. Timelines quoted for complete resorption vary between devices and sites and should not be given to a patient as a fixed figure.

[Biological effects of magnesium-based implants and their clinical implications in orthopaedic trauma surgery](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11665827/)

### 7. Timing of Geriatric Hip Fracture Fixation: What the Data Support

*European Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery & Traumatology and related cohorts · 2024 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** In a cohort of more than 30,000 patients, short-term mortality was lowest for surgery performed 6 to 15 hours after admission but remained essentially uniform across the whole first 24 hours. ### 9. The "Bikini" Incision in Direct Anterior Total Hip Arthroplasty

*Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery and related series · 2021 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** An oblique skin incision aligned with the natural skin creases and tension lines, used with the muscle-sparing internervous direct anterior approach. Series report similar functional outcomes to the traditional longitudinal incision with fewer wound complications and better scar appearance, and in obese patients the bikini incision was not associated with an increased complication rate compared with non-obese patients. A single-surgeon series of 215 cases found it safe even with large acetabular cups of 56 mm or more.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Addresses the specific weakness of the direct anterior approach in a high-BMI patient — a vertical incision sitting in an abdominal skin fold — while keeping the internervous plane, which matters as anterior hip replacement moves into ambulatory surgery centres.

**Practical implications.** One trade-off is consistently reported and belongs in the consent discussion: a prospective ultrasonographic, electrophysiological and clinical study found the bikini incision increases lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury, since the transverse incision crosses the nerve's course rather than running along it. Most numbness is transient but it is more common than with a longitudinal incision. Note also that the evidence base is single-surgeon series and narrative reviews rather than randomised trials.

[Bikini anterior hip replacements in obese patients are not associated with an increased risk of complication](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34510240/)

### 10. Extended Antibiotic Prophylaxis in High-Risk Arthroplasty: Genuine Equipoise, Not a Negative Result

*The Journal of Arthroplasty · 2024 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The evidence on extended oral antibiotic prophylaxis after primary and aseptic revision joint arthroplasty is contested rather than settled. A 2024 meta-analysis found patients receiving extended prophylaxis were 35% less likely to develop periprosthetic joint infection than those given standard care, and several series report that a 7-day postoperative course — most often cefadroxil — reduced one-year infection rates in patients at high risk. Other studies found no difference, and some found higher infection rates.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The reason not to give extended prophylaxis routinely is not that benefit has been excluded. It is that trial quality is weak and heterogeneous, and that the costs — antimicrobial resistance, Clostridioides difficile infection and other antibiotic-related adverse events — fall on patients who may not benefit.

**Practical implications.** Decide by risk, not by default in either direction. The measures with the strongest support are unglamorous and unchanged: glycaemic control, nasal decolonisation, and rigorous sterile technique. If extended oral prophylaxis is used, restrict it to a defined high-risk group with a documented rationale rather than adding it to every discharge prescription.

[Extended prophylactic antibiotics for primary and aseptic revision total joint arthroplasty: a meta-analysis](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(24)00014-7/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the submitted finding runs against the weight of the evidence.** REVERSED. The submission states that trials of aggressive non-standard prophylaxis 'showed no significant reduction in surgical site infections compared to standardized perioperative intravenous antibiotic protocols', and builds a recommendation against them on that basis. The literature does not support a clean negative: a 2024 meta-analysis found 35% lower odds of periprosthetic joint infection with extended prophylaxis, and 7-day courses reduced one-year infection in high-risk patients in several series — precisely the population the submission names. Other studies show no difference or higher infection rates, so the honest position is equipoise. Rewritten so the conclusion the submission reaches (do not do this routinely) is retained but rests on the real reasons — weak trial quality, resistance, C. difficile — rather than on a finding of no benefit that would not survive being looked up. The practical advice on glycaemic control, nasal decolonisation and sterile technique was sound and is kept.
