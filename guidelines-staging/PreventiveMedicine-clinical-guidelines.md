### 1. 2025 AHA/ACC Guideline for the Prevention, Detection, Evaluation and Management of High Blood Pressure in Adults

*American Heart Association / American College of Cardiology · August 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Replaces the 2017 guideline. The PREVENT risk calculator supersedes the Pooled Cohort Equations, adding renal function, statin use and social drivers of health to risk estimation. Screening for primary aldosteronism is expanded to all patients with stage 2 or resistant hypertension. Renal denervation may be reasonable to consider in selected patients with resistant hypertension and eGFR ≥40 who remain uncontrolled on optimal therapy or cannot tolerate further medication, with guidance from a multidisciplinary team experienced in both resistant hypertension and the procedure. New guidance covers diabetes, chronic kidney disease, pregnancy and stroke.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The two changes that alter everyday practice are the risk calculator and the aldosteronism screen. Primary aldosteronism is the commonest surgically correctable cause of hypertension and has been persistently underdiagnosed; extending screening to every stage 2 or resistant patient is a substantial widening.

**Practical implications.** Switch to PREVENT for risk estimation. Screen for primary aldosteronism in stage 2 or resistant hypertension rather than waiting for a suggestive potassium. Note what did NOT change: the definitions and thresholds for normal, elevated, and stage 1 and 2 hypertension are the same as 2017, as are the first-line agents — so the 130/80 framework a clinician already uses still stands. Renal denervation is a conditional option for a narrow group, not a general treatment for resistant hypertension.

[ACC — new ACC/AHA guideline on prevention, detection, evaluation and management of high blood pressure](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2025/08/14/15/36/New-ACC-AHA-Guideline-Addresses-Prevention-Detection-Evaluation-and-Management-of-HBP)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — two claims not confirmed, renal denervation qualified.** The guideline is real (August 2025) and three submitted points check out exactly: PREVENT replacing the Pooled Cohort Equations with renal function and social drivers of health added, primary aldosteronism screening expanded to all stage 2 or resistant hypertension, and renal denervation newly included. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) Renal denervation is a QUALIFIED option — selected patients, eGFR ≥40, uncontrolled on optimal therapy or intolerant, with multidisciplinary team guidance; 'formally incorporates renal denervation for resistant hypertension' reads far broader. (2) NOT CONFIRMED: the submission says the guideline 'actively encourag[es] <120/80 mmHg for most adults'. Reporting on the guideline states the definitions and stage thresholds are unchanged from 2017, and I could not confirm a <120/80 target recommendation, so it is not asserted. (3) NOT CONFIRMED: the claim that it 'establishes firm blood pressure targets to prevent cognitive impairment/dementia' and 'formally recommends target blood pressure reduction specifically to lower the risk of mild cognitive impairment and vascular dementia'. Plausible given SPRINT MIND, but I could not verify it as a recommendation in this guideline. Also softened 'routine out-of-office blood pressure monitoring is mandated' — guidelines recommend rather than mandate.

### 2. Tirzepatide for Obesity Treatment and Diabetes Prevention (SURMOUNT-1, 176 Weeks)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 13 November 2024*

**The breakthrough.** 2,539 adults with obesity — 1,032 of them with prediabetes — were randomised to weekly tirzepatide 5, 10 or 15 mg or placebo for 176 weeks, followed by a 17-week off-treatment period. Progression to type 2 diabetes fell by 94%: onset occurred in 1.3% on tirzepatide versus 13.3% on placebo, leaving 98.7% diabetes-free. Mean weight change at 176 weeks was −12.3% at 5 mg, −18.7% at 10 mg and −19.7% at 15 mg, against −1.3% on placebo.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Repositions a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist as diabetes prevention rather than only weight loss. A 94% relative risk reduction over more than three years is a larger effect than lifestyle intervention or metformin has achieved in this population.

**Practical implications.** Quote the estimand you mean. The frequently cited 22.9% weight reduction is the efficacy estimand for 15 mg — what happens in patients who stay on treatment; the treatment-regimen result at 176 weeks is 19.7%. Both are real and they answer different questions. And note why the trial included a 17-week off-treatment period: this is long-term therapy for a chronic disease, not a course, and the design exists to examine what happens on stopping.

[NEJM — tirzepatide for obesity treatment and diabetes prevention](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMoa2410819)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — figures confirmed, estimand and date clarified.** Verified: NEJMoa2410819, PMID 39536238, 2,539 participants with 1,032 prediabetic, 176 weeks plus a 17-week off-treatment period. The 94% risk reduction and the 98.7% diabetes-free figure are both accurate (1.3% versus 13.3% onset). TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission's 'sustained weight reductions up to 22.9%' is the efficacy estimand for the 15-mg dose; the mean percent change reported at 176 weeks was −19.7% at 15 mg (−12.3% at 5 mg, −18.7% at 10 mg) versus −1.3% on placebo. Citing only 22.9% presents the most favourable analysis without saying so, so both are given. (2) Date: published 13 November 2024, not 2025. Added the 17-week off-treatment period, since the submission frames this as arresting metabolic deterioration without noting that the benefit is tied to continued treatment.

### 3. Lepodisiran, a Long-Duration siRNA Targeting Lipoprotein(a) (ALPACA)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 2025 (ACC Scientific Sessions, simultaneous publication)*

**The breakthrough.** In the phase 2 ALPACA trial a single subcutaneous dose of lepodisiran — an siRNA silencing hepatic apolipoprotein(a) synthesis — reduced Lp(a) by an average of 93.9% across days 60 to 180 at the highest dose tested, 400 mg. The effect was durable rather than merely deep: roughly 90% reduction persisted at one year and about 75% at 18 months.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Demonstrates that a genetically driven, statin-unresponsive risk factor can be suppressed almost completely by a single injection with a dosing interval measured in months to a year — which is what would make treating Lp(a) practical at population scale.

**Practical implications.** This is phase 2: it establishes that Lp(a) can be lowered profoundly and durably, not that lowering it prevents events. The outcome trial is what will decide that, and it has not reported. The corollary that is actionable now is measurement — Lp(a) is largely genetically determined and stable through life, so a single measurement identifies inherited risk that a lipid panel misses entirely.

[ALPACA — lepodisiran reduced Lp(a) by nearly 94% at the highest tested dose](https://investor.lilly.com/news-releases/news-release-details/lillys-lepodisiran-reduced-levels-genetically-inherited-heart)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — durability figure was overstated.** Verified: phase 2 ALPACA, presented at ACC 2025 with simultaneous NEJM publication, single subcutaneous dose, 93.9% mean Lp(a) reduction over days 60-180 at 400 mg. ONE CORRECTION: the submission says levels were lowered 'by over 94% for up to a full year'. The 93.9% figure is the day 60-180 average; at one year the reduction is approximately 90%, and at 18 months approximately 75%. The submitted phrasing merges the peak effect with the durability window. Corrected, with the 18-month figure added because it is what supports the annual-dosing case the submission makes. Also stated explicitly that phase 2 shows biomarker lowering, not event reduction — the submission's 'eliminate residual genetic risk' presumes the outcome trial has already succeeded.

### 4. ACIP Lowers Universal Pneumococcal Vaccination to Age 50

*CDC / Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices · ACIP vote October 2024; MMWR 2025*

**The breakthrough.** ACIP voted 14 to 1 to lower the age-based pneumococcal conjugate vaccine recommendation from 65 to 50, and the CDC endorsed it. Adults aged 50 and over may receive PCV15, PCV20 or PCV21 (Capvaxive); if PCV15 is given, PPSV23 follows one year later, whereas PCV20 or PCV21 needs no additional PPSV23. The rationale is the burden of pneumococcal disease in adults aged 50 to 64, particularly those with high-risk conditions.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Replaces risk-factor assessment in middle age with a single age trigger, which is the kind of simplification that actually moves coverage rates.

**Practical implications.** Serotype coverage differs enough to matter when choosing: in adults 50 and over, PCV21 covers the serotypes responsible for about 84% of invasive pneumococcal disease against roughly 52% for PCV20. Remember PCV15 remains an option but commits the patient to a PPSV23 dose a year later, so a single-visit completion means PCV20 or PCV21.

[CIDRAP — CDC endorses ACIP recommendation to lower age for pneumococcal vaccination to 50](https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/adult-non-flu-vaccines/cdc-endorses-acip-recommendation-lower-age-pneumococcal-vaccination-50)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — one vaccine option omitted.** Verified: ACIP voted 14:1 to lower the age-based recommendation from 65 to 50 and the CDC endorsed it; the rationale is disease burden in the 50-64 group. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission lists only 'single-dose PCV20 or PCV21 (Capvaxive)'. PCV15 also remains an option for this age group, with PPSV23 one year later — omitting it would have a clinician turn away a patient offered PCV15, or miss the follow-up dose. (2) Date: the ACIP vote was October 2024, with MMWR publication following; the submission dates it to 2025. ADDED: the serotype coverage difference (about 84% of invasive disease for PCV21 versus about 52% for PCV20 in adults 50+), which is the practical basis for choosing between them and is absent from the submission.

### 5. USPSTF Recommendation: Screening for Breast Cancer Starting at Age 40

*JAMA · 11 June 2024 (announced 30 April 2024)*

**The breakthrough.** The US Preventive Services Task Force now recommends biennial screening mammography for women aged 40 to 74 — a Grade B recommendation — lowering the starting age from 50. The change is estimated to save 19% more lives than the previous recommendation. A major driver was equity: Black women have about a 40% higher risk of dying from breast cancer than White women, and are more often diagnosed young and with aggressive disease. Evidence remains insufficient to assess screening beyond age 74.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A Grade B rating obliges ACA-compliant insurers to cover the screen without cost-sharing, so this is a coverage change as much as a clinical one.

**Practical implications.** Order biennial mammography from 40. Two limits are worth knowing: the recommendation stops at 74 because the evidence does, not because benefit demonstrably ceases; and the Task Force also issued an insufficient-evidence statement on supplemental imaging for dense breasts, so a woman with dense tissue has no USPSTF-backed answer. Note that this aligned the starting age with specialty societies but did not settle the interval — several still favour annual screening over biennial.

[JAMA — USPSTF recommendation statement: screening for breast cancer](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2818283)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — figure and date, and the conflict is not fully resolved.** Verified: biennial mammography ages 40-74, Grade B, published in JAMA 11 June 2024 following the 30 April 2024 announcement, PMID 38687503. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) FIGURE: the estimate is 19% more lives saved, not the 20% submitted. (2) Date: 2024, not 2025. (3) The submission says the update 'resolv[es] longstanding policy conflicts between preventive task forces and specialty oncology societies'. It aligned the starting age, but the interval remains contested — several societies recommend annual rather than biennial screening — so the conflict is narrowed rather than resolved. ADDED: the 40% higher breast cancer mortality in Black women, which the Task Force cites as a reason for the change and which the submission omits entirely, and the insufficient-evidence statement on supplemental imaging for dense breasts.

### 6. Semaglutide and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Obesity without Diabetes (SELECT)

*New England Journal of Medicine · November 2023*

**The breakthrough.** 17,604 adults with BMI ≥27, established cardiovascular disease and no diabetes were randomised across 804 sites in 41 countries to weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo, with mean follow-up of 39.8 months. Major adverse cardiovascular events — cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction or non-fatal stroke — fell by 20%. Two further findings matter as much: incident diabetes fell by 73%, and renal outcomes improved by about 22%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes semaglutide as cardiovascular secondary prevention in people without diabetes, which decouples the drug from glycaemic indications altogether. The 73% reduction in incident diabetes in a non-diabetic cohort is arguably the more remarkable number.

**Practical implications.** Consider it alongside statins and antihypertensives in a patient with established cardiovascular disease and BMI ≥27, rather than as a weight-loss adjunct. The 73% diabetes reduction and the renal signal both belong in the discussion, since they broaden who benefits well beyond the cardiac endpoint.

[NEJM — semaglutide and cardiovascular outcomes in obesity without diabetes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37952131/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — one figure not confirmed, two major findings added.** Verified: SELECT, NEJMoa2307563, PMID 37952131, 17,604 participants (8,803 semaglutide / 8,801 placebo), 804 sites in 41 countries, mean follow-up 39.8 months, 20% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events in adults with BMI ≥27 and established cardiovascular disease without diabetes. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) NOT CONFIRMED: the submission claims 'heart failure outcomes by 18%'. I could not verify an 18% heart failure figure and it is not asserted; the confirmed secondary findings are given instead. (2) Date: November 2023, not 2025. (3) The submission states that ACC/AHA and ADA guidelines were updated to recommend GLP-1 receptor agonists in non-diabetic secondary prevention on the basis of this trial; I did not verify those guideline updates, so the entry describes the trial evidence rather than asserting guideline text. ADDED: the 73% reduction in incident diabetes and the roughly 22% improvement in renal outcomes, both absent from the submission and both larger effects than the primary endpoint.

### 7. Oral Muvalaplin for Lowering Lipoprotein(a) (KRAKEN)

*JAMA · 2024*

**The breakthrough.** Muvalaplin is the first oral small molecule designed to prevent Lp(a) assembly. In the phase 2 KRAKEN trial, placebo-adjusted reductions at the 12-week primary endpoint reached 85.8% measured with an intact Lp(a) particle assay, and 70.0% measured with a conventional apo(a) assay, with acceptable safety and tolerability.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Opens an oral route to a target previously addressable only by injection. If it holds through phase 3, a daily tablet changes the adherence and distribution picture for a lifelong risk factor entirely.

**Practical implications.** The two figures are the thing to understand: 85.8% and 70.0% describe the same result measured two ways, because conventional apo(a) assays detect fragments that the intact-particle assay does not. Quote the assay with the number, or the effect looks 15 percentage points larger than a standard laboratory would report. This remains phase 2 — no outcome data, and phase 3 has not completed.

[JAMA — oral muvalaplin for lowering of lipoprotein(a): a randomized clinical trial](https://www.hcplive.com/view/muvalaplin-an-oral-lpa-lowering-agent-shows-promise-in-phase-2-kraken-trial)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — the headline figure depends on which assay is used.** Verified: phase 2 KRAKEN trial published in JAMA, muvalaplin as the first oral small-molecule Lp(a) disruptor, 12-week primary endpoint. ONE SUBSTANTIVE CORRECTION: the submission cites a single figure, 'reduced circulating Lp(a) levels by up to 85%', without saying which assay produced it. The trial reported placebo-adjusted reductions of up to 85.8% by intact Lp(a) particle assay but up to 70.0% by conventional apo(a) assay — a 15-point difference driven by assay method, since apo(a) assays also detect fragments. Reporting only the higher number would overstate what a clinician's own laboratory would measure. Both are now given with the reason they differ. Date corrected to 2024, and the phase-2 status made explicit.

### 8. Opportunistic AI Quantification of Coronary Artery Calcium on Routine Chest CT

*Multicentre validation studies in imaging and digital health journals · 2023 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Deep-learning models can quantify coronary artery calcium and thoracic aortic calcification from non-gated chest CT acquired for unrelated reasons — most consequentially, lung cancer screening scans. The appeal is that the risk information is already latent in scans being performed by the million, at no additional radiation or scan cost.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Turns an existing imaging stream into a cardiovascular risk-stratification programme. The population undergoing lung cancer screening overlaps heavily with the population at elevated cardiovascular risk, so the same scan can address both.

**Practical implications.** The realistic use is triggering a statin conversation in someone whose calcium burden was previously unknown. Two caveats before relying on it: non-gated CT was not designed for calcium scoring and its scores are not interchangeable with dedicated gated CAC scoring, and an incidental finding is only useful if a defined pathway exists to act on it — an automated score in a report nobody routes to primary care changes nothing. I could not confirm a preventive cardiology guideline that formally endorses opportunistic AI screening, so treat that as a direction of travel rather than a standard.

[The Lancet Digital Health](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/landig/home)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — direction supported, specifics not confirmed.** The technical direction is real and well established in the imaging literature: deep learning can quantify coronary artery calcium from non-gated routine chest CT, and opportunistic screening from lung cancer screening scans is an active field. I did NOT verify the specific multicentre validation studies the submission attributes to The Lancet Digital Health and Radiology, and the submitted hyperlink is a journal home page rather than a paper — which is itself a signal that the citation was not pinned down. I also could not confirm the claim that this 'supported updated preventive cardiology consensus guidelines advocating for opportunistic screening'; the entry says so explicitly rather than asserting a guideline that may not exist. Two caveats added that the submission omits and that determine whether this is useful: non-gated scores are not interchangeable with dedicated gated CAC scoring, and an incidental score with no routing pathway changes no prescribing. Flagged for the physician to confirm the primary citations before this ships as authoritative.

### 9. CONSORT 2025: Updated Reporting Guideline for Randomised Trials

*The BMJ, JAMA and The Lancet (co-publication) · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The first major revision of the CONSORT statement since 2010, developed to bring trial reporting into line with how trials are now designed and run — including digitally delivered and algorithm-driven interventions, patient-reported outcomes, and reporting on who was actually enrolled.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reporting standards determine what a reader can assess. Where a trial's intervention is a piece of software that changes between versions, or where an eligible population was never recruited, the older checklist did not require the reader to be told.

**Practical implications.** For preventive practice, the value is downstream: appraising a digital health app, a wearable-derived endpoint or a lifestyle intervention is only possible when the report specifies what was delivered, to whom, and how outcomes were captured. When reading an older trial, treat the new items as a checklist of what probably was not reported.

[CONSORT 2025 statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40228833/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — the statement is real, its contents not confirmed item by item.** CONSORT 2025 exists and was co-published across major journals including The BMJ, JAMA and The Lancet, and the submission's framing of it as the first comprehensive update since CONSORT 2010 is consistent with that 15-year gap. I did NOT verify the specific new content the submission attributes to it — 'strict reporting criteria for AI-driven interventions, digital biomarkers, patient-reported outcomes, and health equity metrics', nor that it 'mandates transparent reporting on digital therapeutics, remote monitoring algorithms, and diverse demographic representation'. Those are plausible for a 2025 revision but are asserted here in general terms only. The submission's 'mandates' is also too strong for a reporting guideline, which journals adopt rather than enforce as regulation; reworded. Flagged for confirmation against the published checklist before any specific item is quoted.

### 10. Early Initiation of SGLT2 Inhibitors for Cardiorenal Protection in Chronic Kidney Disease

*Pooled trial analyses and KDIGO guidance · 2022 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The cardiorenal benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors in chronic kidney disease is established across trials and does not depend on the patient having diabetes — which reframes these drugs as kidney and cardiovascular protection rather than glucose-lowering agents, and moves the decision point earlier, to mildly reduced eGFR or albuminuria detected in primary care.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The practical bottleneck is not the prescription but the diagnosis. Albuminuria is the earliest marker of the disease these drugs modify, and it is invisible unless a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio is actually ordered.

**Practical implications.** Order annual uACR in patients with diabetes, hypertension or cardiovascular disease — a normal creatinine does not exclude the disease, and albuminuria typically precedes any fall in eGFR. Treat a raised uACR as an indication to consider an SGLT2 inhibitor rather than as a number to monitor. I could not verify the specific pooled Lancet analysis the submission cites, nor the claim that KDIGO and ADA guidelines 'mandate' this, so the entry rests on the established direction rather than on a named document.

[KDIGO clinical practice guidelines](https://kdigo.org/guidelines/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — direction well supported, cited sources not confirmed.** The clinical direction is well established: SGLT2 inhibitors reduce kidney disease progression and cardiovascular events in chronic kidney disease irrespective of diabetes status, and both KDIGO and ADA guidance support albuminuria screening and early initiation. But I did NOT verify the specific 'pooled clinical trial analyses published in The Lancet' the submission cites, and the submitted hyperlink is The Lancet's home page rather than a paper — the same signal as the AI calcium entry. 'Mandate' is also too strong for guideline recommendations and has been reworded. The entry is written around what is genuinely actionable and verifiable in primary care — annual uACR, treating albuminuria as an indication rather than an observation — rather than around a named analysis I could not pin down. Flagged for the physician to supply or confirm the primary citation.
