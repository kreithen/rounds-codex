### 1. AAP/NASPGHAN Clinical Practice Guideline: Diagnosis and Management of Faltering Weight

*Pediatrics · 2026;157(4):e2025075764*

**The breakthrough.** The first joint AAP and NASPGHAN guideline on the problem formerly called failure to thrive, developed by a multidisciplinary panel working from 2021 to 2024 using GRADE. It replaces the label "failure to thrive" with "faltering weight" and moves diagnosis from percentiles to z-scores. A child meets criteria with any one of: weight-for-length or BMI-for-age below −1.65 z (about the 5th percentile); in a child under 2, weight-gain velocity below −2 z for age (2.3rd percentile); or a decline of 1 z-score or more in weight, weight-for-length or BMI.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The decisive change is the one-z-score decline. Diagnosis no longer waits for a child to reach the 3rd or 5th percentile or to cross two major percentile lines — a single z-score drop is enough, which moves recognition considerably earlier.

**Practical implications.** Plot z-scores, not percentiles, and act on the trajectory rather than the position. The guideline supports de-escalating reflexive laboratory and imaging workups in favour of structured nutrition assessment, catch-up caloric targets and multidisciplinary feeding evaluation. Note that the terminology change is not cosmetic: "failure to thrive" attributes failure to the child or the parent, and the new label describes an observation.

[Pediatrics — AAP/NASPGHAN clinical practice guideline for diagnosis and management of faltering weight](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/4/e2025075764/206901/Clinical-Practice-Guideline-for-Diagnosis-and)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the diagnostic thresholds given were from a different classification system.** The guideline is real and correctly identified: first joint AAP/NASPGHAN CPG, GRADE, panel 2021-2024, Pediatrics 2026;157(4):e2025075764, and it does replace 'failure to thrive' with 'faltering weight' and move from percentiles to z-scores. THE THRESHOLDS ARE WRONG. The submission gives severity bands of mild −1 to −1.9, moderate −2 to −2.9, severe ≤−3. Those are the ASPEN/Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics paediatric MALNUTRITION severity indicators, not this guideline's diagnostic criteria. The guideline's criteria are: weight-for-length or BMI-for-age < −1.65 z; or, under age 2, weight-gain velocity < −2 z; or a decline of ≥1 z-score. A resident applying the submitted bands would use the wrong cut-off in both directions — waiting for −2 before diagnosing, when a 1 z-score decline already qualifies. Corrected, with the one-z-score point moved into the impact field because it is the substantive change and the submission missed it. Date corrected: 2026, not 2025.

### 2. International Consensus Criteria for Pediatric Sepsis and Septic Shock (Phoenix)

*JAMA · February 2024*

**The breakthrough.** The SCCM Pediatric Sepsis Definition Task Force retired the 2005 SIRS-based criteria and derived the Phoenix Sepsis Score from a large international database, systematic review and modified Delphi process. Sepsis in a child under 18 with suspected infection is now a Phoenix score of at least 2 points, indicating potentially life-threatening dysfunction of the respiratory, cardiovascular, coagulation or neurological systems. Septic shock is sepsis with at least 1 cardiovascular point — severe hypotension for age, blood lactate above 5 mmol/L, or a need for vasoactive medication.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Anchors the definition to measured organ dysfunction rather than to vital-sign derangements that most febrile children have. The mortality gradient validates it: score ≥2 carried in-hospital mortality of 7.1% in higher-resource and 28.5% in lower-resource settings, more than eight times that of children with suspected infection not meeting criteria; septic shock 10.8% and 33.5% respectively.

**Practical implications.** Learn the four organ systems and the three cardiovascular triggers, because the cardiovascular point is what separates sepsis from septic shock and therefore what drives escalation. The resource-setting difference in mortality is not a footnote — the same score means a very different prognosis depending on where the child is, which matters when reading literature from another health system.

[JAMA — international consensus criteria for pediatric sepsis and septic shock](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2814296)

> **Citation check — matched.** Verified and accurate as submitted, including the Phoenix score ≥2 threshold, the four organ systems, and all three cardiovascular criteria for septic shock (severe hypotension for age, lactate >5 mmol/L, vasoactive requirement). Two additions rather than corrections: the date is February 2024, not 2025, and the in-hospital mortality figures have been added because they are what justify the cut-off and the submission omits them. One softening: the submission says the criteria 'significantly reduce false-positive sepsis alarms in pediatric emergency departments and ICUs'. That is the design intent and the direction of separate prospective comparisons against the 2005 criteria, but it is not a finding of the consensus paper itself, so it is not stated as one.

### 3. Omalizumab for Multiple Food Allergies (OUtMATCH Stage 1)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 2024*

**The breakthrough.** In stage 1 of this NIH-sponsored randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 180 participants aged 1 to 55 — 177 of them children and adolescents — allergic to peanut and at least two other foods received omalizumab or placebo every two or four weeks for 16 to 20 weeks. 67% met the primary endpoint versus 7% on placebo, meaning they could tolerate a substantially larger quantity of peanut before a moderate-to-severe reaction. Secondary endpoints for cashew, milk and egg improved similarly, across milk, egg, wheat, cashew, hazelnut and walnut. Safety was comparable apart from more injection-site reactions. The FDA approved omalizumab on 16 February 2024 for reducing allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, from accidental exposure in patients aged 1 year and over.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first pharmacological option that raises the reaction threshold across several foods at once, in children as young as one, without food-specific desensitisation.

**Practical implications.** Counsel this precisely, because the indication is narrow and easy to overstate. Omalizumab raises the threshold for an accidental exposure; it does not permit deliberate ingestion of the allergen, and strict avoidance and adrenaline autoinjectors continue unchanged. The effect depends on continued dosing — this is ongoing treatment, not a cure or a course, and stopping it removes the protection.

[NEJM — omalizumab for the treatment of multiple food allergies](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2312382)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — 'disease-modifying' is wrong, and the avoidance advice was unsafe.** The trial is real and the results check out: OUtMATCH stage 1, NEJMoa2312382, 180 participants aged 1-55 (177 children/adolescents), 67% versus 7% on the primary endpoint, FDA approval 16 February 2024. FOUR CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission calls omalizumab 'a disease-modifying monotherapy'. It is not disease-modifying — the FDA indication is reduction of reactions on accidental exposure, the benefit is threshold-raising, and it depends on continued dosing. (2) SAFETY: the submission says it eases 'reliance on strict food avoidance'. It does not. Patients must continue strict avoidance and carry adrenaline; a family told otherwise could feed a child an allergen. That is the most consequential error in this list and the entry now states the opposite explicitly. (3) 'Led to updated AAP and AAAAI allergy guidelines' could not be confirmed; the FDA approval is the verifiable regulatory event and is cited instead. (4) Trial name is OUtMATCH, not OUToMATCH; date 2024, not 2025.

### 4. Nirsevimab Effectiveness Against RSV ICU Admission and Acute Respiratory Failure

*MMWR / CDC-supported multicentre investigation · December 2024 to April 2025, reported November 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A prospective 27-hospital case-control investigation across 24 states estimated nirsevimab effectiveness at 80% against RSV-associated infant ICU admission and 83% against acute respiratory failure — defined as illness requiring CPAP, bilevel positive airway pressure or invasive mechanical ventilation — with doses received a median of 52 and 50 days before symptom onset respectively.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Confirms the benefit at the severe end of the disease spectrum, which is where a passive-immunisation programme has to prove itself. Most infants in the cohort were previously healthy, so the effect is not confined to the high-risk groups palivizumab was reserved for.

**Practical implications.** Protection wanes measurably within a single season: 86% effectiveness in the first 7 to 59 days after administration falling to 66% from 60 to 183 days. That is the number that matters for timing — an infant dosed early in the autumn is less protected in a late-peaking season. Supports the CDC/ACIP and AAP recommendation for infants under 8 months entering their first RSV season.

[Nirsevimab effectiveness against ICU admission for RSV in infants, 24 states, December 2024-April 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41348586/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — supported, with waning added.** Verified and accurate as submitted: 80% against RSV-associated ICU admission and 83% against acute respiratory failure, from a prospective 27-hospital case-control investigation across 24 states, December 2024 to April 2025. IMPORTANT ADDITION: effectiveness fell from 86% at 7-59 days to 66% at 60-183 days after administration. The submission presents a single flat figure, which would have a clinician assume uniform season-long protection; the waning is what determines dosing timing and belongs in the entry. Also added that most infants were previously healthy, since that is what distinguishes this from the palivizumab era, and the operational definition of acute respiratory failure so the 83% is interpretable.

### 5. Whole-Body Hypothermia in Preterm Infants 33 to 35 Weeks with Neonatal Encephalopathy

*JAMA Pediatrics · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The NICHD Neonatal Research Network randomised infants of 33 to 35 weeks' gestation with moderate or severe hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, at under 6 hours of age, to hypothermia at 33.5 °C for 72 hours or normothermia at 37 °C, across 19 centres between July 2015 and December 2022. Death or disability at 18 to 22 months occurred in 35% (29/83) with hypothermia versus 29% (20/69) with normothermia (adjusted relative risk 1.11, 95% credibility interval 0.74 to 2.00). Death occurred in 20% (18/88) versus 12% (9/78), adjusted relative risk 1.38 (0.79 to 2.85). Bayesian analysis with a neutral prior gave a 74% probability of increased death or disability and an 87% probability of increased death with cooling.

**Clinical guideline impact.** This is not merely a null result. The point estimates and the Bayesian probabilities both point towards harm, which is a stronger basis for changing practice than an absence of benefit would be.

**Practical implications.** Restrict therapeutic hypothermia to infants of 36 weeks' gestation or more, and do not cool a late-preterm infant off-label outside a trial. When explaining the decision to a family, the honest statement is not that cooling was unhelpful but that in the only randomised trial in this gestational range there was an 87% probability that it increased death.

[JAMA Pediatrics — whole-body hypothermia for neonatal encephalopathy in preterm infants 33 to 35 weeks' gestation](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2830701)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — the result is stronger than submitted.** Verified: NICHD Neonatal Research Network randomised trial, 19 centres, July 2015 to December 2022, infants 33-35 weeks with moderate or severe HIE cooled at under 6 hours, no reduction in death or disability at 18-22 months. The submission is correct as far as it goes but UNDERSTATES the finding. It says cooling 'did not reduce the primary outcome' and mentions 'potential adverse event risks'. The actual data point towards harm: 35% versus 29% for death or disability, 20% versus 12% for death, and a Bayesian analysis giving 74% probability of increased death or disability and 87% probability of increased death. Those figures have been added, because 'no benefit' and 'probably harmful' support different conversations with a family and different institutional policy. The submission's practical conclusion — restrict cooling to ≥36 weeks and avoid off-label use — is right and is kept.

### 6. Characterizing Long COVID in Children and Adolescents (RECOVER-Pediatrics)

*JAMA · 21 August 2024, with a separate young-children analysis since*

**The breakthrough.** The NIH RECOVER-Pediatrics observational cohort, over 5,300 participants at more than 60 institutions, identified four distinct symptom clusters in school-age children and three in adolescents. The most predictive symptoms differed by age: in school-age children, trouble with memory or focusing, back or neck pain, abdominal pain, headache, specific fears, school refusal, itchy skin or rash, disturbed sleep, nausea or vomiting and light-headedness; in adolescents, change or loss of smell or taste, body, muscle or joint pain, daytime tiredness or low energy, tiredness after walking, back or neck pain, memory or concentration difficulty, headache and light-headedness. These patterns tracked with worse overall health and quality of life. A separate RECOVER analysis has since described distinct symptom patterns in infants and toddlers.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes that paediatric long COVID is not one syndrome and not the adult syndrome scaled down. School refusal and specific fears sitting among the most predictive symptoms in school-age children is the finding a clinician is least likely to anticipate.

**Practical implications.** Screen by developmental stage rather than against a single symptom list, and take somatic and behavioural presentations in younger children seriously rather than routing them straight to a psychological explanation. Note that the school-age and adolescent clusters come from the JAMA cohort paper, while the infant and toddler patterns are a separate later analysis — they are not all one publication.

[JAMA — characterizing long COVID in children and adolescents](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2822770)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — two studies were merged into one, and the cluster findings were missing.** Real: RECOVER-Pediatrics, JAMA, published 21 August 2024, over 5,300 participants at more than 60 institutions. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission describes one study identifying clusters 'across developmental stages, highlighting distinct presentations in infants/toddlers versus older children and teens'. The JAMA cohort paper covers school-age children and adolescents; the infant and toddler patterns are a SEPARATE later RECOVER analysis. Merging them attributes findings to a paper that does not contain them. (2) The actual structure — four clusters in school-age children, three in adolescents — was absent from the submission, along with the symptom lists, which are the usable content. Both added. Date corrected to 2024. The submission's example symptoms ('post-exertional malaise and dysautonomia in teens') are plausible but were not among the most predictive symptoms reported, so the published lists are used instead.

### 7. Fluoride Exposure and Children's IQ Scores: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

*JAMA Pediatrics · January 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A meta-analysis of 74 cross-sectional and prospective cohort studies published between 1989 and 2023 found an inverse association between fluoride exposure and children's IQ. In the 13 studies with individual-level exposure measures, IQ was 1.63 points lower per 1 mg/L increase in urinary fluoride. The association persisted when restricted to the 22 high-quality studies and was consistent across subgroups by sex, age, country, outcome measure, exposure timing and exposure matrix, including at exposures below 1.5 mg/L.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Moved a contested literature into a mainstream paediatric journal with a quantified effect estimate, and prompted an unusually public methodological argument — a JAMA Pediatrics editorial urging caution, published critiques, and a formal response from the authors.

**Practical implications.** The context that makes this interpretable is missing from most summaries: the 74 studies were conducted in 10 countries and none in the United States, and 52 of them were rated high risk of bias. Most exposures studied are well above the 0.7 mg/L used in US community water fluoridation. The defensible clinical response is to total up a child's systemic fluoride — water source, supplements, swallowed toothpaste — before adding a systemic supplement, while continuing topical fluoride, which is where the caries benefit lies and which this analysis does not address.

[JAMA Pediatrics — fluoride exposure and children's IQ scores: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2828425)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — supported, with the contested context restored.** Verified: JAMA Pediatrics January 2025, 74 studies from 1989-2023, inverse association, effect estimate of −1.63 IQ points per 1 mg/L urinary fluoride in the 13 individual-level studies, association persisting below 1.5 mg/L in high-quality evidence. THREE ADDITIONS, all of which change how a pediatrician should use this. 52 of the 74 studies were rated high risk of bias and only 22 high quality. The studies were done in 10 countries and NONE in the United States, at exposures generally well above the 0.7 mg/L of US community fluoridation. And the paper drew a substantial published critique, including a JAMA Pediatrics editorial counselling caution — a clinician quoting the headline as settled will be met with it. ONE CORRECTION: the submission says the study 'prompted public health and pediatric dental organizations to re-examine total daily fluoride thresholds'. I could not confirm an organisational threshold review, so it is not asserted; the practical advice about totalling systemic exposure while continuing topical fluoride is sound and is kept.

### 8. Residual Exacerbations on Mepolizumab in Urban Children with Asthma (MUPPITS-2)

*The Lancet (parent trial) and Nature Communications (mechanism) · Trial 2022; transcriptomic analysis 2025*

**The breakthrough.** MUPPITS-2 randomised urban children with exacerbation-prone eosinophilic asthma to mepolizumab added to guidelines-based care, and found a 27% relative reduction in severe exacerbations over 12 months, concentrated in the autumn. Nasal airway transcriptomics then showed what mepolizumab does and does not switch off: correlation among eosinophil-specific genes was lost, but correlation among mast-cell-specific genes, type 2 cytokines and airway epithelial inflammatory genes was conserved and in places strengthened. A separate analysis found activated sputum eosinophils still associated with exacerbations in treated children.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Explains why a biologic that works does not work completely. Depleting eosinophils does not dismantle the wider type 2 network or the epithelial inflammatory programme, and exacerbation risk persists through both.

**Practical implications.** Do not withdraw controller therapy because a child is on a biologic: continue inhaled corticosteroid and reliever therapy, and treat autumn viral season as the high-risk window it demonstrably is. The honest framing for a family is a roughly one-quarter reduction in severe exacerbations, not their elimination. Be careful with the shorthand that breakthrough attacks are 'non-type 2' — mast-cell and type 2 cytokine networks were preserved on treatment, and activated eosinophils were still implicated, so residual type 2 activity is part of the picture rather than absent from it.

[Nature Communications — mepolizumab alters gene regulatory networks of nasal airway type-2 and epithelial inflammation in urban children with asthma](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-63629-2)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the mechanism is not the clean non-type-2 story submitted.** Real work, correctly identified: MUPPITS-2 in The Lancet (2022, 27% relative reduction in severe exacerbations, autumn-predominant) with the nasal transcriptomic analysis in Nature Communications (2025, PMID 40897727). CORRECTION TO MECHANISM. The submission says breakthrough exacerbations 'were driven by non-Type 2 inflammatory pathways (specifically epithelial hypersecretion and viral macrophage responses)'. The published finding is subtler and partly the opposite: mepolizumab dissolved eosinophil gene correlation but CONSERVED and sometimes strengthened correlation among mast-cell genes, type 2 cytokines and epithelial inflammatory genes, and a separate JACI analysis found activated sputum eosinophils still associated with exacerbations. So residual type 2 activity persists rather than being replaced by a purely non-T2 process. The specific pathways named in the submission were not confirmed and are not asserted. The submission's clinical conclusion — keep controller therapy, do not rely on the biologic alone — is right and is kept, with the 27% figure added so the magnitude is visible.

### 9. Exagamglogene Autotemcel: Long-Term Outcomes in Sickle Cell Disease and Beta-Thalassemia

*NEJM (pivotal trials) and Blood / ASH (long-term follow-up) · 2023 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Final results of CLIMB SCD-121: 46 patients with severe sickle cell disease and a mean 4.2 vaso-occlusive crises per year at baseline received exa-cel, an ex vivo CRISPR-Cas9 edited autologous therapy that reactivates fetal haemoglobin. At a median follow-up of 41.3 months, 45 of 45 evaluable patients were free of vaso-occlusive crises and of hospitalisation for them, with a mean crisis-free duration of 35.3 months. Neutrophil engraftment occurred at a median of 27 days and platelet engraftment at 35 days. Durable benefit now extends beyond 5 years in both transfusion-dependent thalassaemia and sickle cell disease.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes gene editing as a durable option that does not require a donor, removing both the matched-donor bottleneck and the risk of graft-versus-host disease.

**Practical implications.** Read the population before calling this a paediatric result: this cohort was 12 adolescents and 34 adults with a mean age of 21.4 years, and the original approval was for patients aged 12 and over. Transfusion independence is the endpoint in the thalassaemia trial and crisis-freedom in the sickle cell trial — two different trials, not one composite result. The therapy requires myeloablative busulfan conditioning, which brings its own toxicity, infertility risk and need for a specialist centre.

[NEJM — exagamglogene autotemcel for severe sickle cell disease](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2309676)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — presented as a paediatric result from a mostly adult cohort.** The efficacy data are real and if anything stronger than submitted: CLIMB SCD-121 final results show 45 of 45 evaluable patients crisis-free and hospitalisation-free at a median 41.3 months' follow-up, mean crisis-free duration 35.3 months, with durable benefit beyond 5 years across both indications. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) POPULATION: the submission describes outcomes 'in pediatric patients'. The cohort was 12 adolescents and 34 adults, mean age 21.4 years, and the label at the time covered ages 12 and over — this is not a young-children result, which is the separate 2026 trial. (2) The submission merges vaso-occlusive crisis freedom and transfusion independence into one finding; they are endpoints of two different trials in two different diseases. (3) 'Restructured pediatric hematology guidelines to position autologous gene therapy as a curative therapeutic pathway' overstates what was confirmed; the durable-benefit data and the removal of the donor requirement are stated instead, with busulfan conditioning, infertility risk and centre requirements named because the submission mentions conditioning only in passing.

### 10. Closing the Evidence Gap for Drugs in Children: Strengthening the Pediatric Research Equity Act

*New England Journal of Medicine (Perspective) · April 2026*

**The breakthrough.** An analysis of how PREA is actually working found that only 28% of the pediatric trials required for drugs approved between 2015 and 2021 had been completed by May 2024. As of 2023 the FDA had granted more than 700 extensions to pediatric study deadlines and classified 250 studies as delayed. Rare-disease designations exempt close to half of new drugs from pediatric testing altogether. Proposed reforms, including the Innovation in Pediatric Drugs Act, would remove the rare-disease exemption.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Quantifies why so much paediatric prescribing remains off-label two decades after the legislation that was supposed to fix it. The exemption, not the requirement, is doing most of the work.

**Practical implications.** The clinical consequence is the one paediatricians already live with: dosing by extrapolation for years after a drug reaches adults. Note that the reforms described are legislative proposals, not enacted requirements — nothing currently obliges a sponsor to run phase 3 paediatric trials before adult approval, and citing that as existing policy would be wrong.

[NEJM — closing the evidence gap for drugs in children: measures to strengthen the Pediatric Research Equity Act](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMp2518278)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — proposals described as accomplished policy, and the figures were missing.** Real: NEJM Perspective, NEJMp2518278, PMID 42012006, published April 2026 (not 2025 — the date is corrected in the field but the entry is left on the submitted year, as with other out-of-year citations in this project). TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission says the piece 'Accelerated FDA and international regulatory initiatives REQUIRING sponsors to initiate Phase 3 pediatric trials prior to adult product approval and remove orphan-drug exemptions'. Those are proposals — the Innovation in Pediatric Drugs Act among them — not enacted requirements, and a resident citing them as current regulation would be wrong. Reworded. (2) The substance was absent: 28% of required trials completed, over 700 deadline extensions, 250 studies delayed, and rare-disease designation exempting nearly half of new drugs. Those figures are the argument and have been added.

### 1. Zorevunersen in Children and Adolescents with Dravet Syndrome

*New England Journal of Medicine · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Zorevunersen is an antisense oligonucleotide that up-regulates NaV1.1 sodium channels, addressing the SCN1A haploinsufficiency that causes most Dravet syndrome. Across two phase 1-2a open-label multicentre studies (MONARCH and ADMIRAL) and their extensions, 81 patients aged 2 to 18 on standard antiseizure medication were enrolled from 2020 to 2023, with 75 continuing in extension for up to three years. Patients given 70 mg intrathecally in the phase 1-2a studies followed by up to 45 mg in extension had a median reduction in convulsive-seizure frequency ranging from 58.8% to 90.9% across successive one-month intervals over the first 20 months of extension, with improvements in quality of life and functioning reported by both clinicians and caregivers.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first antisense approach aimed at the genetic cause of a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy rather than at the seizures it produces, and the cognitive and behavioural signal is what makes the disease-modification hypothesis worth testing.

**Practical implications.** Understand the evidence stage before offering this: these are open-label phase 1-2a studies with no control arm, and the authors describe zorevunersen as a *potential* disease-modifying treatment pending further development. Quote the range, not the ceiling — a median reduction between 59% and 91% depending on the interval is the honest figure. Delivery is intrathecal and repeated, so a care pathway has to accommodate serial lumbar punctures in a child with a severe epilepsy.

[NEJM — zorevunersen in children and adolescents with Dravet syndrome](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41780062/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — trial phase, and the seizure figure was the best interval rather than the result.** Real and correctly identified: NEJMoa2506295, PMID 41780062, zorevunersen targeting SCN1A haploinsufficiency to up-regulate NaV1.1. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) PHASE: the submission implies a definitive trial. These are two phase 1-2a OPEN-LABEL studies (MONARCH and ADMIRAL) plus extensions, 81 patients enrolled, 75 continuing — no control group, so the effect size cannot be separated from natural history and concomitant therapy. (2) The submission quotes 'up to a 91% reduction in monthly seizure frequency'. The reported result is a median change ranging from −58.82% to −90.91% across one-month intervals; citing only the ceiling is the most favourable possible reading. Range given instead. (3) 'Disease-modifying' is softened to potential, which is the paper's own wording. The submission's practical point about serial lumbar punctures is right and kept; monitoring of CSF protein specifically could not be confirmed and is not asserted.

### 2. Routine Gastric Residual Volume Measurement in Critically Ill Children (GASTRIC-PICU)

*JAMA · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre, open-label, non-inferiority randomised trial across 23 PICUs in the United Kingdom, Scotland, Northern Ireland and Switzerland enrolled 4,700 children aged 0 to 16 starting enteral feeds while requiring invasive ventilation, between June 2023 and December 2025. Omitting routine gastric residual volume assessment was non-inferior to checking at least every 6 hours for survival and days alive without mechanical ventilation at 30 days (median 25 days, IQR 21 to 27, in both groups). The proportion of energy requirements met by 72 hours was 80.3% without routine checks versus 76.8% with them.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A 4,700-child trial is decisive for a practice that was never based on evidence in the first place. Aspirating and measuring gastric contents every few hours was inherited ritual, and the trial shows nothing is lost by stopping.

**Practical implications.** Stop routine six-hourly residual checks in the ventilated child who is feeding, and monitor clinically instead — abdominal distension, vomiting, discomfort. Keep expectations calibrated: the nutritional gain was real but modest, about 3.5 percentage points of energy target by 72 hours, and the primary result is non-inferiority on survival and ventilator-free days rather than a demonstrated improvement in outcome.

[JAMA — gastric residual volume assessment in critically ill children: the GASTRIC-PICU randomized clinical trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/2850408)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — supported, design and effect size clarified.** Verified: JAMA, 23 PICUs across the UK, Scotland, Northern Ireland and Switzerland, 4,700 children aged 0-16, June 2023 to December 2025, non-inferiority open-label design, no routine GRV non-inferior for survival and ventilator-free days at 30 days (median 25 days both groups), energy targets 80.3% versus 76.8% at 72 hours. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission says omission 'resulted in earlier delivery of target enteral nutrition with no increase in ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) or aspiration events'. The measured nutritional difference is 3.5 percentage points of energy target by 72 hours — real but not the substantial acceleration implied — and VAP and aspiration as specifically reported outcomes could not be confirmed, so they are not asserted; the confirmed primary outcome is stated instead. (2) The non-inferiority design was missing and matters: this shows nothing is lost, not that outcomes improve. Trial size added, since 4,700 children is the reason this settles the question.

### 3. Fremanezumab for Preventive Treatment of Episodic Migraine in Children and Adolescents (SPACE)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 14 January 2026*

**The breakthrough.** SPACE is the first phase 3 trial of an anti-CGRP monoclonal antibody in children. 237 patients aged 6 to 17 with episodic migraine were randomised to monthly fremanezumab 120 mg, 220 mg or placebo. Both doses produced greater reductions in monthly migraine days and higher response rates than placebo over 3 months, with fewer days of moderate-to-severe headache and reduced acute migraine medication use. Safety was consistent with adult experience and no new signals emerged. The FDA had already expanded the indication to paediatric episodic migraine on monthly dosing, based on earlier SPACE results.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Fills a real gap. Paediatric migraine prophylaxis has rested on drugs borrowed from adults with poor tolerability, and the CHAMP trial had previously shown topiramate and amitriptyline no better than placebo in this age group.

**Practical implications.** A monthly subcutaneous injection avoids the daily adherence problem and the cognitive and weight effects that make topiramate hard to sustain in a school-age child. Get the drug class right: fremanezumab is an anti-CGRP monoclonal ANTIBODY, not a CGRP receptor antagonist — the gepants are the antagonists, they are oral, and they have not been established in this population.

[NEJM / SPACE — fremanezumab for prevention of episodic migraine in children and adolescents](https://www.neurologylive.com/view/published-space-study-reinforces-efficacy-fremanezumab-pediatric-episodic-migraine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — drug class mislabelled, details added.** Verified: SPACE, randomised double-blind placebo-controlled phase 3, 237 patients aged 6-17, fremanezumab 120 mg and 220 mg monthly, published in NEJM online 14 January 2026, first phase 3 evidence for an anti-CGRP monoclonal antibody in children, safety consistent with adults. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) TERMINOLOGY: the submission's guideline-impact line says the trial 'Established CGRP antagonists as a recommended, evidence-based preventive class'. Fremanezumab is a monoclonal antibody against CGRP, not a CGRP receptor antagonist — the gepants are the antagonists and were not studied here. Conflating them would have a clinician reach for the wrong drug. ### 5. Topical Berdazimer Gel 10.3% for Molluscum Contagiosum (B-SIMPLE4)

### 6. The 2026 AAP Immunization Schedule Diverges from the CDC Schedule

*Pediatrics (policy statement) · 26 January 2026; Pediatrics 157(3):e2025075754*

**The breakthrough.** The AAP's 2026 schedule, released in Red Book Online, continues to recommend routine vaccination against 18 preventable diseases. The CDC schedule now covers 11. **Clinical guideline impact.** There is no longer one national childhood immunization schedule in the United States. Which document a clinician follows is now a choice with consequences for practice, for insurance coverage and for what a family is told.

**Practical implications.** Know which schedule your institution, your state and your payers follow, because they may not agree. The hepatitis B birth dose is the sharpest divergence: the AAP position is universal administration within 24 hours to prevent perinatal transmission, while ACIP has made it a shared decision — and a missed birth dose is not recoverable in the infant who was exposed. ### 9. Antibiotic Stewardship in Children and the WHO AWaRe Framework

*eClinicalMedicine and international surveillance studies · 2024 to 2026*
