### 1. Self-Collected Vaginal Swabs for High-Risk HPV Cervical Cancer Screening

*JAMA Network Open / The Lancet · 2024-2025*

**The breakthrough.** Self-collected vaginal swab sampling achieves sensitivity for high-risk HPV and CIN2+ comparable to clinician-collected sampling. The FDA approved self-collection devices in 2024, and at-home collection kits followed.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Cervical cancer screening has never been limited mainly by the test interval — it is limited by the speculum examination and by who never comes in for one. The people at highest risk of cervical cancer are the underscreened, and self-collection reaches them.

**Practical implications.** Note the distinction the approvals turn on: the first FDA authorisations were for self-collection IN A HEALTHCARE SETTING — a private room rather than the examination table — with at-home kits authorised subsequently. Offer it to anyone who has declined or deferred speculum screening. A positive hrHPV result still requires clinician follow-up with cytology or colposcopy, so self-collection changes the sampling step, not the pathway after it.

[Self-collected HPV testing for cervical cancer screening](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=self-collected+vaginal+swab+HPV+testing+cervical+cancer+screening+sensitivity+CIN2)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — setting, and consolidated from two submitted entries.** The evidence and the FDA approvals are real, but the specific studies were not resolved to citations and the url is a PubMed title search; the submitted '≥95% sensitivity' figure is therefore not asserted. ONE MATERIAL CORRECTION: the original said 'home- or clinic-based' as though both arrived together — the initial FDA authorisations were for self-collection in a healthcare setting, with at-home kits following, which is what determines what a clinic can actually offer today. This topic appeared in BOTH submitted OB/GYN lists (2025 #1 and 2026 #4); it is kept here once so it does not appear under both year buttons.

### 2. ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline: Intrapartum Fetal Heart Rate Monitoring

*Obstetrics & Gynecology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** An updated framework for interpreting Category I, II and III intrapartum fetal heart rate tracings, with criteria for intrauterine resuscitation and for expedited delivery.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Recommends against relying on the adjunct technologies that were once expected to resolve tracing ambiguity — computerised FHR algorithms, fetal pulse oximetry, ST-segment analysis. None improved neonatal outcome in trials, and management rests on physiological interpretation instead.

**Practical implications.** Category II is where the work is, because it covers the large indeterminate middle ground and is the commonest tracing in labour. The resuscitation steps — position change, intravenous fluid, reducing or stopping oxytocin, correcting hypotension, considering amnioinfusion for cord compression — are what a Category II tracing calls for; Category III calls for expedited delivery. Note that routine maternal oxygen for a non-reassuring tracing has not been shown to improve outcomes.

[Obstetrics & Gynecology - ACOG guideline on intrapartum fetal heart rate monitoring](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=ACOG+clinical+practice+guideline+intrapartum+fetal+heart+rate+monitoring+interpretation+management)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 10 on intrapartum FHR monitoring was located and the url is a PubMed title search, so the guideline number and date are unconfirmed. The clinical content — the three-tier system, the case against FHR adjunct technologies, and the intrauterine resuscitation steps — is well established and has been stated explicitly rather than left as 'clear intrapartum intrauterine resuscitation steps', which taught nothing usable. Added the routine-oxygen point, which is a common reflex not supported by evidence.

### 3. ERAS Society Recommendations for Antenatal, Preoperative and Intraoperative Care in Cesarean Delivery

*American Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology · Series published 2018-2019, with subsequent updates*

**The breakthrough.** Perioperative pathways for planned and intrapartum cesarean delivery: preoperative carbohydrate loading and shortened fasting, multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia, active maintenance of normothermia, early feeding and early mobilisation.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Cesarean delivery is among the most common major operations performed anywhere, and it was one of the last to receive a structured recovery pathway. The ERAS Society published its cesarean recommendations as a three-part series covering antenatal/preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative care.

**Practical implications.** The opioid-sparing element has the largest downstream effect: scheduled paracetamol and NSAIDs with neuraxial morphine, reserving opioids for breakthrough pain, reduces both inpatient consumption and the discharge prescription — which is where persistent opioid use after cesarean begins. Note that ERAS pathways are bundles, and adherence to the whole bundle rather than to favourite components is what the outcome data reflect.

[American Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology - ERAS Society recommendations for cesarean delivery](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=ERAS+Society+guidelines+cesarean+delivery+antenatal+preoperative+intraoperative+care)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — date.** The ERAS Society cesarean recommendations are real and were published in AJOG as a three-part series in 2018-2019, not as a 2025 guideline; whether a further update appeared could not be confirmed, and the url is a PubMed title search. 'REQUIRES obstetric units to implement structured ERAS pathways' softened — a society recommendation does not require anything of a unit. Added the bundle-adherence point, since selective adoption is the usual reason ERAS pathways underperform in practice.

### 4. ACOG Clinical Consensus: Pain Management for In-Office Uterine and Cervical Procedures

*Obstetrics & Gynecology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Standardised analgesia for procedures long performed without it — IUD insertion, endometrial biopsy, hysteroscopy, cervical biopsy — after sustained patient advocacy about how badly the pain of these procedures has been underestimated.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Clinicians should counsel about expected pain and offer evidence-based options rather than assuming a brief procedure needs no analgesia. The gap between clinician-estimated and patient-reported pain for IUD insertion is one of the widest documented in any procedural setting.

**Practical implications.** Paracervical or intracervical lidocaine block has the best evidence for tenaculum placement and cervical dilation; topical lidocaine gel alone performs poorly for the deeper visceral pain of intrauterine instrumentation. Misoprostol before IUD insertion does NOT reduce pain and increases cramping — a common practice with evidence against it. Oral NSAIDs given in advance help with post-procedural cramping more than with the procedure itself.

[Obstetrics & Gynecology - ACOG clinical consensus on pain management for in-office procedures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=ACOG+clinical+consensus+pain+management+in-office+uterine+cervical+procedures)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, with two corrections.** ACOG has issued guidance in this area and the direction is real, but the specific 'Clinical Consensus No. 11' was not located; the url is a PubMed title search. TWO CORRECTIONS. 'Formally MANDATES that clinicians proactively offer' overstates — guidance recommends counselling and offering options. And 'eliminating the practice of unmedicated intrauterine procedures' is an aspiration, not an effect. Added what actually works and what does not: paracervical block over topical gel for visceral pain, and misoprostol pretreatment as a practice with evidence against it. This topic appeared in both submitted lists and is kept here once.

### 5. USPSTF Preeclampsia Risk Criteria and Uptake of Low-Dose Aspirin Prophylaxis

*JAMA Network Open · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A cohort analysis found that current risk criteria classify a very large majority of pregnant people as at increased risk for preeclampsia, that moderate-risk demographic factors alone predict poorly, and that aspirin was prescribed to only a minority of eligible patients.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A screening rule that flags almost everyone stops functioning as a screening rule. The consequence described here is alert fatigue: broad criteria plus low prescribing suggests clinicians are discounting the flag rather than acting on it.

**Practical implications.** Prioritise aspirin in patients with objective high-risk features — previous preeclampsia, chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, chronic kidney disease, autoimmune disease, multifetal gestation — where the number needed to treat is favourable. Start before 16 weeks; the mechanism depends on affecting placentation, so late initiation is much less effective. ACOG's recommended dose is 81 mg daily, which differs from the 150 mg used in the European trials.

[JAMA Network Open - preeclampsia risk criteria and aspirin prophylaxis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=USPSTF+preeclampsia+risk+criteria+low-dose+aspirin+prophylaxis+cohort+prescription+rates)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific national cohort study matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search, so the '90% classified as increased risk' and '37% prescribed' figures are not asserted here. The underlying problem — that broad moderate-risk criteria capture most pregnancies and that aspirin uptake is low — is well documented. Added the specific high-risk features, the before-16-weeks timing that determines whether aspirin works at all, and the ACOG 81 mg dose, which the submission gave as '81-150 mg' without noting the divergence.

### 6. Uterine Fibroids and Long-Term Cardiovascular Risk

*JAMA Network Open · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A large population-based retrospective analysis reported that a diagnosis of uterine fibroids was independently associated with substantially higher long-term cardiovascular disease risk — coronary disease and stroke — with the association strongest in younger women.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reframes fibroids from a local structural problem to a possible marker of systemic cardiometabolic risk. Shared risk factors are plausible: obesity, hypertension, insulin resistance and inflammation are associated with both fibroids and vascular disease.

**Practical implications.** Reasonable to check blood pressure, lipids and glycaemic status in a woman presenting with symptomatic fibroids, particularly a young one — she may be in the office for heavy bleeding and never otherwise get cardiovascular screening. But this is an ASSOCIATION in retrospective data, with obvious confounding and detection bias (women in gynaecologic care get measured more), so it justifies screening rather than a claim that fibroids cause vascular disease.

[JAMA Network Open - uterine fibroids and cardiovascular disease risk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=uterine+fibroids+cardiovascular+disease+risk+population+cohort+JAMA+Network+Open)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific analysis matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search, so the '2.7 million women' and '81% higher risk' figures are not asserted. The claim that this 'Reconfigured general gynecology practice parameters' could not be confirmed and was removed. Added the confounding and detection-bias caveat: the submission presented an independent association from retrospective data as though it established fibroids as a systemic risk marker, and a resident should be able to tell those apart.

### 7. Prophylactic Tranexamic Acid in Cesarean Delivery for Placenta Previa

*BMJ · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A randomised double-blind trial of intravenous tranexamic acid given at cord clamping during cesarean delivery for placenta previa reported reduced postpartum haemorrhage and reduced blood loss of 1000 mL or more.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Placenta previa is a setting where the bleeding risk is known in advance and the operation is planned, which makes it an unusually good target for prophylaxis — unlike unselected delivery, where the number needed to treat is poor.

**Practical implications.** Read alongside the wider TXA evidence, which is more mixed than a single positive trial suggests: TRAAP found no significant reduction in its primary outcome after vaginal delivery, and TRAAP2 found a statistically significant but clinically modest effect after cesarean. Prophylaxis makes most sense where baseline risk is high — abnormal placentation, known coagulopathy, prior haemorrhage — rather than as a routine addition to every delivery.

[BMJ - prophylactic tranexamic acid in cesarean delivery for placenta previa](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=tranexamic+acid+placenta+previa+cesarean+postpartum+hemorrhage+randomized+BMJ)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific BMJ trial matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search. Retained because prophylactic TXA in high-risk placental delivery is a plausible and much-studied question. 'Updated international obstetric hemorrhage protocols to recommend' could not be confirmed and was removed. Added the TRAAP and TRAAP2 context, because a reader given only a single positive trial in previa would reasonably generalise to routine prophylaxis — which the larger trials do not support.

### 8. Non-Hormonal Neurokinin Receptor Antagonists for Menopausal Vasomotor Symptoms

*NEJM / The Lancet · Fezolinetant approved 2023; boxed warning added December 2024*

**The breakthrough.** Fezolinetant, an oral selective NK3 receptor antagonist, produces rapid and sustained reduction in moderate-to-severe vasomotor symptoms without oestrogen — the first genuinely mechanism-targeted non-hormonal option, acting on the KNDy neurons that drive thermoregulatory instability after oestrogen withdrawal.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Genuinely valuable for the patients who cannot take oestrogen at all: breast cancer survivors on endocrine therapy, and women with prior thromboembolic or significant cardiovascular disease.

**Practical implications.** SAFETY, which the submission omitted: on 16 December 2024 the FDA added a BOXED WARNING to fezolinetant for rare but serious liver injury, after a postmarketing case of hepatocellular injury within 40 days of starting the drug. Transaminase elevations above three times the upper limit occurred in 2.3% of women in trials. Baseline and periodic liver function testing is required, and the patient needs to know the symptoms — fatigue, nausea, pruritus, jaundice, pale stool, dark urine — and to stop and call.

[FDA - warning about rare serious liver injury with fezolinetant (Veozah)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-communications/fda-adds-warning-about-rare-occurrence-serious-liver-injury-use-veozah-fezolinetant-hot-flashes-due)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — a boxed safety warning was omitted.** The efficacy claim is real; fezolinetant was FDA-approved in 2023 for moderate-to-severe vasomotor symptoms. THE CORRECTION IS A SAFETY ONE: the submission said these agents deliver relief 'without adverse endometrial proliferation' and, in the companion 2026 list, explicitly 'without liver toxicity'. Fezolinetant carries an FDA BOXED WARNING for serious liver injury, added 16 December 2024, with mandatory liver function monitoring. Presenting it as free of hepatic risk would have a clinician skip that monitoring. 'Primary first-line therapies' softened — it is an option for women who cannot take hormone therapy, not a first-line replacement for it.

### 9. Conception Rates in Endometriosis-Associated Infertility

*The Lancet · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A long-term population cohort reported that women with infertility attributed to endometriosis achieve higher cumulative conception rates than has generally been assumed, compared with other causes of infertility.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Matters mostly for what it changes in the consultation. A diagnosis of endometriosis is often received as a verdict on fertility, and the cumulative-conception framing is both more accurate and less damaging than a single-cycle probability.

**Practical implications.** Counsel on cumulative rather than per-cycle chances. On surgery, be careful about the direction of the trade-off: excision of endometriomas can reduce ovarian reserve, so repeated or aggressive surgery for the sake of fertility can work against it. Diagnostic laparoscopy is no longer required to diagnose endometriosis, which can be made clinically and by imaging — so 'early diagnostic laparoscopy' is not the current standard.

[The Lancet - conception rates in endometriosis-associated infertility](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=endometriosis+infertility+cumulative+conception+rates+population+cohort+Lancet)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, with one correction.** No specific 30-year cohort of over 4 million women was located; the url is a PubMed title search and those figures are not asserted. ONE CORRECTION: the submission recommended 'early diagnostic laparoscopy and conservative surgical optimization... to PRESERVE intrinsic ovarian reserve'. Current practice does not require laparoscopy to diagnose endometriosis, and endometrioma excision can REDUCE ovarian reserve rather than preserve it — so as written it pointed the wrong way on both counts.

### 10. Perinatal Opioid Use Disorder Management after Elimination of the DATA-Waiver

*Obstetrics & Gynecology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Practice guidance for obstetrician-gynaecologists following the removal of the federal DATA-2000 waiver requirement, which means any clinician with a standard DEA registration can now prescribe buprenorphine.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Pregnancy is a period of unusually high engagement with healthcare for a population that otherwise has poor access, so the obstetric visit is often the only opportunity to start treatment. Untreated opioid use disorder in pregnancy carries far greater risk than treatment does.

**Practical implications.** Buprenorphine or methadone are both appropriate in pregnancy and both are preferred to withdrawal management, which has high relapse and overdose risk. Note the access asymmetry: buprenorphine can now be prescribed from any office, while methadone for OUD still requires a federally certified opioid treatment programme. Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome is an expected, treatable consequence of maternal treatment — not a reason to withhold or taper it.

[Obstetrics & Gynecology - perinatal opioid use disorder practice update](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=ACOG+perinatal+opioid+use+disorder+buprenorphine+DATA+waiver+Obstetrics+Gynecology)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, with two corrections.** The DATA-waiver elimination (Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023) is real, but the specific ACOG practice update was not located; the url is a PubMed title search. TWO CORRECTIONS. Methadone was implicitly grouped with buprenorphine as office-prescribable — it still requires a certified opioid treatment programme. And 'reducing neonatal abstinence syndrome SEVERITY' inverts the framing: maternal treatment is expected to produce neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome, which is treatable, and fear of it is a documented reason clinicians wrongly withhold or taper treatment.

### 1. First-Trimester Preeclampsia Screening with PlGF, sFlt-1 and Uterine Artery Doppler

*Obstetrics & Gynecology / practice advisory · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Multi-marker first-trimester screening — placental growth factor (PlGF), soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1), maternal mean arterial pressure and uterine artery Doppler pulsatility index, combined in an algorithm — identifies women at high risk of PRETERM preeclampsia far better than clinical risk factors alone. The ASPRE trial showed aspirin 150 mg daily in women selected this way reduced preterm preeclampsia by about 60%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The competing-risks algorithm developed by the Fetal Medicine Foundation is used routinely in parts of Europe and the UK. ACOG, however, continues to recommend screening by CLINICAL RISK FACTORS and has not adopted first-trimester biomarker screening as its standard — so this is a genuine transatlantic divergence rather than a settled change.

**Practical implications.** Where the algorithm is available, it concentrates aspirin on the women who benefit rather than flagging most of the population. Timing is the non-negotiable part: aspirin must start before 16 weeks to affect placentation. Note the dose divergence — ASPRE and the European algorithm use 150 mg; ACOG recommends 81 mg. And the screening performs well for PRETERM preeclampsia specifically, much less so for term disease.

[First-trimester screening for preeclampsia with angiogenic markers](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=first-trimester+preeclampsia+screening+PlGF+sFlt-1+uterine+artery+Doppler+aspirin+ASPRE)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the guideline claim is reversed.** The screening approach and the ASPRE result are real, but the specific 2026 ACOG Practice Advisory was not located; the url is a PubMed title search. THE CORRECTION: the submission said biomarker screening 'REPLACED traditional demographic-only screening in updated ACOG and SMFM advisories, establishing early-trimester angiogenic biomarker testing as the PREFERRED STANDARD'. ACOG has not adopted first-trimester biomarker screening and continues to recommend clinical risk-factor assessment — the entry now presents this as a US-versus-Europe divergence, which is what it is. Added the 150 mg versus 81 mg dose divergence and the preterm-versus-term performance distinction.

### 2. Dual Neurokinin-1/3 Receptor Antagonists for Menopausal Vasomotor Symptoms

*NEJM / JAMA · 2024-2026*

**The breakthrough.** Elinzanetant, a dual NK1/NK3 receptor antagonist, produced rapid and sustained reduction in the frequency and severity of moderate-to-severe hot flushes across the phase 3 OASIS programme, with additional benefit on sleep disturbance — the NK1 component is the reason it addresses sleep as well as flushing.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A second mechanism-targeted non-hormonal option alongside the NK3-selective fezolinetant, for women who cannot or will not take oestrogen.

**Practical implications.** DO NOT generalise a clean hepatic profile across the class. Fezolinetant, the NK3-selective agent, carries an FDA BOXED WARNING for rare serious liver injury added in December 2024, with required liver function monitoring; transaminase elevations above three times the upper limit occurred in 2.3% of trial participants. Any patient starting a neurokinin antagonist needs to know which agent they are on and what monitoring it requires.

[Dual NK1/NK3 receptor antagonist for menopausal vasomotor symptoms](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=elinzanetant+neurokinin+1+3+receptor+antagonist+vasomotor+symptoms+phase+3+OASIS)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — a safety claim was inverted.** The dual NK1/NK3 mechanism and the phase 3 programme are real, though the specific publication was not resolved and the url is a PubMed title search. THE CORRECTION IS THE IMPORTANT ONE: the submission stated these agents work 'WITHOUT LIVER TOXICITY or endometrial thickening'. Fezolinetant — an NK3 antagonist in the same class discussion — carries an FDA boxed warning for serious liver injury and requires liver function monitoring. Even if elinzanetant's hepatic profile proves cleaner, a blanket class claim of no liver toxicity would have a clinician skip mandated monitoring. 'Primary first-line options' softened to what it is: an option for women who cannot take hormone therapy.

### 3. Continuous Glucose Monitoring in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

*Obstetrics & Gynecology · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Continuous glucose monitoring in gestational diabetes improves time in the pregnancy target range and has been examined for effects on macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycaemia, compared with intermittent fingerstick testing.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The strongest randomised evidence for CGM in pregnancy comes from CONCEPTT, in women with TYPE 1 diabetes, where CGM improved neonatal outcomes. Extending that to gestational diabetes is a reasonable extrapolation but a weaker evidence base, and most women with GDM are managed on diet alone.

**Practical implications.** Most defensible in GDM requiring insulin, where the information changes titration decisions and where the neonatal risks are concentrated. The pregnancy target range is tighter than the non-pregnant one — commonly 63 to 140 mg/dL — and CGM makes postprandial excursions visible in a way that four fingersticks a day cannot. Weigh against cost, and against the burden of continuous data in a patient already anxious about her pregnancy.

[Continuous glucose monitoring in gestational diabetes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=continuous+glucose+monitoring+gestational+diabetes+time+in+range+macrosomia+neonatal+hypoglycemia)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, with the evidence base qualified.** No specific ACOG guideline update endorsing CGM in GDM was located; the url is a PubMed title search. QUALIFICATION ADDED: the submission stated CGM 'significantly reduc[es] fetal macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycemia' in GDM as an established finding. The randomised neonatal-outcome evidence for CGM in pregnancy is strongest in TYPE 1 diabetes (CONCEPTT); for gestational diabetes specifically it is weaker, and the entry now says so. The 63-140 mg/dL range from the submission is retained as the pregnancy target.

### 4. Prophylactic Tranexamic Acid after Vaginal Delivery: What the Trials Actually Show

*NEJM · TRAAP 2018; TRAAP2 2021; IPD meta-analysis 2024*

**The breakthrough.** TRAAP randomised 4,079 women having a planned vaginal delivery at term to 1 g intravenous tranexamic acid or placebo, on top of prophylactic oxytocin. The PRIMARY outcome — postpartum haemorrhage of 500 mL or more — was NOT significantly reduced: 8.1% versus 9.8%, relative risk 0.83, with the confidence interval crossing 1. A secondary outcome, provider-assessed clinically significant haemorrhage, was lower.

**Clinical guideline impact.** TRAAP2, in cesarean delivery, found a statistically significant but clinically modest reduction. An individual patient data meta-analysis was published in The Lancet in 2024. Taken together the picture is a small effect at best after vaginal delivery, not a demonstrated benefit — which is why prophylactic TXA has not entered routine third-stage management.

**Practical implications.** Do not add prophylactic TXA to the routine active management of the third stage on the strength of these trials. Where it clearly belongs is TREATMENT of established postpartum haemorrhage, given within 3 hours (the WOMAN trial), and prophylaxis in specifically high-risk surgical deliveries. Oxytocin remains the prophylaxis that works.

[NEJM - tranexamic acid for the prevention of blood loss after vaginal delivery (TRAAP)](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800942)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — original stated the opposite of the primary result.** The submission said a large prospective trial 'established that prophylactic IV tranexamic acid... in high-risk vaginal births SIGNIFICANTLY REDUCES blood loss ≥500 mL and postpartum blood transfusion rates', and that consensus protocols now integrate prophylactic TXA into active management of the third stage. TRAAP (NEJM 2018, 4,079 women) is the definitive trial of exactly this and its PRIMARY OUTCOME — PPH ≥500 mL — was NOT significantly reduced (8.1% vs 9.8%, RR 0.83). Rewritten to the actual findings across TRAAP, TRAAP2 and the 2024 IPD meta-analysis, and to distinguish prophylaxis from treatment, where TXA does have clear evidence (WOMAN trial, within 3 hours).

### 5. First-Trimester CMV Screening with Valaciclovir to Prevent Congenital Infection

*American Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology / The Lancet · 2020-2026*

**The breakthrough.** High-dose oral valaciclovir given after confirmed primary maternal cytomegalovirus infection in the first trimester reduced vertical transmission in a randomised placebo-controlled trial (Shahar-Nissan and colleagues, The Lancet 2020) — from about 30% to 11% of fetuses infected.

**Clinical guideline impact.** This is the finding that reopened the universal-screening question. The historic argument against screening was that identifying primary infection changed nothing; an effective intervention removes that objection. ACOG has NOT adopted universal CMV screening, and serologic interpretation remains difficult — IgM persists for months and IgG avidity testing is needed to date the infection.

**Practical implications.** Congenital CMV is the commonest infectious cause of sensorineural hearing loss in children, which is what makes this worth the trouble. If screening is undertaken, it has to be first-trimester and paired with a plan: confirmatory avidity testing, and treatment within a window where it can affect transmission. Screening without that pathway generates anxiety and amniocenteses without benefit.

[The Lancet - valaciclovir to prevent vertical transmission of cytomegalovirus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=valacyclovir+prevent+vertical+transmission+cytomegalovirus+first+trimester+randomised+Lancet)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the guideline position.** The valaciclovir trial is real (Lancet 2020) and the transmission reduction is accurately described in outline; the specific 2026 screening trial in the submission was not located and the url is a PubMed title search. CORRECTION: the submission said this 'Prompted maternal-fetal medicine societies to re-evaluate long-standing opposition... shifting TOWARD early gestational serologic screening protocols' — ACOG has not adopted universal CMV screening, and the entry now says so. Added the serologic interpretation difficulty (IgM persistence, avidity testing), which is the practical reason universal screening remains contested.

### 6. ERAS Protocols in Major Gynecologic Oncology Surgery

*Gynecologic Oncology / ERAS Society · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Structured perioperative pathways in gynaecologic oncology surgery — preoperative carbohydrate loading and shortened fasting, opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia, goal-directed fluid management, early feeding and early ambulation — associated with shorter length of stay and fewer complications.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Gynaecologic oncology was an early adopter of ERAS, and the outcome data are among the better ones in surgical oncology. The effect is attributable to the bundle rather than to individual components, and adherence rate predicts benefit.

**Practical implications.** The opioid-sparing element matters beyond the admission: a patient discharged after major pelvic surgery without an opioid prescription she does not need is the durable win. Note that these are largely before-and-after and cohort comparisons rather than randomised trials, so secular improvements in surgical care are a plausible partial explanation for reported gains.

[ERAS in gynecologic oncology surgery](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=enhanced+recovery+after+surgery+gynecologic+oncology+guidelines+length+of+stay+surgical+site+infection)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** ERAS in gynaecologic oncology is well established, but the specific multi-centre study was not located; the url is a PubMed title search, so the '2 days shorter stay' and '35% fewer surgical site infections' figures are not asserted. 'Establish MANDATORY perioperative pathways' softened — society guidelines recommend rather than mandate. Added the study-design caveat: much of this literature is before-and-after rather than randomised, which is the main reason to treat the effect sizes as indicative.

### 7. Fertility-Sparing Surgery for Early-Stage Cervical Cancer

*The Lancet Oncology / SGO and NCCN guidance · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Radical trachelectomy with pelvic lymph node assessment preserves the uterus in selected women with early-stage cervical cancer, with oncologic outcomes reported as comparable to radical hysterectomy in tumours under 2 cm.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Two developments matter alongside it. ConCerv and the SHAPE trial showed that in carefully selected LOW-RISK early cervical cancer, LESS radical surgery — simple hysterectomy or conisation with nodal assessment — is oncologically adequate, so the field is moving toward de-escalation generally. And LACC showed minimally invasive radical hysterectomy had WORSE survival than open surgery, which is why the surgical approach matters as much as the extent.

**Practical implications.** Selection is everything: tumour under 2 cm, no lymphovascular space involvement, negative nodes, and a patient who genuinely wants future fertility. Counsel honestly about obstetric outcomes after trachelectomy — second-trimester loss and preterm birth rates are substantially raised, and a cerclage is usually placed. Most of the trachelectomy evidence is observational rather than randomised.

[Fertility-sparing surgery in early-stage cervical cancer](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=radical+trachelectomy+fertility+sparing+early+stage+cervical+cancer+oncologic+outcomes+survival)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — study design and missing context.** Fertility-sparing surgery is real and guideline-endorsed for selected early disease, but no multi-year prospective trial establishing 'equivalent 5-year disease-free survival and overall survival' was located; the trachelectomy evidence base is largely OBSERVATIONAL, and the url is a PubMed title search. 'Gold standard recommendation' overstates an option requiring narrow selection. Added three things the submission omitted: the de-escalation direction from ConCerv and SHAPE, the LACC finding that minimally invasive radical hysterectomy was inferior, and the raised preterm birth and second-trimester loss rates after trachelectomy — which is the counselling point for a patient choosing this specifically for fertility.

### 8. Mesh and Native Tissue Repair in Apical Pelvic Organ Prolapse

*American Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology / AUGS-IUGA guidance · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Abdominal sacrocolpopexy with mesh — laparoscopic or robotic — provides better anatomical durability for apical prolapse than native tissue vaginal repair, and remains standard practice. This is a fundamentally different operation from the TRANSVAGINAL mesh kits that the FDA ordered off the market in 2019.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The distinction is the whole point. Transvaginal mesh for prolapse was withdrawn after unacceptable rates of erosion, pain and reoperation; abdominally placed sacrocolpopexy mesh was never withdrawn and has good long-term data. Conflating them is the commonest source of confusion in counselling a patient who has read about 'mesh'.

**Practical implications.** Mesh complications after sacrocolpopexy are not negligible: the extended CARE trial reported mesh erosion approaching 10% by 7 years, considerably higher than is often quoted. Durability has to be weighed against that, against the patient's age and activity, and against uterosacral or sacrospinous ligament suspension as a mesh-free alternative with a good evidence base.
