## 2025

10 entries · **10 with a citation problem — see the checks below**

### 1. Middle Meningeal Artery Embolization for Chronic Subdural Hematoma (EMBOLISE)

*NEJM · November 2024*

**The breakthrough.** Adjunctive middle meningeal artery embolization with a liquid embolic agent, added to surgical evacuation or to conservative management, significantly reduced hematoma recurrence or progression requiring reoperation compared with standard care alone in subacute and chronic subdural hematoma.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first randomised evidence for an endovascular adjunct in a condition managed surgically for a century. It treats the mechanism — the neovascularised, leaky membrane fed by the MMA — rather than just draining the product of it, which is why recurrence falls rather than merely being delayed.

**Practical implications.** Most useful in the patients who recur: elderly, anticoagulated, or already reoperated. Note it is ADJUNCTIVE in this trial — embolization did not replace evacuation of a symptomatic collection, and a patient with mass effect and deficit still needs the burr holes.

[NEJM - embolization of the middle meningeal artery for chronic subdural hematoma](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2313472)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year.** Real and positive; NEJM, November 2024 rather than 2025. 'Establishes MMA embolization as a DEFINITIVE adjunctive standard of care in guidelines' softened — the trial supports it strongly but no guideline revision was confirmed. Added the point the entry blurred: embolization was adjunctive to evacuation or conservative care, not an alternative to evacuating a symptomatic collection.

### 2. Congress of Neurological Surgeons Guidelines on Emerging Therapies for Brain Metastases

*Neurosurgery · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A multidisciplinary guideline series covering parenchymal and leptomeningeal brain metastases, integrating targeted systemic therapy, stereotactic radiosurgery and laser interstitial thermal therapy into the surgical decision algorithm.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The substantive change is that a brain metastasis is no longer automatically a local-treatment problem. Brain-penetrant targeted agents — osimertinib in EGFR-mutant lung cancer, alectinib in ALK-rearranged disease, tucatinib-based regimens in HER2-positive breast cancer — achieve intracranial responses that can defer or replace local therapy in selected patients.

**Practical implications.** Mutational status now belongs in the operative discussion alongside lesion count and location. LITT is a genuine option for recurrent lesions and for radiation necrosis, where re-craniotomy is high-risk and the distinction between tumour and necrosis is often only resolved histologically.

[Neurosurgery - CNS guidelines on brain metastases](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Congress+of+Neurological+Surgeons+guidelines+brain+metastases+emerging+therapies+Neurosurgery)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** The CNS does publish guideline series in Neurosurgery and the clinical content is consistent with current neuro-oncology practice, but no specific 2025 document was located and the '23 evidence-based recommendations' figure is therefore not asserted; the url is a PubMed title search. 'Establishes LITT as a valid alternative to open craniotomy' softened to an option in the settings where it is actually used. Named agents were adjusted to the ones with established intracranial activity.

### 3. Vorasidenib in IDH1- or IDH2-Mutant Low-Grade Glioma (INDIGO), and Targeted Therapy in Diffuse Midline Glioma

*NEJM · INDIGO published 2023*

**The breakthrough.** INDIGO showed that vorasidenib, a brain-penetrant oral IDH1/2 inhibitor, more than doubled progression-free survival and significantly delayed the need for radiation and chemotherapy in residual or recurrent grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma after surgery. For H3K27M-mutant diffuse midline glioma, the imipridone dordivaprone (ONC201) has shown activity in single-arm studies.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Changes what the operation is for. In IDH-mutant grade 2 glioma the surgeon is now securing tissue and cytoreduction ahead of an effective oral maintenance therapy, with the specific goal of deferring radiotherapy and its cognitive cost in a young patient with years of expected survival.

**Practical implications.** Molecular status has to be known early, which argues for prompt IDH testing rather than waiting on a full panel. Maximal safe resection remains the priority — vorasidenib defers adjuvant toxicity, it does not substitute for cytoreduction. Note the asymmetry in evidence: INDIGO is a randomised phase 3 trial; the H3K27M data are not.

[NEJM - vorasidenib in IDH1- or IDH2-mutant low-grade glioma](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2304194)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year and evidence asymmetry.** INDIGO is real and the PFS finding is accurate, but it was published in NEJM in 2023, not 2025. The submission bracketed vorasidenib and dordivaprone together as 'multi-center Phase 3 data'; only vorasidenib has a randomised phase 3 trial, and the entry now says so. This trial also appeared in the second neurosurgery list submitted; it is kept here once rather than on both year pages, since the same study appearing under 2025 and 2026 would read as a fault.

### 4. Endovascular Thrombectomy for Medium and Distal Vessel Occlusion (ESCAPE-MeVO, DISTAL, DISCOUNT)

*NEJM · February 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Three randomised trials tested thrombectomy against best medical therapy in medium and distal vessel occlusion — M2/M3, A2, P2 territories. All three were NEGATIVE: thrombectomy did not improve 90-day functional outcome. ESCAPE-MeVO randomised 530 patients within 12 hours of last seen normal.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The ESCAPE-MeVO investigators state that their results 'do not support routine endovascular treatment of acute ischaemic stroke due to medium-vessel occlusion.' The accompanying NEJM editorial was titled 'Medium- and Distal-Vessel Occlusion — The Limit of Thrombectomy?' Guidelines have not expanded candidacy to MeVO, because the trials argue against it.

**Practical implications.** Reserve thrombectomy for proximal large vessel occlusion. Intravenous thrombolysis remains the treatment for MeVO. The technical ability to navigate a microcatheter into a distal branch turned out not to translate into patient benefit — smaller territory at risk, more procedural risk per unit of tissue saved.

[NEJM - medium- and distal-vessel occlusion: the limit of thrombectomy?](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMe2500492)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** Real and correctly reported — the submission was appropriately cautious here, describing the need for strict sub-selection and the haemorrhage risk without claiming benefit, which is notably more accurate than the version of this entry submitted in the Emergency Medicine list, where it was reversed into a positive finding. Sharpened to state plainly that all three trials were negative, and added the trial size and the investigators' own conclusion. 'Establishing dedicated micro-device selection criteria for distal occlusions' could not be confirmed and was removed.

### 5. Low-Intensity Pulsed Ultrasound with Microbubbles for Blood-Brain Barrier Opening in Recurrent Glioblastoma

*The Lancet Oncology · 2023*

**The breakthrough.** A phase 1 trial used a skull-implanted ultrasound array with intravenous microbubbles to transiently open the blood-brain barrier in recurrent glioblastoma, enabling delivery of albumin-bound paclitaxel — a drug that does not otherwise reach the brain — with carboplatin. Sonication was safe and repeatable, and opening was reversible within about an hour.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes non-invasive, repeatable BBB opening as a workable drug-delivery platform, which reframes the problem in neuro-oncology: many systemic agents fail in glioma because of access rather than because of biology.

**Practical implications.** This is a delivery technology at an early stage, not a treatment with demonstrated survival benefit. What it opens up is the reuse of potent agents already known to work against glioma cells in vitro but discarded because they never reached the tumour.

[The Lancet Oncology - ultrasound-based blood-brain barrier opening in recurrent glioblastoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=low-intensity+pulsed+ultrasound+microbubbles+blood-brain+barrier+paclitaxel+recurrent+glioblastoma+Lancet+Oncology)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — phase and year.** The work is real (Northwestern, Sonabend and colleagues, Lancet Oncology 2023) but it is a PHASE 1 trial, not the multi-trial validation the submission implied, and the year is 2023. The specific citation was not resolved from the searches run, so the url is a PubMed title search. 'Validates MRI-guided non-invasive sonication' and 'establishing protocol safety for repeated outpatient BBB opening' overstate a phase 1 result and were softened; the delivered agent was albumin-bound paclitaxel with carboplatin.

### 6. Predicting Long-Term Pain Freedom after Microvascular Decompression for Trigeminal Neuralgia

*Neurosurgery · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A scoring system combining clinical features with high-resolution MRI findings, intended to predict durable pain freedom after microvascular decompression for classical trigeminal neuralgia.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Standardises what counts as neurovascular conflict on preoperative imaging — the distinction between true arterial compression with distortion of the nerve, venous contact, and incidental contact without deformity, which is where a good deal of disagreement about MVD candidacy actually lives.

**Practical implications.** Best outcomes follow classical, episodic, lancinating pain in a trigeminal distribution with clear arterial compression on high-resolution imaging (CISS or FIESTA sequences). Constant background pain, sensory loss, or absent arterial conflict predicts poorly, and those patients are better served by considering ablative options — rhizotomy or radiosurgery — with realistic expectations set beforehand.

[Neurosurgery - predicting outcomes after microvascular decompression for trigeminal neuralgia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=microvascular+decompression+trigeminal+neuralgia+predictive+scoring+outcome+Neurosurgery)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific validation study matching this description was located, and the institutional attribution (Columbia and NewYork-Presbyterian) is unconfirmed; the url is a PubMed title search. The underlying clinical principle — that classical presentation plus demonstrated arterial conflict predicts durable relief, and that atypical or non-compressive cases do worse — is well established, and has been stated explicitly here rather than left as 'a refined scoring system', which taught nothing usable.

### 7. Awake Full-Endoscopic Lumbar Decompression under Regional Anaesthesia

*Journal of Neurosurgery: Spine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Prospective registry data on full-endoscopic lumbar discectomy and decompression performed under targeted epidural or regional anaesthesia rather than general anaesthesia — 'awake spine surgery' — with standardised surgical and anaesthetic pathways for outpatient care.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The value is in who becomes operable. A frail or cardiopulmonary-compromised patient with disabling neurogenic claudication may be a poor candidate for general endotracheal anaesthesia but a reasonable one for an awake endoscopic decompression.

**Practical implications.** Fewer intraoperative haemodynamic swings and faster recovery than open or microdiscectomy under general anaesthesia. Two honest caveats: registry data are not randomised, so selection favours the awake approach, and the technique has a real learning curve — endoscopic decompression results are strongly operator-dependent, and an awake patient reduces tolerance for a long operation.

[Journal of Neurosurgery: Spine - awake full-endoscopic lumbar decompression](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=awake+full-endoscopic+lumbar+decompression+regional+anesthesia+registry+Journal+of+Neurosurgery+Spine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific multicentre registry publication matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search. 'Establishes standardized surgical and anesthetic pathways' and the comparative claim of faster recovery than open microdiscectomy are presented as registry observations rather than established findings, and the non-randomised design plus the learning-curve caveat were added — both bear directly on whether the reported advantage would hold in a given surgeon's hands.

### 8. Long-Term Adaptive Deep Brain Stimulation in Parkinson Disease (ADAPT-PD)

*JAMA Neurology · 22 September 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The first trial of CHRONIC adaptive deep brain stimulation — sensing subthalamic beta-band local field potentials and adjusting stimulation amplitude in real time. 68 participants with moderate-to-advanced Parkinson disease, already implanted with bilateral leads and STABLE on continuous DBS at baseline. The primary outcome was met: most achieved good on-time without troublesome dyskinesia relative to their stable continuous DBS.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes that chronic closed-loop stimulation is tolerable, effective and safe — a feasibility and non-inferiority result. It does NOT establish superiority over continuous DBS: the trial was NONRANDOMISED and open-label, with 68 patients selected for being already stable on continuous stimulation, which is not the population in which continuous DBS fails.

**Practical implications.** Stimulation-related adverse events were mostly transient worsening during the setup phase and nearly all resolved with reprogramming; no serious device-related complications. Sensing-capable devices are a reasonable choice where available, but no guideline recommends closed-loop systems as the preferred primary implant, and adaptive stimulation still requires expert programming rather than replacing it.

[JAMA Neurology - long-term personalized adaptive deep brain stimulation in Parkinson disease](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12455485/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — superiority claimed from a nonrandomised trial.** Real: JAMA Neurology, online 22 September 2025, 68 participants, 70.6% men, mean age 62.2, mean disease duration 13.5 years. THE CORRECTION MATTERS because this claim appeared in three of the four submitted lists: ADAPT-PD is NONRANDOMISED and OPEN-LABEL, and its primary outcome was performance relative to stable continuous DBS. It cannot show that adaptive DBS 'supercedes' or 'outperforms' continuous DBS. Nor has any guideline made sensing-capable pulse generators 'preferred primary implants'. Also removed 'eliminates the need for manual trial-and-error programming' — setup required reprogramming in most participants.

### 9. Timing of Decompressive Craniectomy in Malignant Middle Cerebral Artery Infarction

*Pooled randomised evidence (DECIMAL, DESTINY, HAMLET, DESTINY II) · Established evidence base*

**The breakthrough.** In patients aged 60 or under with malignant MCA infarction, decompressive craniectomy WITHIN 48 HOURS substantially reduces death and severe disability: in the pooled analysis of 93 randomised patients with NIHSS above 16, survival with mRS 4 or better was 75% versus 24%, an absolute risk reduction of about 51%. DESTINY II extended the survival benefit to patients over 60, at the cost of greater disability among survivors.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The 48-hour window is where the randomised evidence sits. Surgery beyond 48 hours has NOT been shown superior to best medical management, and the optimal timing outside that window has never been addressed by a randomised trial or pooled analysis — it remains an open question supported only by observational series.

**Practical implications.** Decide early. The decision is as much about acceptable disability as about survival: decompression converts deaths into survivors, many of whom are left significantly disabled, and that conversation belongs with the family before the operation rather than after it. Age above 60 shifts the balance further toward disability.

[Decompressive craniectomy for malignant middle cerebral artery infarction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8926127/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REPLACED — the cited trial does not exist and the claim is contrary to available evidence.** No Lancet Neurology international randomised trial of decompressive craniectomy beyond the 48-hour window was located. The submission stated it 'confirm[ed] that decompressive craniectomy retains mortality-reduction benefits up to 72 hours post-stroke onset' — the available evidence points the other way: craniectomy performed more than 48 hours after onset does not appear superior to best medical management, and no randomised trial has addressed late timing. Rewritten around the randomised evidence that does exist, with the disability-versus-survival trade-off made explicit, since that is the substance of the consent conversation.

### 10. Cerebrospinal Fluid Cell-Free DNA Sequencing for Molecular Diagnosis of CNS Tumours

*Nature Medicine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Sequencing of cell-free DNA from cerebrospinal fluid — obtained intraoperatively or by lumbar puncture — for molecular profiling of primary brain tumours and leptomeningeal disease, without tissue biopsy.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Potentially transformative where tissue carries prohibitive risk: brainstem, thalamic and pineal region lesions, and leptomeningeal disease, where the diagnosis is often made late because cytology is insensitive and repeated punctures are needed.

**Practical implications.** Yield depends on tumour location and CSF circulation — periventricular and leptomeningeal disease shed detectably, whereas a peripheral hemispheric lesion may not, so a negative result does not exclude tumour. Treat it as a way to obtain molecular information when tissue is unobtainable, not as a substitute for biopsy when biopsy is safe.

[Nature Medicine - CSF cell-free DNA for CNS tumour diagnosis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=cerebrospinal+fluid+cell-free+DNA+sequencing+CNS+tumor+molecular+diagnosis+Nature+Medicine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific prospective Nature Medicine validation study matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search. The approach is genuine and under active investigation. 'Endorses CSF liquid biopsy as an OFFICIAL diagnostic tool in neuro-oncology guidelines' could not be confirmed and was removed — no guideline endorsement was identified. Added the location-dependent yield, which is the limitation that determines whether a negative result means anything.

## 2026

8 entries · **8 with a citation problem — see the checks below**

### 1. Squid Middle Meningeal Artery Embolization for Subacute and Chronic Subdural Haematoma (STEM)

*Presented 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Middle meningeal artery embolization with a non-adhesive liquid embolic agent (Squid), tested standalone or adjunctive against conservative or surgical management, reported reduced treatment failure and hematoma progression in subacute and chronic subdural haematoma.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Adds a second embolic agent and, importantly, a STANDALONE arm — EMBOLISE tested embolization as an adjunct to evacuation or conservative care, whereas the question of whether embolization alone can substitute for surgery in a selected patient is the one that changes referral pathways.

**Practical implications.** Transradial access makes this a short-stay or day-case procedure, which is the practical draw for a frail anticoagulated patient in whom general anaesthesia and burr holes carry real risk. A patient with mass effect and a neurological deficit still needs the collection drained.

[STEM - Squid embolization of the middle meningeal artery for subdural haematoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=STEM+trial+Squid+middle+meningeal+artery+embolization+subdural+hematoma)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** The MMA embolization trial programme is real and STEM is a recognised trial name in it, but this specific publication was not located and the url is a PubMed title search. TWO CLAIMS REMOVED: 'significantly reduces... major neurological events' and 'Provides LEVEL I clinical trial evidence confirming that endovascular dural artery devascularization addresses the pathophysiological cause of capillary leak' — neither was confirmed, and a trial does not confirm a pathophysiological mechanism. Also note the submission listed EMBOLISE separately in the same batch; EMBOLISE is kept on the 2025 page so the same trial does not appear under both years.

### 2. Early Minimally Invasive Removal of Intracerebral Haemorrhage (ENRICH)

*NEJM · April 2024*

**The breakthrough.** Early (within 24 hours) image-guided minimally invasive trans-sulcal parafascicular surgery for spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage improved 180-day utility-weighted mRS compared with guideline-based medical management. The benefit was driven by the LOBAR subgroup; the trial's adaptive design enrolled predominantly lobar haemorrhage after early analyses.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The first positive surgical trial in spontaneous ICH after decades of neutral results (STICH, STICH II, MISTIE III). What changed was the approach — a trans-sulcal tubular corridor with early evacuation rather than late craniotomy through cortex.

**Practical implications.** Applies to LOBAR haemorrhage evacuated early through a minimally invasive corridor at a centre with the equipment and the workflow to do it within hours. It does not license craniotomy for deep basal ganglia bleeds, and it does not apply to late presentation. The adaptive enrolment is the reason the lobar restriction matters rather than being a subgroup footnote.

[NEJM - trial of early minimally invasive removal of intracerebral hemorrhage](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2308440)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year and the strength of the guideline claim.** Real and positive; NEJM, April 2024. The lobar-driven benefit is correctly identified in the submission. Two softenings: 'establishing MIPS as a FIRST-LINE recommendation in AHA/ASA and neurosurgical guidelines' was not confirmed, and the adaptive design detail was added because it explains why the result belongs to lobar haemorrhage specifically rather than to ICH in general.

### 3. Decompressive Craniectomy for Severe Deep Supratentorial Intracerebral Haemorrhage (SWITCH)

*The Lancet · 1 June 2024*

**The breakthrough.** Decompressive craniectomy WITHOUT haematoma evacuation, added to best medical treatment, in severe deep basal ganglia or thalamic ICH. 42 European centres, 201 patients enrolled and 190 analysed. The result was a TREND toward better 6-month outcome that did NOT reach statistical significance — the trial was stopped early for lack of funding and is underpowered.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Provides weak evidence that craniectomy MIGHT be superior to medical treatment alone in this population. The investigators' own framing is that of a signal requiring confirmation, not a practice change.

**Practical implications.** The finding that matters for consent: survival was associated with severe disability in BOTH groups. This is a life-saving operation in the literal sense, and the survivors are severely disabled either way — which is the conversation to have with a family, not a claim of improved outcome. The result does not transfer to haemorrhage in other locations.

[The Lancet - decompressive craniectomy for spontaneous deep intracerebral haemorrhage (SWITCH)](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(24)00702-5/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — a non-significant trend was reported as a demonstrated benefit.** Real: The Lancet, 1 June 2024, 42 centres, 201 patients, 190 in the primary analysis. THE CORRECTION: the submission said the trial 'demonstrat[ed] reductions in 180-day mortality and severe disability' and that guidelines were updated to include craniectomy as 'an evidence-based salvage procedure'. The trial was STOPPED EARLY for funding, was underpowered, and its primary outcome was a non-significant trend — described in the accompanying literature as 'weak evidence'. Added the finding the entry omitted entirely: survival came with severe disability in both arms.

### 4. Thrombectomy for Stroke with Large Ischaemic Core (SELECT2)

*NEJM · February 2023*

**The breakthrough.** Endovascular thrombectomy plus medical care in acute stroke with a LARGE ischaemic core (ASPECTS 3-5 or core volume 50 mL or more) within 24 hours improved functional outcome compared with medical care alone, with better 1-year survival on follow-up.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Together with ANGEL-ASPECT and RESCUE-Japan LIMIT, removed large core volume as an automatic exclusion from thrombectomy. A large established infarct is no longer a reason not to intervene.

**Practical implications.** Substantially widens the candidate pool, and the practical consequence is that a large core on initial imaging should trigger a thrombectomy conversation rather than close it. Note that most patients in these trials still ended up disabled — the benefit is a shift in the distribution of disability, not restoration to independence, and functional independence remained the minority outcome.

[NEJM - trial of endovascular thrombectomy for large ischemic stroke (SELECT2)](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2214403)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year.** Real and positive; NEJM, February 2023 rather than 2026. Entry criteria and the 24-hour window are correctly stated in the submission. Added the companion trials that made the guideline change possible, and the caveat that functional independence remained the minority outcome — 'ensuring patients with extensive baseline cerebral ischemia receive endovascular intervention' as written implies a better expected outcome than the trials delivered.

### 5. Thrombectomy in Stroke with Extended Lesion and Extended Time Window (TENSION)

*The Lancet · 2023*

**The breakthrough.** Thrombectomy in large-core ischaemic stroke up to 12 hours from onset, with eligibility determined by non-contrast CT and ASPECTS rather than by CT perfusion or MRI, improved functional outcome versus medical management.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The imaging point is the important one: benefit was demonstrable using basic non-contrast CT, so advanced perfusion software is not a prerequisite for selecting large-core patients. That has direct consequences for equity of access in community and resource-limited centres.

**Practical implications.** Removes the delay of waiting for automated perfusion processing before a transfer decision. A community hospital with a CT scanner and someone who can read ASPECTS can identify a candidate and move them.

[The Lancet - TENSION, thrombectomy in extended lesion and extended time window](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(23)02032-9/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year.** Real and positive; The Lancet, 2023 rather than 2026. The non-contrast CT selection point and the 12-hour window are accurately reported in the submission, as is the equity implication. No substantive correction beyond the date.

### 6. Guideline for the Early Management of Patients With Acute Ischaemic Stroke

*Stroke (AHA/ASA) · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A multi-society update to the acute ischaemic stroke guideline, synthesising the past several years of trial evidence: tenecteplase in place of alteplase, thrombectomy eligibility extended to large-core presentations, prehospital routing, and early complication management.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Consolidates changes that had already happened in practice ahead of the guideline — tenecteplase adoption, large-core thrombectomy after SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT, RESCUE-Japan LIMIT and TENSION — into a single reference for systems of care.

**Practical implications.** Note what the evidence does NOT support extending: three randomised trials in 2025 (ESCAPE-MeVO, DISTAL, DISCOUNT) were negative for medium and distal vessel occlusion, so thrombectomy eligibility expands to large cores, not to small distal vessels. Tenecteplase's single bolus is the change with the biggest operational effect, because it removes an infusion pump from an interfacility transfer.

[Stroke - AHA/ASA guideline for the early management of acute ischaemic stroke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=guideline+early+management+acute+ischemic+stroke+AHA+ASA+Stroke+tenecteplase+thrombectomy)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific 2026 guideline document was located and the url is a PubMed title search; the individual components (tenecteplase preference, large-core thrombectomy) are well supported independently. TWO CLAIMS CORRECTED. The submission said the guideline expands eligibility 'for large core AND DISTAL VESSEL OCCLUSIONS' — the 2025 MeVO trials were negative, so that direction is not supported and the entry now says so. And 'incorporating mobile stroke units' plus 'MANDATES direct transport to endovascular-capable centers' and 'formalizes intensive blood pressure lowering' could not be confirmed; mandate language was removed.

### 7. Consensus Practice Guidelines on Sacroiliac Joint Complex Pain

*Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A multispecialty international working group consensus covering diagnostic block protocols, radiofrequency ablation criteria, and selection parameters for minimally invasive sacroiliac joint fusion.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Sacroiliac joint pain has been diagnosed inconsistently and operated on variably, and the diagnostic block threshold is the crux — an objective criterion for who has SI joint pain at all, before anyone considers fusion.

**Practical implications.** A diagnostic block giving at least 50% relief is the conventional threshold before proceeding to ablation or fusion. The value to a spine surgeon is defensive as much as clinical: documented block response is what distinguishes appropriate selection from operating on axial low back pain of uncertain origin, which is where SI fusion earned its poor reputation.

[Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine - consensus practice guidelines on sacroiliac joint complex pain](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=consensus+practice+guidelines+sacroiliac+joint+pain+multispecialty+international+working+group)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** A multispecialty consensus on sacroiliac joint pain is consistent with recent Regional Anesthesia & Pain Medicine output, but the specific document was not located and the url is a PubMed title search. The 50% block-relief threshold stated in the submission is the conventional criterion in this literature and is retained as such rather than attributed to this document specifically.

### 8. Ventral versus Dorsal Surgery for Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy (CSM-S)

*JAMA · 2024*

**The breakthrough.** A randomised trial comparing anterior (ventral) with posterior (dorsal) surgical approaches for cervical spondylotic myelopathy found NO significant difference in 1-year patient-reported physical function between the two.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Removes approach dogma. If neither approach is superior on patient-reported function, the choice properly rests on anatomy and alignment — sagittal alignment, number of compressive levels, whether cervical lordosis is preserved, and the location of the compression.

**Practical implications.** In a patient with preserved lordosis and multilevel posterior-predominant compression, posterior laminoplasty or laminectomy with fusion is a defensible choice over multilevel anterior fusion, with less instrumented fusion and fewer approach-related risks such as dysphagia and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury. Kyphosis or focal anterior compression still argues for a ventral approach.

[JAMA - ventral versus dorsal spinal surgery in cervical spondylotic myelopathy](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2817403)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year.** Real; JAMA, 2024 rather than 2026, and the null result on 1-year patient-reported physical function is correctly reported in the submission. Added the specific approach-related risks that distinguish the two operations, since 'reducing instrumented fusion costs, surgical risk, and recovery times' does not say what the risks actually are — and dysphagia and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury are the ones that drive the decision.
