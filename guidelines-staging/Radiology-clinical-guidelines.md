### 1. Abbreviated Breast MRI versus Digital Breast Tomosynthesis in Dense Breasts (ECOG-ACRIN EA1141)

*JAMA · February 2020*

**The breakthrough.** 1,444 evaluable average-risk women aged 40 to 75 with heterogeneously or extremely dense breasts underwent both abbreviated breast MRI and digital breast tomosynthesis at 47 US sites and one German site between December 2016 and November 2017. Abbreviated MRI detected all 17 invasive cancers; tomosynthesis detected 7 of 17. The invasive cancer detection rate was 11.8 per 1,000 women (95% CI 7.4 to 18.8) with abbreviated MRI against 4.8 per 1,000 (2.4 to 10.0) with tomosynthesis.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A within-patient comparison, so each woman served as her own control — which is why a 1,444-patient study can produce a result this clear. Detecting 17 of 17 against 7 of 17 is not a marginal difference.

**Practical implications.** Supports abbreviated MRI as supplemental screening in dense breasts where access allows. Two limits belong with it: the trial was not powered for and did not report mortality or interval-cancer outcomes, so the benefit demonstrated is detection, not lives saved; and MRI's additional detection comes with additional biopsies for findings that are not cancer, which is the trade a woman is actually being asked to make. Note also that heterogeneously dense breasts were included, not only extremely dense.

[JAMA — comparison of abbreviated breast MRI versus digital breast tomosynthesis for breast cancer detection among women with dense breasts undergoing screening](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2761645)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — sample size wrong by sevenfold and the date by five years.** The trial is real and the direction is right: ECOG-ACRIN EA1141, JAMA, PMID 32096852, abbreviated breast MRI detecting significantly more invasive cancers than digital breast tomosynthesis in women with dense breasts. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) SAMPLE SIZE: the submission describes 'a 10,000-patient prospective study'; the trial had 1,444 evaluable women. (2) DATE: published February 2020, not 2025. (3) POPULATION: the submission says 'extremely dense breast tissue'; the trial included heterogeneously dense as well, which materially widens who it applies to. The detection figures have been added — 17 of 17 versus 7 of 17, and 11.8 versus 4.8 per 1,000 — since they are more persuasive than the submission's general claim. Also added that the trial did not report mortality or interval-cancer outcomes and that additional detection brings additional benign biopsies, neither of which the submission mentions. The claim that ACR supplemental screening guidelines now prioritise fast MRI over secondary mammographic modalities was not verified.

### 2. Endovascular Thrombectomy for Large Ischaemic Core Stroke (SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 2023*

**The breakthrough.** SELECT2 and ANGEL-ASPECT randomised patients with large-core ischaemic stroke — the group excluded from earlier thrombectomy trials on the reasoning that infarcted tissue cannot be salvaged and reperfusion risks haemorrhage — and both found better functional outcomes with thrombectomy than with medical management alone.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Overturned an exclusion criterion rather than extending an indication. Patients with ASPECTS in the 3-to-5 range were previously routed to supportive care as a matter of course.

**Practical implications.** Refer for thrombectomy assessment rather than excluding on core size alone. Two things to keep in the discussion: the benefit is a shift in the distribution of disability, not a return to normal — many treated patients remain dependent, and the honest framing is a better chance of an outcome the patient would accept; and symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage is more frequent in this population than in small-core thrombectomy. The trials were published in 2023, not 2025, so this is established practice rather than emerging.

[NEJM — trial of endovascular thrombectomy for large ischemic strokes (SELECT2)](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2214403)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — date, and the magnitude of benefit.** Verified in substance: SELECT2 and ANGEL-ASPECT both published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2023, both showing benefit from endovascular thrombectomy in large-core ischaemic stroke, and both driving the expansion of eligibility in stroke guidance. THREE CORRECTIONS. (1) DATE: 2023, not 2025 — this is now standard practice, and presenting it as a 2025 development misstates where the field is. (2) The submission says thrombectomy 'yields significant functional independence'; the benefit is a shift in the modified Rankin distribution, with many treated patients still dependent, and overstating it sets up a conversation with a family that the outcome will not support. (3) ADDED: the higher rate of symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage in the large-core population, which the submission omits entirely while describing the alternative as 'comfort or palliative pathways'.

### 3. Photon-Counting CT for Coronary Plaque Characterisation

*Radiology and cardiovascular imaging literature · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Photon-counting detector CT counts individual photons with energy discrimination rather than integrating charge, which improves spatial resolution and reduces blooming from dense calcium — the artefact that makes a calcified coronary segment uninterpretable on conventional CT.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Calcium blooming is the specific technical reason coronary CT sends patients to invasive angiography. Reducing it addresses the failure mode rather than the modality.

**Practical implications.** The clinical promise is fewer non-diagnostic studies in heavily calcified vessels and therefore fewer invasive catheterisations for anatomy that could have been read non-invasively. Two caveats: photon-counting scanners are still concentrated in a small number of centres, so the finding does not yet change what most patients can access; and better plaque characterisation only changes outcomes if it changes management, which for soft plaque means statin intensification — a decision usually reachable from risk factors and calcium score without it. I could not verify the specific multicentre trial or the claim that SCCT guidelines now establish photon-counting CT as the definitive standard for equivocal calcification.

[Photon-counting CT in cardiovascular imaging](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=photon-counting+detector+CT+coronary+plaque+calcium+blooming+spatial+resolution)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — physics accurate, guideline claim not confirmed.** The technology and its advantage over energy-integrating detectors are accurately described, and reduced calcium blooming with improved spatial resolution is the established rationale for photon-counting CT in coronary imaging. I did not verify the specific multicentre Radiology trial, nor the claim that SCCT guidelines have been updated to establish photon-counting CT as 'the definitive non-invasive imaging standard for equivocal coronary calcifications' — that is a specific guideline assertion and no supporting document was found. Also softened 'dramatically decreases false-positive coronary artery disease diagnoses, sparing thousands of patients unnecessary invasive cardiac angiograms', which is a modelled implication rather than a measured outcome. ADDED: limited scanner availability, and the point that improved characterisation only helps if it changes management — for soft plaque, statin intensification is usually reachable without it.

### 4. PSMA-PET at Biochemical Recurrence and 177Lu-PSMA Theranostics

*Nuclear medicine literature · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** PSMA-PET detects prostate cancer recurrence at PSA levels well below the threshold at which CT and bone scan become informative, and the same target supports 177Lu-PSMA radioligand therapy — imaging and treatment against one molecule.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The theranostic pairing is the conceptual advance: the scan that finds the disease identifies the patients whose disease can be treated by the therapeutic version of the same ligand.

**Practical implications.** PSMA-PET at biochemical recurrence changes management by finding oligometastatic disease that conventional imaging misses — which is a genuine benefit and also a source of stage migration, since patients reclassified as metastatic by a more sensitive scan may be treated more intensively without evidence that doing so extends life. Hold those two together. I could not verify the specific trial the submission describes, nor the claim that SNMMI and NCCN guidelines recommend PSMA-PET restaging at PSA as low as 0.2 ng/mL, nor that early-line 177Lu-PSMA 'eradicates' oligometastatic lesions — eradication is not an established endpoint of radioligand therapy.

[PSMA-PET and 177Lu-PSMA theranostics in prostate cancer](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=PSMA+PET+biochemical+recurrence+detection+rate+low+PSA+177Lu-PSMA+theranostics)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — one claim goes beyond any endpoint.** PSMA-PET's superior detection at low PSA and the theranostic pairing with 177Lu-PSMA are both well established, so the direction is sound. I did not verify the specific multicentre trial, the journal, or the claim that SNMMI and NCCN guidelines recommend restaging at PSA as low as 0.2 ng/mL. ONE SUBSTANTIVE CORRECTION: the submission says this combination 'detects and ERADICATES oligometastatic prostate lesions before standard CT/bone scans can visualize them'. Eradication is not an endpoint any radioligand trial has demonstrated; the measured endpoints are PSA response, progression-free survival and overall survival. ADDED: stage migration, which the submission does not mention — a more sensitive scan reclassifies patients as metastatic and can lead to more intensive treatment without evidence of benefit, and that is the standing critique of PSMA-PET-driven management.

### 5. Yttrium-90 Radioembolisation Combined with Immune Checkpoint Blockade in Unresectable HCC

*Interventional oncology literature · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Transarterial radioembolisation delivers yttrium-90 microspheres into the hepatic arterial supply of a tumour. Combining it with immune checkpoint blockade tests whether local radiation can enhance systemic immune response in hepatocellular carcinoma, where checkpoint inhibition is now first-line systemic therapy.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reframes radioembolisation from a salvage or bridging procedure into a partner for systemic therapy, which is a substantial change in where it sits in the treatment algorithm.

**Practical implications.** The realistic goal is downstaging toward resection or transplantation in a patient who is currently outside criteria, and that requires the interventional radiologist, hepatologist and transplant surgeon deciding together before treatment rather than after. Two cautions: hepatic function is the constraint in HCC, and adding radiation to the liver in a cirrhotic patient risks decompensation that closes off transplantation; and combining local radiation with checkpoint blockade raises immune-related hepatitis, which is hard to distinguish from progression or radiation effect. I could not verify the trial or its survival claim.

[Transarterial radioembolisation with immune checkpoint blockade in hepatocellular carcinoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=yttrium-90+transarterial+radioembolization+immune+checkpoint+inhibitor+hepatocellular+carcinoma+trial)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — 'unprecedented survival' not asserted.** The combination is a real and active area: Y-90 radioembolisation is established in HCC and checkpoint blockade is first-line systemic therapy, so testing them together is a genuine trial concept. I did not verify the Lancet Oncology publication the submission cites or its outcomes, and the claim of 'unprecedented overall survival rates' is not asserted — nor is the claim that SIR and EASL algorithms have been modified to position radioembolisation as an upfront partner to immunotherapy. ADDED, because the submission presents only upside: in a cirrhotic patient, hepatic decompensation after liver-directed radiation can remove transplant eligibility altogether, which inverts the stated goal; and immune-related hepatitis on combination therapy is genuinely difficult to distinguish from progression or radiation change.

### 6. AI Triage for Intracranial Haemorrhage and Pulmonary Embolism

*Radiology: Artificial Intelligence and real-world validation studies · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Triage algorithms analyse non-contrast head CT and CT pulmonary angiography as studies arrive and reorder the reading worklist, moving suspected intracranial haemorrhage or pulmonary embolism to the front rather than leaving them in chronological order.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The intervention is on queue position, not on diagnosis. In a large hospital where a critical study can sit unread behind routine work, reordering the list is where the time is actually saved.

**Practical implications.** Understand what the tool is regulated to do: these are triage and notification devices, not diagnostic ones — they flag a study for earlier human reading and are not permitted to render the diagnosis. Two consequences follow. False negatives do not raise an alert, so a normal-looking worklist must never be read as reassurance, and a study can still be positive after the algorithm is silent. And measured time-to-treatment savings depend entirely on someone being available to act on the alert; the same software in a department with no spare capacity saves nothing. I could not verify the specific studies or the 40-minute figure.

[Radiology: Artificial Intelligence — real-world impact of AI triage](https://pubs.rsna.org/journal/ai)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — 'autonomous' is the wrong word.** AI triage for intracranial haemorrhage and pulmonary embolism is real, FDA-cleared and widely deployed, and time-to-treatment reductions have been reported in real-world studies — so the direction is sound. I did not verify the specific multi-hospital validation studies or the 'over 40 minutes' figure, and the claim that ACR and ACEP workflow guidelines now integrate automated prioritisation was not confirmed. ONE CORRECTION OF SUBSTANCE: the submission's title calls these 'Autonomous AI Triage'. They are cleared as triage and notification devices, explicitly not autonomous and not diagnostic — they change reading order and are not permitted to make the call. That distinction is what makes the false-negative point critical: an unflagged worklist is not a negative result, and the entry now says so. Added that measured time savings depend on someone being free to act on the alert.

### 7. 68Ga-FAPI PET/CT in Tumours with Low FDG Avidity

*Nuclear medicine literature · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** FAPI tracers bind fibroblast activation protein on cancer-associated fibroblasts in tumour stroma rather than tracking glucose metabolism, giving a different contrast mechanism from FDG. The clearest application is in tumours where FDG performs poorly — pancreatic, gastric and biliary cancers, and disease adjacent to organs with high physiological glucose uptake.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Complementary rather than competing. FAPI is informative precisely where FDG is not, which is a more useful property than being better everywhere.

**Practical implications.** The likely value is detecting peritoneal and small-volume disease that changes a decision to operate. The specificity caveat is important: fibroblast activation protein is expressed in fibrosis, inflammation and healing tissue, so FAPI uptake is not tumour-specific and post-treatment or inflammatory change can mimic malignancy. Availability is also limited, as tracer production is not widespread. I could not verify the pan-cancer trial the submission cites or the claim that SNMMI consensus has adopted FAPI-PET for staging gastrointestinal malignancy.

[68Ga-FAPI PET/CT in oncological imaging](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=68Ga-FAPI+PET+CT+versus+FDG+pancreatic+gastric+biliary+cancer+staging)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — specificity limit added.** FAPI PET is real and the comparison with FDG in low-avidity gastrointestinal tumours is a genuine and active research area, so the submission's direction holds. I did not verify the specific pan-cancer diagnostic trial in the Journal of Nuclear Medicine, nor the claim that SNMMI diagnostic consensus has adopted FAPI-PET for staging these malignancies. ADDED, because it is the main limitation and the submission omits it entirely: fibroblast activation protein is expressed in fibrosis, inflammation and healing tissue, so FAPI uptake is not tumour-specific and inflammatory or post-treatment change can be mistaken for malignancy. The submission's claim that it 'eliminates diagnostic blind spots' and avoids 'futile exploratory laparotomies' is softened accordingly — a tracer with imperfect specificity can also cause a resectable patient to be denied surgery.

### 8. MR-Guided Focused Ultrasound Thalamotomy for Essential Tremor

*Long-term follow-up of pivotal trials · 2016 pivotal trial; long-term follow-up since*

**The breakthrough.** MR-guided focused ultrasound creates a thermal lesion in the ventral intermediate thalamic nucleus through the intact skull, guided by real-time MR thermometry, with no incision or implanted hardware. Long-term follow-up of the pivotal trial cohort addresses whether tremor suppression persists over years.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Durability is the substantive question for a lesioning procedure, since a lesion cannot be adjusted afterwards. Sustained benefit at five years is what would put it alongside deep brain stimulation rather than behind it.

**Practical implications.** The trade-off against deep brain stimulation is the one to explain: focused ultrasound is incisionless and single-session, but the lesion is permanent and unmodifiable, whereas DBS is adjustable and reversible and can be done bilaterally with an established safety profile — bilateral lesioning carries higher risk of speech and gait effects. Adverse effects of thalamotomy include paraesthesia, gait unsteadiness and dysarthria, some persistent. Skull density ratio determines whether a patient is even treatable. I could not verify the specific five-year data or the claim that AAN guidelines now designate focused ultrasound an equal first-line alternative to DBS.

[MR-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy for essential tremor](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1600159)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — comparison with DBS overstated.** MRgFUS thalamotomy is real, FDA-approved for essential tremor, and the pivotal randomised trial is published in the New England Journal of Medicine, with long-term follow-up reports in the literature — so the substance is sound. I did not verify the specific five-year multicentre dataset, nor the claim that AAN and CNS guidelines designate it 'an equal first-line interventional alternative' to DBS. THE CORRECTION IS THE COMPARISON. The submission presents it as strictly superior — 'completely eliminates the need for open craniotomy, skull drilling, or implanted brain hardware'. It is incisionless, but the lesion is permanent and unadjustable where DBS is reversible and titratable and can be performed bilaterally; bilateral lesioning carries greater risk of speech and gait complications. Added the recognised adverse effects and the skull density ratio constraint, which determines candidacy and which the submission does not mention. Also note the submission's inclusion of Parkinson's disease in the title: the indications and evidence differ from essential tremor and are not asserted here.

### 9. Genicular Artery Embolisation for Knee Osteoarthritis

*Interventional radiology literature · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Genicular artery embolisation targets the abnormal hypervascular synovial tissue associated with osteoarthritic knee pain, occluding the feeding branches through a percutaneous catheter as an outpatient procedure.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Offers something for a large and awkward group: patients with painful knee osteoarthritis who are not candidates for or do not want arthroplasty, where the alternative is analgesia and injections.

**Practical implications.** The design question is everything here, because knee osteoarthritis pain responds strongly to placebo and to any invasive procedure — which is why a sham-controlled trial is the only informative kind, and why results from uncontrolled series should be discounted. Note also that embolisation does not alter cartilage or joint structure; it targets pain, so it delays arthroplasty rather than substituting for it. Non-target embolisation causing skin or bone ischaemia is the procedural risk. I could not verify the sham-controlled randomised trial the submission cites or its results.

[Genicular artery embolisation for knee osteoarthritis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=genicular+artery+embolization+knee+osteoarthritis+randomized+sham+controlled+trial)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — the sham-control claim is the load-bearing one.** Genicular artery embolisation is a real procedure with a growing evidence base and genuine interest as an option for patients who are poor arthroplasty candidates. I did not verify the 'landmark randomized sham-controlled trial published in Radiology' the submission describes, and that is the claim the whole entry rests on: knee osteoarthritis pain is unusually placebo-responsive and any invasive procedure produces a large non-specific effect, so a sham-controlled result would be decisive while an uncontrolled one would be close to uninformative. Written to make that distinction explicit rather than asserting the result. ADDED: that embolisation targets pain without altering joint structure — the submission's 'significantly delaying or replacing the need for major joint replacement surgery' implies disease modification that the mechanism does not support — and non-target embolisation as the procedural risk.

### 10. Multiparametric MRI for Non-Invasive Staging of Metabolic Liver Disease

*Hepatology and radiology literature · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Multiparametric liver MRI measures corrected T1 as a marker of inflammation and fibrosis alongside proton density fat fraction and iron content, aiming to characterise metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis without biopsy and, crucially, repeatably.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Repeatability is the real advance. Liver biopsy is invasive, samples a tiny fraction of the organ and cannot be performed serially, which makes it a poor instrument for monitoring response to an anti-fibrotic drug over years.

**Practical implications.** The strongest use is longitudinal monitoring in a patient already diagnosed, not initial diagnosis — a scan that can be repeated safely every six months answers a question biopsy cannot. Keep the staging limits in mind: non-invasive markers perform best at distinguishing no or minimal fibrosis from advanced fibrosis and least well in the intermediate range where treatment decisions are hardest, and MRI-based measures are affected by iron loading and severe steatosis. I could not verify the specific validation trial, and the journal named in the submission — 'Gastroenterology & Radiology' — does not exist as a single publication.

[Multiparametric MRI in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=multiparametric+MRI+cT1+liver+fibrosis+inflammation+MASH+non-invasive+validation)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — the named journal does not exist.** Multiparametric liver MRI including corrected T1, fat fraction and iron quantification is real, commercially available and under active validation in metabolic liver disease, so the substance is credible. TWO PROBLEMS WITH THE CITATION. The submission names the journal as 'Gastroenterology & Radiology', which is not a publication — Gastroenterology and Radiology are two separate journals, and the conflation suggests the citation was not checked. And I could not verify the prospective validation trial or the claim that AASLD guidelines have adopted this as a non-invasive surrogate biomarker for monitoring MASH treatment response. ADDED: the performance limitation that matters clinically — non-invasive markers discriminate well at the extremes of fibrosis and poorly in the intermediate range where decisions are hardest — and the point that the strongest case is serial monitoring rather than initial diagnosis, which is a narrower claim than 'replaces painful, invasive needle liver biopsies'.
