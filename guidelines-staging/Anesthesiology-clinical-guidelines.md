## 2025

10 entries · **10 with a citation problem — see the checks below**

### 1. Multi-Society Clinical Practice Guidance on Perioperative Management of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

*Multisociety consensus (ASA, ASMBS, ASRA, ISPCOP, SAGES) · December 2024*

**The breakthrough.** Replaced rigid, time-based holding of Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists — such as withholding weekly doses for 7 days — with dynamic risk stratification based on GI symptom screening and point-of-care gastric ultrasound (POCUS), plus shared decision-making about proceeding.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Adopted across five societies, establishing a common algorithm for evaluating delayed gastric emptying and mitigating aspiration risk rather than a single hold interval.

**Practical implications.** Eliminates unnecessary cancellations for asymptomatic patients on long-acting GLP-1 RAs, and brings bedside gastric ultrasound into the preoperative assessment when the holding interval cannot be verified.

[Multi-society guidance on perioperative GLP-1 receptor agonists](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11666732/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Was titled and dated as a 2025 ASA guideline. It is a five-society clinical practice guidance released December 2024. Clinical content was accurate and is unchanged; the title, attribution and date now match the document.

### 2. Long-Term Survival after Propofol or Sevoflurane Anaesthesia for Major Cancer Surgery: Follow-up of a Multicentre Randomised Trial

*British Journal of Anaesthesia · 2023*

**The breakthrough.** Long-term follow-up of a multicentre randomised trial in older patients undergoing major cancer surgery found no significant difference in survival between propofol-based total intravenous anaesthesia and sevoflurane-based volatile anaesthesia.

**Clinical guideline impact.** No guideline recommends TIVA for oncologic benefit. The two trials designed to settle the question definitively — GAS TIVA (22 sites, non-small-cell lung cancer) and VAPOR-C (lung and colorectal, 2x2 factorial with lidocaine) — are still reporting, so the honest position is equipoise rather than a closed question.

**Practical implications.** Choose the maintenance technique on hemodynamics, PONV risk and drug availability. The theoretical immunomodulatory argument for TIVA has no outcome data behind it.

[British Journal of Anaesthesia - propofol vs sevoflurane, long-term survival](https://www.bjanaesthesia.org/article/S0007-0912(23)00032-6/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ replaced.** The original cited a 2025 Lancet trial providing 'definitive' 3-year recurrence-free survival data. No such trial exists, and it claimed guidelines had been changed on the strength of it. Replaced with the real randomised evidence that does exist, and the practice conclusion — technique choice is not an oncologic decision — is preserved and now correctly sourced.

### 3. Individualized Perioperative Blood Pressure Management in Patients Undergoing Major Abdominal Surgery: The IMPROVE-multi Randomized Clinical Trial

*JAMA · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A negative trial, and an important one. Individualized MAP targets set from each patient's preoperative mean nighttime MAP did NOT reduce the composite of acute kidney injury, myocardial injury, nonfatal cardiac arrest or death within 7 postoperative days, compared with routine management at a MAP target of 65 mmHg or higher, in patients at high risk of postoperative complications.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Supports keeping the population-based MAP ≥ 65 mmHg threshold as the default rather than moving to personalized relative targets. The appeal of individualizing pressure to a patient's own baseline is intuitive, and this trial is the reason it has not become a standard.

**Practical implications.** Treat MAP below 65 mmHg promptly, and do not invest in baseline-relative targets or predictive hemodynamic software on the expectation of an organ-protection benefit — that benefit has not been demonstrated in a randomized trial.

[JAMA - IMPROVE-multi individualized blood pressure trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41076588/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ replaced — original stated the opposite result.** The original cited an 'HPI-SMART Trial' that does not exist, said it showed individualized relative MAP targets significantly reduce AKI and myocardial injury, and said international hemodynamic standards were rewritten to recommend targeting within 10-15% of baseline. IMPROVE-multi is the real 2025 JAMA trial on this exact question and found no benefit. The entry now states the actual finding.

### 4. ASA Practice Guideline for Monitoring and Antagonism of Neuromuscular Blockade

*Anesthesiology (ASA practice guideline) · 2023*

**The breakthrough.** Established quantitative neuromuscular monitoring at the adductor pollicis, confirming a train-of-four ratio ≥ 0.9 before extubation, and held that subjective visual or tactile TOF assessment cannot rule out residual paralysis.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Recommends sugammadex over neostigmine at deep, moderate and shallow depths of rocuronium-induced block, with neostigmine a reasonable alternative at minimal depth. Quantitative monitoring applies while giving the antagonist, not only before extubation.

**Practical implications.** Facilities are fitting ORs with quantitative acceleromyography or electromyography and documenting a numerical TOF ratio before tracheal extubation. Residual block remains a live safety problem, which is what the guideline exists to address.

[ASA - Monitoring and Antagonism of Neuromuscular Blockade](https://www.asahq.org/standards-and-practice-parameters/practice-guideline-monitoring-and-antagonism-of-neuromuscular-blockade)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Was presented as a new 2025 guideline. It is the ASA guideline published in 2023. Every clinical claim checked out and is unchanged; the date and title now match. Also softened 'mandated' to what the document does — it recommends.

### 5. Low versus High Intraoperative Fraction of Inspired Oxygen and Postoperative Organ Complications: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials

*Anesthesiology and Perioperative Science · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Twenty randomized trials, 5,793 patients. Low intraoperative FiO₂ produced less atelectasis (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.63-0.97) but MORE acute kidney injury (RR 1.64, 95% CI 1.15-2.34). The tradeoff runs in both directions, which is why no single target has been adopted.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Separately, a meta-analysis of 30 studies and 18,055 patients found high FiO₂ (mainly 80%) did not reduce surgical site infection versus low FiO₂ (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.79-1.03). That removes infection prevention as a justification for routine hyperoxia, but it does not establish a restrictive target in its place.

**Practical implications.** Titrate to adequate oxygenation rather than leaving the blender at a high default, and understand what is being traded — atelectasis against kidney injury. No society has adopted a 92-96% intraoperative SpO₂ target, and the evidence does not yet support one.

[Systematic review - low vs high intraoperative FiO₂](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44254-025-00123-4)

> **Citation check — ⚠ replaced — original overstated a nonexistent trial.** The original cited a 2025 NEJM pragmatic trial proving intraoperative hyperoxia increases pulmonary complications, and said major societies had rewritten oxygen guidelines to a 92-96% SpO₂ target. No such trial was found and no such target has been adopted. The 'high FiO₂ does not reduce SSI' half was correct and is retained with its real source; the rest now reports what the evidence actually shows, including that low FiO₂ carries its own harm signal.

### 6. Elective Peri-operative Management of Adults Taking GLP-1 Receptor Agonists, GIP Agonists and SGLT2 Inhibitors: A Multidisciplinary Consensus Statement

*Anaesthesia · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Set a 3-day preoperative hold for sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors before elective surgery, and a pathway for evaluating ketosis — including blood beta-hydroxybutyrate — when the hold was not achieved.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A multidisciplinary consensus spanning anaesthesia, endocrinology, surgery and pharmacy, giving one protocol for euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (eDKA) prevention across specialties. The consensus states plainly that there is no Class 1 evidence in this area, so it rests on expert agreement rather than trial data.

**Practical implications.** Gives a structured approach for urgent and emergent procedures where the drug could not be held, including metabolic panel and blood ketone screening. Note that eDKA presents with a normal or near-normal glucose, so a reassuring fingerstick does not exclude it.

[Anaesthesia - peri-operative GLP-1, GIP and SGLT2 inhibitor consensus](https://associationofanaesthetists-publications.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/anae.16541)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Two errors fixed. The original said 'Endorsed by Circulation' — it was published in Anaesthesia and not endorsed by Circulation. And the hold is 3 days, not '3-4 days'. Added the consensus's own statement that no Class 1 evidence exists, since a resident should know this is expert opinion.

### 7. Restrictive versus Liberal Red-Cell Transfusion in Cardiac Surgery (TRICS III)

*NEJM · 2017 (6-month outcomes 2018)*

**The breakthrough.** More than 5,000 adults at moderate-to-high predicted risk of death undergoing on-pump cardiac surgery. A restrictive threshold (transfuse below 7.5 g/dL from induction) was noninferior to a liberal one (below 9.5 g/dL in the OR, 8.5 g/dL on the ward) for the composite of death, myocardial infarction, stroke or new dialysis: 11.4% versus 12.5%, within the prespecified 3% noninferiority margin. Transfusion rates were 52.3% versus 72.6%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Underpins the restrictive thresholds in current Patient Blood Management guidance for cardiac surgery, and the finding held at 6 months.

**Practical implications.** Substantially reduces blood product use and the exposure that comes with it (TRALI, TACO), and eases blood bank demand, without a penalty in major morbidity or mortality.

[NEJM - TRICS III cardiac surgery transfusion trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1711818)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — wrong trial name and year.** The original called this the 'TITRE-3 Trial' and dated it 2025 with a '90-day MACCE' endpoint. No trial by that name exists; the description is TRICS III, NEJM 2017. The clinical teaching was correct throughout and is unchanged — only the citation, year and endpoint description are fixed, with the trial's actual numbers added. (TITRe2 is a separate, earlier UK trial with a different design.)

### 8. Spinal versus General Anaesthesia for Hip Fracture Surgery: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Using a Consensus Core Outcome Set (REGAIN and RAGA)

*British Journal of Anaesthesia · 2022 (REGAIN, NEJM 2021; RAGA, JAMA 2022)*

**The breakthrough.** Two large randomized trials settled this. REGAIN randomized 1,600 previously ambulatory patients aged 50+ across 46 US and Canadian hospitals: delirium did not differ by technique — 33.9% versus 37.8% in those with cognitive impairment, 16.2% versus 16.0% in those without — nor did ambulation or survival at 60 days. RAGA randomized 950 patients aged 65+ across 9 Chinese teaching hospitals and likewise found no difference in delirium.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Regional anaesthesia is no longer recommended for neurocognitive protection in hip fracture surgery. Geriatric perioperative guidance has moved the emphasis to avoiding excessive anaesthetic depth and burst suppression, which is modifiable, rather than to the choice of technique, which is not the lever it was believed to be.

**Practical implications.** Choose spinal or general on the patient's anatomy, hemodynamic tolerance and preference. Pain scores, length of stay and independent ambulation are also comparable, so there is no hidden cost to either choice.

[British Journal of Anaesthesia - spinal vs general for hip fracture, meta-analysis](https://www.bjanaesthesia.org/article/S0007-0912(22)00427-5/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — wrong trial name and year.** The original cited a 2025 'RACE-2 Trial' in JAMA Surgery with processed-EEG-guided GA as the comparator. No such trial exists. The conclusion is well supported, by REGAIN (NEJM 2021) and RAGA (JAMA 2022) and this BJA meta-analysis synthesising them with a consensus core outcome set. Teaching preserved, citations corrected, real numbers added.

### 9. 2024 AHA/ACC Multisociety Guideline for Perioperative Cardiovascular Management for Noncardiac Surgery

*Circulation / JACC · 2024*

**The breakthrough.** Set out when to measure cardiac troponin around noncardiac surgery and how to act on it, replacing the 2014 guideline. Preoperative high-sensitivity troponin identifies chronic myocardial injury as well as patients at increased perioperative risk.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Measuring cTn before surgery is reasonable in patients with known cardiovascular disease, or aged ≥ 65, or aged ≥ 45 with symptoms suggestive of CVD, undergoing elevated-risk noncardiac surgery. Endorsed by nine societies (AHA, ACC, ACS, ASNC, HRS, SCA, SCCT, SCMR, SVM).

**Practical implications.** Detects myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS) in patients who never report chest pain, which is most of them. Detection is what triggers cardiology evaluation and secondary prevention — though the guideline does not claim a specific mortality reduction from screening itself.

[JACC - 2024 perioperative cardiovascular management guideline](https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.06.013)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Three fixes. It is a 2024 full guideline, not a 2025 focused update. It is not joint with ESAIC — it is a nine-society US document. And the original claim that early detection 'measurably lowers 1-year mortality' is not something the guideline asserts, so it has been reworded to what detection actually does.

### 10. Viscoelastometric-Guided Early Fibrinogen Concentrate Replacement during Postpartum Haemorrhage (OBS2)

*British Journal of Anaesthesia · 2017*

**The breakthrough.** A negative double-blind randomized trial, and the reason early fibrinogen is not given empirically. Fibrinogen concentrate triggered by a FIBTEM A5 ≤ 15 mm did not reduce allogeneic transfusion: adjusted incidence rate ratio 0.72 (95% CI 0.3-1.7), P = 0.45. FIDEL likewise found no benefit from early systematic fibrinogen after vaginal delivery.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Guidance continues to reserve fibrinogen replacement for measured hypofibrinogenemia during ongoing bleeding, rather than giving it early on a viscoelastic trigger. Viscoelastic testing earns its place by identifying which patients are actually hypofibrinogenemic — not by prompting routine early replacement.

**Practical implications.** Use ROTEM or TEG to find true hypofibrinogenemia and treat that. The appeal of pre-emptive fibrinogen in obstetric hemorrhage is strong and has been tested twice; both trials were negative.

[British Journal of Anaesthesia - OBS2 fibrinogen in postpartum haemorrhage](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000709121753754X)

> **Citation check — ⚠ replaced — original stated the opposite result.** The original cited a 2025 Lancet multicentre trial proving VHA-guided fibrinogen reduces ICU admissions and blood product exposure, and said obstetric hemorrhage standards now strongly recommend it over plasma. No such trial exists. OBS2 is the real randomized trial of this intervention and it was negative, as was FIDEL. The entry now reports that, and keeps the genuinely useful role of viscoelastic testing.

## 2026

10 entries · **10 with a citation problem — see the checks below**

### 1. 2026 ASA Practice Guideline on Perioperative Pain Management Using Local and Regional Analgesia for Cardiothoracic Surgeries, Mastectomy, and Abdominal Surgeries

*Anesthesiology · January 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Procedure-specific recommendations for fascial plane blocks — TAP, erector spinae plane, serratus anterior and parasternal plane blocks — across cardiothoracic, mastectomy and abdominal surgery, replacing a one-technique-fits-all approach with guidance tied to the operation.

**Clinical guideline impact.** From the ASA Task Force on Perioperative Pain Management. For ADULTS the recommendation is CONDITIONAL, and covers minimally invasive cardiothoracic surgery and open hernia repair. The STRONG recommendation is for CHILDREN after open cardiac or thoracic surgery. The Task Force notes its own evidence base was limited by low methodologic quality, inconsistent outcome measurement and small single-centre samples.

**Practical implications.** Reduces 24-hour opioid consumption — up to roughly 60 mg oral morphine equivalents — and both rest and dynamic pain scores, which makes fascial plane blocks a reasonable default in opioid-sparing multimodal pathways. Treat the adult recommendation as conditional when weighing it against block time and expertise.

[Anesthesiology - 2026 ASA guideline on regional analgesia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41363869/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** The document is real and correctly cited. The original said the guideline elevated fascial plane blocks to 'Strong Recommendation' status for open surgeries in both adults and children. It is conditional for adults (and for minimally invasive cardiothoracic surgery plus open hernia repair, not open surgery generally); strong only for children after open cardiac or thoracic surgery. The guideline's own stated evidence limitations have been added.

### 2. Intervals for Oscillometric Arterial Pressure Monitoring during Noncardiac Surgery: The 2.5-Minute versus 5-Minute Randomized Clinical Trial

*Anesthesiology · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A negative trial. Cycling non-invasive oscillometric blood pressure every 2.5 minutes instead of every 5 did NOT reduce the time-weighted average of MAP below 65 mmHg. Both arms were nearly identical and both very low — almost certainly because 98% of the 264 patients were on norepinephrine infusions, which left little hypotension for faster measurement to catch.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Does not displace the conventional 5-minute interval. The result is best read as showing that measurement frequency is not the binding constraint when hypotension is already being treated actively; it does not establish that 2.5-minute cycling is useless in a population managed without vasopressor infusions.

**Practical implications.** No change to routine practice is warranted on this trial. Severe hypotension (MAP < 50 mmHg) was 5.3% versus 9.9%, P = 0.24 — a difference that looks appealing and is not statistically significant, in a single-centre trial of 264 patients. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a finding.

[Anesthesiology - 2.5-minute vs 5-minute blood pressure monitoring trial](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06314074)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — original stated the opposite result.** The trial is real (NCT06314074) and it was NEGATIVE. The original said it proved shorter intervals reduce hypotensive depth and duration, and told readers to shorten NIBP cycling to 2.5 minutes during induction and high-risk phases — the opposite of what the trial found, and a practice change based on a null result. Rewritten to the actual finding, with the norepinephrine confound that explains it.

### 3. Videolaryngoscopy versus Direct Laryngoscopy for Double-Lumen Tube Intubation: The DOLVI Multicentre Randomized Trial

*British Journal of Anaesthesia · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre randomized trial across four Spanish hospitals found videolaryngoscopy improved first-attempt success for double-lumen tube placement — 84.7% versus 76.9% — with better glottic visualisation, in patients requiring lung isolation for thoracic surgery.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Glottic visualisation, oesophageal intubation and tube malposition were secondary outcomes and all favoured videolaryngoscopy. An accompanying BJA editorial asks whether this makes videolaryngoscopy the new standard for double-lumen placement; guidelines have not yet been revised, so this is the direction of travel rather than a change already made.

**Practical implications.** Reduces procedural difficulty, loss of glottic view, oesophageal intubation and airway trauma with a tube that is large, stiff and awkward to place. A reasonable first choice where a videolaryngoscope is available.

[British Journal of Anaesthesia - DOLVI multicentre trial](https://www.bjanaesthesia.org/article/S0007-0912(26)00207-2/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Real and accurately summarised, including the first-attempt success figures. One softening: the original said the trial 'directly updates thoracic airway management guidelines'. It has not — that is the editorialist's question, not a guideline revision.

### 4. Management of Direct Oral Anticoagulants in Adult Patients Undergoing Cardiac Surgery: A Joint Consensus Statement by the SCA and STS

*Anesthesia & Analgesia / The Annals of Thoracic Surgery · April 2026*

**The breakthrough.** One agreed approach to direct oral anticoagulants in adult cardiac surgery — cessation timing, laboratory monitoring, bridging, reversal and postoperative resumption — for patients going onto cardiopulmonary bypass.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Published jointly by the Society of Cardiovascular Anesthesiologists and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons, appearing in both journals. Preoperative hold duration is keyed to renal function, with criteria distinguishing targeted reversal agents from prothrombin complex concentrates.

**Practical implications.** Aims to reduce chest tube bleeding, transfusion and emergency re-operation while avoiding needless delay of urgent cases. Those are the consensus's stated objectives — a consensus statement does not itself measure them.

[Anesthesia & Analgesia - SCA/STS joint DOAC consensus](https://journals.lww.com/anesthesia-analgesia/fulltext/2026/04000/management_of_direct_oral_anticoagulants_in_adult.10.aspx)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Real and correctly attributed. One softening: the original presented reduced bleeding, transfusion and re-operation as achieved outcomes. They are the consensus's goals; a consensus statement cannot demonstrate them.

### 5. Efficacy and Safety of Remimazolam Tosylate versus Propofol for Sedation of Postoperative Mechanically Ventilated Patients in Intensive Care Units: A Phase 3 Trial

*Anesthesiology · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre, randomized, single-blind, noninferiority phase 3 trial found remimazolam tosylate noninferior to propofol for SHORT-TERM sedation of postoperative mechanically ventilated ICU patients, and well tolerated. Mean terminal half-life 1.97 ± 1.62 h.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Adds a flumazenil-reversible, organ-independent option alongside propofol for postoperative ventilated patients. No society guideline has been revised to incorporate it — this is a single phase 3 trial, not a change in standards.

**Practical implications.** Companion trials report less hypotension with remimazolam, which is the practical draw in a patient who is already vasopressor-dependent. It also sidesteps propofol infusion syndrome and hypertriglyceridemia — but note the trial studied short-term sedation, so extended use is an extrapolation.

[Anesthesiology - remimazolam vs propofol phase 3 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855428/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Real (PMID 41855428, NCT06222294). Two fixes: the trial studied SHORT-TERM sedation, not 'prolonged postoperative mechanical ventilation' as the original title and practical implications both stated; and the original said it 'formally integrates remimazolam into PACU and surgical ICU guidelines', which has not happened.

### 6. Guidelines to the Practice of Anesthesia (Revised 2026 Edition)

*Canadian Journal of Anesthesia · January 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The Canadian Anesthesiologists' Society's annual revision of its basic standards of anesthesia care — the 49th edition, and the 18th consecutive year published in the Canadian Journal of Anesthesia.

**Clinical guideline impact.** CAS revises existing sections each year only where high-quality literature supports a change, and adds new sections as topics demand. It is the reference standard for anesthesia practice in Canada and is widely cited elsewhere.

**Practical implications.** Worth reading annually rather than assuming the previous edition still applies, since the changes are targeted rather than announced. Consult the document itself for the current monitoring requirements and any environmental sustainability provisions.

[Canadian Journal of Anesthesia - CAS Guidelines to the Practice of Anesthesia 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12630-026-03075-w)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — two specific claims removed as unverified.** The document is real (PMID 41820767). Removed: 'a comprehensive overhaul', which contradicts CAS's own description of targeted annual revision; a new requirement for continuous micro-stream capnography in all sedation locations including NORA; and formal institutional plans to phase out desflurane. Those last two could not be confirmed from the abstract, and the proxy blocks the full text from this container. If they are in the document, they are worth stating — but they should be checked against it first, not asserted.

### 7. Electroencephalographic Monitoring in the Recovery Room for Identification of Patients at Risk for Postoperative Delirium

*Anesthesiology · June 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Automated frontal EEG in the immediate post-anaesthesia recovery period detected persistent slow-wave activity marking a 'vulnerable brain' phenotype, which was associated with increased risk of postoperative delirium before any clinical signs appeared.

**Clinical guideline impact.** An association study, from Charité-Universitätsmedizin Berlin with Harvard and MIT collaborators. The authors propose that integrating recovery-room EEG into postoperative care may support risk stratification. No guideline has been changed.

**Practical implications.** If it holds up, it would let PACU teams start delirium precautions — reorientation, structured analgesia, avoiding anticholinergics — before transfer to the ward, in the patients most likely to need them. Promising, not yet established as a screening test.

[Anesthesiology - recovery-room EEG and delirium risk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41983562/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Real (PMID 41983562). Two softenings: 'accurately identifying patients at high risk' overstates a risk-association finding, and the original said the study 'refines perioperative brain health guidelines by recommending post-anesthesia EEG screening'. The authors propose it; no guideline recommends it.

### 8. Aspirin versus Clopidogrel for Chronic Maintenance Monotherapy after PCI: 10-Year Follow-up of the HOST-EXAM Trial

*The Lancet · March 2026*

**The breakthrough.** 5,438 patients in South Korea who had completed 6-18 months of DAPT after drug-eluting stent PCI without events, randomized to clopidogrel 75 mg or aspirin 100 mg and followed a median 10.5 years with 92.8% completeness. Clopidogrel was better for the primary composite — 25.4% versus 28.5%, HR 0.86 (95% CI 0.77-0.96), p = 0.0050 — and for both ischaemic and bleeding outcomes.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Lower ischaemic AND bleeding events with clopidogrel, which is unusual and is what makes the trial notable. All-cause mortality did NOT differ — worth holding onto, because 'superior' here does not mean a survival benefit. Presented at ACC 2026 with simultaneous Lancet publication.

**Practical implications.** Relevant preoperatively because more post-PCI patients presenting for elective noncardiac surgery will be on clopidogrel monotherapy rather than aspirin. Hold and restart timelines for chronic P2Y12 monotherapy follow from that, though the trial itself did not study the perioperative period.

[The Lancet - HOST-EXAM 10-year follow-up](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(26)00422-8/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Real and accurately summarised. Added the trial's numbers and, importantly, that all-cause mortality did not differ — the original's 'maintains superior reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events' implies a mortality benefit that was not found. Also flagged that the perioperative implications are extrapolation.

### 9. Expert Consensus Statement on the Perioperative Management of Adult Patients Undergoing High-Risk Thyroidectomy

*Anesthesia & Analgesia · June 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A consensus statement from the Society for Head and Neck Anesthesia covering intraoperative neural monitoring (IONM) protocols, vocal cord assessment, and emergency pathways for acute post-thyroidectomy haematoma.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Standardises endotracheal tube positioning for recurrent laryngeal nerve monitoring — tube position determines whether IONM works at all — and sets out bedside wound decompression algorithms for airway compromise.

**Practical implications.** Aims to reduce permanent vocal cord paralysis and speed response to post-thyroidectomy airway compromise on the ward. A post-thyroidectomy haematoma can obstruct the airway within minutes, and decompression cannot wait for return to theatre; a consensus statement sets the pathway but does not itself demonstrate a change in paralysis rates.

[Anesthesia & Analgesia - high-risk thyroidectomy perioperative consensus](https://doi.org/10.1213/ANE.0000000000008141)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Real (DOI 10.1213/ANE.0000000000008141, 10 June 2026). Two fixes: it is from the Society for Head and Neck Anesthesia, not 'multi-society'; and 'significantly reduces permanent vocal cord paralysis rates' is an intended benefit, not a measured outcome of a consensus statement.

### 10. Feasibility of a Multicomponent Protocol to Promote Dreaming during Surgical Anesthesia

*Anesthesiology · July 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A feasibility study of a five-part protocol: preinduction verbal priming about dreaming, propofol as the emergence anaesthetic, EEG-guided emergence, at least 10 minutes of minimised stimulation before emergence, and immediate postemergence interview. Of 452 patients interviewed, 69% reported dreaming — 93% among the 57 with full protocol adherence. 86% of dreams were positive and none very negative.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A feasibility and safety signal, not a standards change. The protocol was safe with no awareness events and no difference in recovery times or analgesic and antiemetic use. Dreamers reported better sleep quality (9.16 ± 1.57 versus 7.65 ± 2.86).

**Practical implications.** An unusually humane line of inquiry — what the patient experiences during and after anaesthesia, rather than only whether they were unconscious. Nothing here changes practice yet: the endpoint was dreaming, and larger trials would be needed to know whether it affects anything patients care about beyond sleep quality.

[Anesthesiology - protocol to promote dreaming during anesthesia](https://journals.lww.com/anesthesiology/fulltext/10.1097/aln.0000000000005968~feasibility-of-a-multicomponent-protocol-to-promote-dreaming)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — original credited it with outcomes it never measured.** The study is real but the original entry described it as an EEG-guided emergence bundle that markedly reduced emergence agitation and post-traumatic stress responses, and said processed EEG emergence tracking had entered post-anaesthesia recovery standards. It measured DREAMING. It did not measure agitation or PTSD, and no standard has changed. Rewritten to what the study actually did and found.
