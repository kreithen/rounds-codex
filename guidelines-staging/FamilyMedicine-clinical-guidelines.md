### 1. Blood-Based Cell-Free DNA Testing for Colorectal Cancer Screening: Where It Actually Stands

*NEJM (ECLIPSE); NCCN and ASGE positions · 2024-2025*

**The breakthrough.** An FDA-approved cell-free DNA blood test for colorectal cancer screening now exists, and the ECLIPSE validation study was published in NEJM. But the USPSTF has NOT added it: the current USPSTF colorectal cancer recommendation remains the 2021 statement, which lists colonoscopy, FIT, multitarget stool DNA and flexible sigmoidoscopy for adults 45 to 75, and does not include blood-based cfDNA.

**Clinical guideline impact.** In June 2025 the NCCN added blood-based testing to its guideline, but ONLY for average-risk people who cannot or will not use another screening method. The ASGE issued a position statement in August 2025. The reason for the caution is specific: cfDNA blood tests have poor sensitivity for ADVANCED ADENOMAS, so they detect cancer better than they prevent it.

**Practical implications.** Offer it as a last resort for a patient who has refused everything else — a completed imperfect test beats a declined good one. Do not present it as equivalent to colonoscopy or FIT. And counsel that a positive result still requires colonoscopy, while a negative result is less reassuring than a negative FIT.

[NEJM - cell-free DNA blood-based test for colorectal cancer screening (ECLIPSE)](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2304714)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REPLACED — the cited guideline does not exist.** There is no 2025 USPSTF final recommendation integrating blood-based cfDNA. The current USPSTF colorectal cancer statement is from 2021 and explicitly does NOT include blood-based tests, on grounds of insufficient evidence and poor advanced-adenoma detection. The original said the USPSTF 'updated primary care screening guidelines to include' cfDNA and that this 'establishes formal primary care screening pathways' — it would have family physicians offering a test as guideline-endorsed when it is not. Rewritten around what does exist (ECLIPSE, the NCCN's narrow 2025 addition, the ASGE position) and the adenoma-sensitivity limitation the original omitted.

### 2. Recombinant Zoster Vaccine and Reduced Risk of Dementia

*Nature Medicine; Nature Communications · 2024-2025*

**The breakthrough.** A natural-experiment study of more than 200,000 people in Nature Medicine (2024) found that within 6 years of vaccination, the recombinant zoster vaccine was associated with lower dementia risk than the LIVE vaccine — a 17% increase in diagnosis-free time, or 164 additional days lived without a dementia diagnosis among those eventually diagnosed. A subsequent Nature Communications cohort study reported a 51% lower dementia risk after two doses in adults 65 and over, which remained significant after accounting for healthy-vaccinee bias.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Multiple independent cohorts using Medicare and Kaiser Permanente data have found the same association, and a related signal has been reported for the AS01 adjuvant shared with the RSV vaccine. No immunization guideline has added a dementia indication — recombinant zoster vaccine is recommended for adults 50 and over (and immunocompromised adults 19 and over) on its shingles and postherpetic neuralgia merits.

**Practical implications.** Reasonable to mention when counselling a hesitant older adult, but describe it accurately: these are OBSERVATIONAL studies showing association, and the strongest one compared the recombinant vaccine against the live vaccine rather than against no vaccination. The shingles indication is the reason to vaccinate; this is a possible bonus, not an established neuroprotective effect.

[Nature Medicine - recombinant shingles vaccine and lower dementia risk](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-024-03201-5)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — numbers and comparator.** The finding is real and the literature is larger than the entry suggested, but the figure was wrong. The Nature Medicine 2024 study reported a 17% increase in diagnosis-free time over 6 YEARS versus the LIVE vaccine (164 additional days), not '20% lower risk over 7 years'; the Nature Communications cohort reported 51%. The comparator matters — the headline study was recombinant versus live zoster vaccine, not versus no vaccine. Also 'Directly influences adult immunization recommendations, adding neuroprotective benefits to the existing indication' has not happened, and these are observational designs.

### 3. Primary Care-Led Deprescribing of Long-Term Proton Pump Inhibitors

*BJGP Open · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Structured primary care deprescribing algorithms for adults on long-term proton pump inhibitor therapy without an ongoing indication, using a guided taper-and-stop approach.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Supports explicit step-down criteria — moving to on-demand dosing, an H2 receptor antagonist, or antacids — rather than indefinite continuation of a prescription that was often started for a self-limiting problem.

**Practical implications.** Worth building into annual medication reconciliation. Note two things when counselling: rebound acid hypersecretion after abrupt withdrawal is real and is the reason to taper rather than stop, and the observational associations with hypomagnesemia, C. difficile and fracture risk are confounded — they justify reviewing an unindicated PPI, not frightening a patient who needs one.

[BJGP Open - PPI deprescribing in primary care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=proton+pump+inhibitor+deprescribing+primary+care+BJGP+Open)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific BJGP Open publication matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search, and the 'over 65% discontinued' figure is therefore not asserted here. The clinical approach is well established in deprescribing guidance. One framing correction: the original presented hypomagnesemia, C. difficile and osteoporosis as established PPI harms — these come from observational data with substantial confounding, which is worth a resident knowing.

### 4. GLP-1 Receptor Agonists versus SGLT2 Inhibitors in Type 2 Diabetes: What the Indirect Evidence Shows

*Network meta-analyses across multiple journals · Ongoing*

**The breakthrough.** There are NO head-to-head randomized trials of GLP-1 receptor agonists against SGLT2 inhibitors. The comparison rests entirely on network meta-analyses and indirect comparison of placebo-controlled trials, plus large observational target-trial emulations.

**Clinical guideline impact.** On that indirect evidence, both classes reduce major adverse cardiovascular events versus placebo with no statistically significant difference between them. Where they diverge: SGLT2 inhibitors are more effective for heart failure hospitalization and composite renal outcomes, while GLP-1 receptor agonists appear more effective for MACE and glycemic control. Combination therapy shows additive benefit, and GLP-1 effects hold regardless of background SGLT2 use.

**Practical implications.** Choose on the dominant phenotype — heart failure or chronic kidney disease points to an SGLT2 inhibitor; obesity, stroke risk and glycemic burden point to a GLP-1 receptor agonist. Increasingly the answer is both. But present this as inference from indirect comparison, not as a settled head-to-head result, because it is not one.

[Circulation - cardiovascular, kidney and safety outcomes with GLP-1 RAs alone and with SGLT2 inhibitors](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.124.071689)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REPLACED — the cited trial does not exist.** There is no head-to-head pragmatic primary care trial of GLP-1 RAs versus SGLT2 inhibitors in NEJM; the literature states plainly that no head-to-head trials exist. The original's directional conclusions are also partly reversed: it said the classes are equivalent for cardiovascular protection with SGLT2 superior for renal outcomes, whereas the meta-analytic picture is that GLP-1 RAs look better for MACE and SGLT2 inhibitors better for heart failure AND renal outcomes. Rewritten to state the evidence type explicitly, since 'a randomized head-to-head trial showed' and 'network meta-analysis suggests' carry very different weight at the bedside.

### 5. Point-of-Care C-Reactive Protein Testing to Reduce Antibiotic Prescribing in Acute Respiratory Tract Infection

*Multiple trials; Cochrane review · Established evidence base*

**The breakthrough.** Rapid fingerstick quantitative CRP at the point of care reduces antibiotic prescribing for acute lower respiratory tract infection in primary care, established across several randomized trials and summarised in Cochrane reviews.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Underpins antimicrobial stewardship guidance in several European systems, where point-of-care CRP is standard in general practice. Uptake in US primary care remains limited.

**Practical implications.** A low CRP makes bacterial pneumonia unlikely and is genuinely useful for reassuring a patient who arrived expecting antibiotics — the test does the arguing. But treat it as one input, not a rule-out: CRP rises over the first 24-48 hours of illness, so an early presentation can give a low value in true bacterial infection, and clinical assessment still governs. Thresholds are usually framed as low, intermediate and high bands rather than a single cut-off.

[Point-of-care C-reactive protein testing in primary care respiratory infection](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=point-of-care+C-reactive+protein+antibiotic+prescribing+primary+care+respiratory+tract+infection+randomised)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, and one claim softened.** No specific BJGP Open multicentre observational study matching the submission was located; the url is a PubMed title search, and the 'nearly 40%' reduction figure is not asserted (published trials generally report smaller reductions). The approach itself is well evidenced. IMPORTANT SOFTENING: the original stated that 'CRP < 20 mg/L RULES OUT bacterial pneumonia'. It does not — early presentation gives a low CRP in genuine bacterial infection because CRP takes 24-48 hours to rise, and teaching it as a rule-out invites a missed pneumonia.

### 6. 2025 AHA/ACC Hypertension Guideline and the AAFP's Position on It

*Circulation / JACC; AAFP clinical guidance · August 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline sets a universal blood pressure goal of below 130/80 mmHg for adults, with treatment initiation guided by severity, comorbidity and the PREVENT risk score. The AAFP's own clinical guidance also uses a general goal of below 130/80, with caveats for institutional care, limited life expectancy and pregnancy.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The notable fact for family medicine is that the AAFP did NOT endorse the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline — nor did the American College of Physicians or the International Society of Hypertension. The AAFP applies a category called 'Affirmation of Value' for guidance it considers useful but which does not meet its criteria for full endorsement, largely reflecting differences over evidence thresholds and the risk of overtreatment in low-risk patients.

**Practical implications.** Standardised measurement is the part that changes outcomes: automated office BP with the patient rested and properly positioned, several readings averaged, and out-of-office confirmation by ambulatory or validated home monitoring before committing someone to lifelong therapy. That confirmation is strongly recommended, not mandatory — and it is the single most effective way to avoid treating white-coat hypertension.

[AAFP - hypertension clinical recommendations](https://www.aafp.org/family-physician/patient-care/clinical-recommendations/all-clinical-recommendations/highbloodpressure.html)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — attribution reversed.** The original said the AAFP 'updated its adult hypertension guidelines, formally adopting a target of <130/80'. The AAFP explicitly did NOT endorse the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline (nor did the ACP or ISH), and uses 'Affirmation of Value' rather than endorsement — a distinction that matters to a family physician, and one of the more instructive facts in this list. AAFP's own guidance does reference <130/80 with caveats, which is now stated accurately. Also 'Establishes out-of-office monitoring as a MANDATORY step' softened to strongly recommended.

### 7. AI-Driven Clinical Decision Support for Opioid Risk Stratification in Primary Care

*Annals of Family Medicine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** An EHR-integrated screening tool to identify primary care patients at elevated risk of opioid use disorder, reported to increase warm-handoff referrals to addiction medicine.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Positions automated risk flagging as a way to surface patients during routine encounters rather than relying on a clinician's recall at the moment of a refill request.

**Practical implications.** The value is in the warm handoff, not the algorithm — a flag that leads to a same-visit introduction to addiction services is different from one that generates a task nobody actions. Two cautions worth carrying: risk-prediction tools in opioid prescribing have a documented history of encoding racial and socioeconomic bias, and a high-risk flag must not become a reason to abruptly taper or discharge a patient, which increases overdose risk.

[Annals of Family Medicine - AI screening for opioid use disorder risk in primary care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=artificial+intelligence+clinical+decision+support+opioid+use+disorder+risk+primary+care+Annals+Family+Medicine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific publication matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search and the '47% increase in referrals' figure is not asserted. Two additions independent of the citation: algorithmic bias in opioid risk prediction is a documented and serious problem that the original did not mention, and the harm of abrupt tapering in response to a risk flag is well established. 'Endorsed by primary care addiction task forces, validating AI-assisted risk modeling as a standardized method' could not be confirmed and has been removed.

### 8. Metabolic Surgery versus Medical/Lifestyle Therapy for Type 2 Diabetes: Long-Term Outcomes (ARMMS-T2D)

*JAMA · 2024*

**The breakthrough.** A pooled long-term follow-up of four randomized trials — STAMPEDE, SLIMM-T2D, TRIABETES and CROSSROADS — in patients with type 2 diabetes and BMI 27 to 45 kg/m². Metabolic surgery gave superior glycemic control, with a between-group HbA1c difference of 1.4% at 7 years and 1.1% at 12 years, less diabetes medication, and higher rates of remission.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Durability out to 12 years is the contribution — the question was never whether surgery works initially but whether the benefit persists. Microvascular complication and mortality benefits come from separate observational cohorts; ARMMS-T2D was not powered for mortality.

**Practical implications.** Worth raising as an early option rather than a last resort in a patient with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes and obesity. One important caveat for 2026: the medical arms of these trials predate widespread use of semaglutide and tirzepatide, so this is surgery versus the medical therapy of a decade ago — not surgery versus a modern GLP-1 or dual agonist. Also note the enrolled BMI range started at 27, lower than the usual referral threshold.

[JAMA - long-term outcomes of medical management versus bariatric surgery in type 2 diabetes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10900968/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected.** Real (ARMMS-T2D, JAMA 2024 — a 2024 publication placed in the 2025 list). Three corrections. BMI was 27-45 kg/m², not '≥35' as submitted. The follow-up is 7 and 12 years, not 10. And the original said this was compared against intensive medical therapy 'including modern GLP-1s' and that it 'proved... lower all-cause mortality' — the medical arms predate modern incretin therapy, and the trial was not powered for mortality; the mortality figures circulating for bariatric surgery come from observational cohorts.

### 9. FIB-4 Index Screening for Advanced Fibrosis in Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) in Primary Care

*The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Automated FIB-4 scoring — calculated from age, AST, ALT and platelet count, all of which are usually already in the record — used in primary care EHRs to stratify patients with MASLD for the risk of advanced fibrosis.

**Clinical guideline impact.** AASLD guidance recommends FIB-4 as the initial non-invasive risk stratification step in patients at elevated risk of MASLD fibrosis, particularly those with type 2 diabetes, obesity or two or more metabolic risk factors, with a two-step pathway to elastography or a specialist biomarker for indeterminate results.

**Practical implications.** Efficient precisely because it needs no new test. Know its limits: FIB-4 is age-dependent and loses specificity over 65, so the conventional low cut-off produces many false positives in older patients; and a large indeterminate zone is the rule rather than the exception, which is what the second-step test exists for. Most patients with steatosis and a low FIB-4 need no referral at all.

[FIB-4 for risk stratification of fibrosis in MASLD in primary care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=FIB-4+primary+care+MASLD+advanced+fibrosis+risk+stratification+validation)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, and one claim corrected.** No specific Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology validation study matching the submission was located; the url is a PubMed title search. The approach is genuine and reflects AASLD guidance. CORRECTION: the original said 'Updated AASLD and AAFP guidelines MANDATE routine FIB-4 screening in ALL primary care patients with Type 2 Diabetes or metabolic syndrome' — guidance recommends risk-based stratification rather than mandating universal screening, and no AAFP mandate was identified. Added the age-dependence limitation and the indeterminate zone, both of which determine whether the tool is used well.

### 10. Screening and Treatment of Perinatal and Postpartum Depression in Primary Care

*USPSTF reaffirmation; zuranolone trials in Am J Psychiatry · USPSTF 2023; zuranolone FDA approval August 2023*

**The breakthrough.** Universal perinatal depression screening is a USPSTF recommendation, reaffirmed in 2023, and it is identifying more women with postpartum depression. Zuranolone, a synthetic allopregnanolone analogue, became the first oral drug approved for postpartum depression in August 2023 — a 14-day nightly course taken with a fat-containing meal, within a year of delivery, with symptom improvement in days rather than weeks.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Closes a real treatment gap: an SSRI takes weeks to work in a condition where the acute risk period is now. Despite availability, most eligible women are still not being offered zuranolone.

**Practical implications.** Screen at prenatal visits and through the postpartum period, and be ready to act on a positive screen the same day. Before prescribing zuranolone, know the constraints: it is a Schedule IV controlled substance, causes marked somnolence with a driving restriction after each dose, and needs an explicit conversation about breastfeeding and about who will care for the infant during treatment. Severe symptoms, psychosis or suicidality need psychiatric involvement regardless.

[Zuranolone for the treatment of postpartum depression](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.20220785)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REPLACED — the cited study could not be found.** No JAMA study evaluating universal perinatal screening paired with primary-care-initiated zuranolone or targeted psychotherapy was located. Zuranolone itself is real and the rapid onset is accurate. Rewritten around the USPSTF screening reaffirmation and the zuranolone approval evidence. Two safety facts were absent from the original and matter a great deal for a family physician prescribing it: zuranolone is a SCHEDULE IV controlled substance with significant sedation and a post-dose driving restriction, and infant care during a 14-day course needs planning. The original's framing of immediate primary care initiation 'without waiting for specialized psychiatric placement' needed that counterweight.

### 1. Social Care via In-Person Navigation or Signposting? A Randomized Trial of the Access to Resources in the Community (ARC) Model

*Annals of Family Medicine · July/August 2026 — 24(4):287*

**The breakthrough.** 326 adult primary care patients in Ottawa and Greater Sudbury, Canada, randomised by Dahrouge and colleagues to the ARC model — a longitudinal, bilingual, patient-centred navigation service embedded in primary care, with up to three months of dedicated navigator support — or to signposting to 211-Ontario, a free multilingual information helpline. Patients receiving navigation were 41% more likely to access at least one needed health or social resource (50.3% versus 35.8%).

**Clinical guideline impact.** Randomised evidence that the INTENSITY of social prescribing support determines whether it works. Signposting is what most systems actually do when they 'address social determinants', and it performed measurably worse.

**Practical implications.** Handing a patient a phone number is not an intervention. If a practice is going to address housing, food, transport and isolation, the finding is that it needs a person doing the navigating — which is a staffing decision, not a referral pathway decision. Note that even in the navigation arm only half accessed a resource, so the ceiling is well short of complete.

[Annals of Family Medicine - social care via in-person navigation or signposting](https://www.annfammed.org/content/24/4/287)

> **Citation check — matched.** Real and accurately summarised. Annals of Family Medicine, July/August 2026, 24(4):287, Dahrouge et al., 326 patients in Ottawa and Greater Sudbury. The submitted rates of 50.3% versus 35.8% are consistent with the reported 41% relative increase in accessing at least one resource. One of the best-sourced entries submitted across any specialty.

### 2. Underused Pharmacologic Therapies for ME/CFS and Long COVID before Specialist Referral

*Annals of Family Medicine · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A Mayo Clinic cohort of 571 patients examined what primary care had prescribed before referral for myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome and long COVID. Medications for sleep and mood were common; treatments aimed at the core features — fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, post-exertional malaise and orthostatic intolerance — were substantially underused.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Points to a symptom-domain framework as the practical organising principle in general practice: treat the orthostatic intolerance, treat the sleep, pace against post-exertional malaise, rather than treating the diagnosis as untreatable while waiting for a referral.

**Practical implications.** Orthostatic intolerance in particular is often identifiable and manageable in primary care — fluid and salt, compression, and where appropriate pharmacologic support. The one thing not to do is prescribe graded exercise as a default: post-exertional malaise means exertion can worsen the illness, which distinguishes ME/CFS from ordinary deconditioning and is the commonest management error.

[Annals of Family Medicine - underuse of therapies for ME/CFS and long COVID in primary care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=ME%2FCFS+long+COVID+primary+care+management+underused+therapies+Mayo+Annals+Family+Medicine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** Not individually confirmed; the url is a PubMed title search. Two entries from this 2026 Family Medicine list were verified in detail and both matched exactly, which raises confidence that the set derives from real Annals content — but that is not confirmation of this specific paper. Added the post-exertional malaise caution against graded exercise, which is central to safe ME/CFS care and was absent. The original's 'supplement protocols' was removed as unsupported.

### 3. General Practitioner Performance in Carotid Plaque Detection Using AI-Assisted Point-of-Care Ultrasound

*Annals of Family Medicine · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A feasibility study of general practitioners using handheld AI-assisted point-of-care ultrasound to screen high-risk patients for carotid plaque. After structured training, GPs reportedly achieved 87% sensitivity and 91% specificity against senior ultrasound specialists, with the carotid bifurcation the hardest region to image reliably.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Relevant to the wider question of what AI-assisted image acquisition lets a generalist do at the bedside. A feasibility study establishes that it can be done, not that doing it improves outcomes.

**Practical implications.** The unanswered question is what a detected plaque changes. Carotid plaque is common with age and asymptomatic stenosis is generally managed medically — so unless the finding alters statin or antiplatelet decisions that risk scoring would not have driven anyway, screening risks generating incidental findings and anxiety. Worth treating as a research direction rather than a service to offer.

[Annals of Family Medicine - AI-assisted POCUS for carotid plaque detection by GPs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=general+practitioner+point-of-care+ultrasound+carotid+plaque+artificial+intelligence+Annals+Family+Medicine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** Not individually confirmed; the url is a PubMed title search, and the 87%/91% figures are reported as claimed rather than asserted. Added the downstream-utility caution: the original framed this as allowing providers to 'identify vascular disease early', which presumes a management consequence a feasibility study cannot demonstrate.

### 4. Long-Standing Practice Registration, Urgent Hospital Admissions and Hospital Costs

*Annals of Family Medicine · July 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A retrospective cohort of 100,450 patients across 48 general practices in the Netherlands, examining two distinct forms of continuity. Patients registered with their practice for more than 5 years had 9% to 21% lower odds of urgent hospital admission and 17% to 28% lower hospital costs than those registered 0 to 5 years.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The two forms of continuity behaved differently, which is the interesting part. DURATION of registration with the practice was associated with both fewer urgent admissions and lower costs. DENSITY — consistently seeing the same individual GP — was associated with 6% to 7% lower hospital costs but NOT with fewer urgent admissions.

**Practical implications.** The lever appears to be the enduring relationship with a practice, not simply seeing the same face each visit. That supports panel stability and long-term registration as quality metrics, and argues against models built on episodic access to whoever is free. Observational data, so residual confounding by health status and social stability is the obvious alternative explanation.

[Annals of Family Medicine - long-standing practice registration and hospital use](https://www.annfammed.org/content/24/4)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** Real and the figures match exactly: Annals of Family Medicine, July 2026, Netherlands, 100,450 patients, 48 practices, 9-21% lower odds of urgent admission and 17-28% lower costs beyond 5 years of registration. Added the distinction the original omitted and which is the study's most useful finding — density of visits with one physician was associated with 6-7% lower costs but NOT fewer urgent admissions, so duration and density are not interchangeable. Also flagged the observational design, since 'definitive empirical backing' as originally written overstates a cohort study.

### 5. 2026-2027 AAFP Immunization Schedules for Children, Adolescents and Adults

### 6. Transmural Collaborative Cardiovascular Risk Management for Patients Taking Antipsychotics (TACTIC)

*BJGP Open · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A feasibility trial of a person-centred, multidisciplinary intervention in general practice bringing together GPs, psychiatrists and patients to address the cardiometabolic risk that accompanies long-term antipsychotic use.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Addresses one of the widest and most persistent gaps in primary care: people on long-term antipsychotics die 15 to 20 years earlier than the general population, mostly of cardiovascular disease, and metabolic monitoring rates for this group are poor everywhere it has been measured. A feasibility trial tests whether a shared-care model can be delivered, not whether it changes outcomes.

**Practical implications.** The practical content is unglamorous and effective: blood pressure, lipids, HbA1c and weight at defined intervals, with a route to act on the results that does not require destabilising effective psychiatric treatment. Clozapine and olanzapine carry the highest metabolic burden; switching is a psychiatric decision, so the shared-care route matters.

[BJGP Open - TACTIC cardiovascular risk management in antipsychotic users](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=TACTIC+cardiovascular+risk+management+antipsychotic+general+practice+BJGP+Open)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** Not individually confirmed; the url is a PubMed title search. Added the mortality-gap context and the drug-specific metabolic risk, which make the entry useful regardless. 'Refines primary care guidelines... establishing structured shared-care framework expectations' overstates what a FEASIBILITY trial does, and has been softened — the original did note it was a feasibility trial while simultaneously crediting it with refining guidelines.

### 7. Older Black and Hispanic Patients' Perceptions of Medicare Annual Wellness Visits

*Annals of Family Medicine · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A qualitative study by Brown and colleagues examining why uptake of the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit differs by race and ethnicity, identifying structural and educational barriers — prominently, patient confusion about what an AWV is, given that it is a non-procedural planning visit rather than a physical examination.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The AWV is free to the beneficiary and is where preventive planning, cognitive screening and advance care planning are supposed to happen, so differential uptake translates fairly directly into differential preventive care.

**Practical implications.** The actionable finding is about pre-visit communication. A patient who books expecting a physical and gets a conversation feels short-changed and may not return; one who is told in advance what the visit is for does not. Qualitative work identifies the barriers — it does not establish that any particular messaging fixes them.

[Annals of Family Medicine - patient perceptions of Medicare Annual Wellness Visits](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Medicare+Annual+Wellness+Visit+Black+Hispanic+older+adults+perceptions+qualitative+Annals+Family+Medicine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** Not individually confirmed; the url is a PubMed title search. One correction: the original said the study 'provided actionable communication solutions' and 'Prompts health systems to overhaul pre-visit messaging' — a qualitative study surfaces barriers and suggested directions; it does not validate solutions. Framed accordingly.

### 8. Dyadic Electronic Medical Record Linkage in Dementia Care

*Annals of Family Medicine · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A Canadian primary care report on linking the electronic medical record of a person living with dementia to that of their primary informal family caregiver, so the pair can be managed as a unit.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Recognises what dementia care actually looks like in general practice: the caregiver's health is a determinant of the patient's outcome, and caregiver strain is the commonest precipitant of crisis and of institutionalisation. Most EMRs have no way to represent that relationship.

**Practical implications.** Enables caregiver strain to be tracked, appointments to be aligned, and support to be offered before a crisis. The obvious caution is consent and privacy: linking two adults' records requires explicit, revocable consent from both, and the caregiver is a patient in their own right, not an adjunct to the person they care for.

[Annals of Family Medicine - dyadic EMR linkage in dementia care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=dyadic+electronic+medical+record+linkage+dementia+caregiver+primary+care+Annals+Family+Medicine)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** Not individually confirmed; the url is a PubMed title search. Added the consent and privacy consideration, which is the first practical obstacle to any record-linkage scheme between two competent adults and was absent from the original. 'Formalizing how family physicians monitor and support caregiver health' softened — a report describing an innovation does not formalise practice.

### 9. Pharmaceutical Promotional Payments and General Practice Prescribing Patterns

*BJGP Open · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A longitudinal study by Mulinari and colleagues of payments from pharmaceutical companies to general practices in the UK, examining associations with regional prescribing patterns over time.

### 10. Primary hrHPV Testing as the Preferred Cervical Cancer Screening Pathway, Including Self-Collection

*USPSTF recommendation statement · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Primary high-risk HPV testing every 5 years is the preferred modality for average-risk individuals aged 30 to 65, and self-collected hrHPV sampling has been incorporated as an option — sampling that can be done by the patient rather than requiring a speculum examination.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Self-collection is the substantive change, because the barrier to cervical screening for many people has never been the test interval but the examination itself. It moves screening toward something that can be completed in a clinic room, or in some programmes at home.

**Practical implications.** Offer self-collection to patients who have declined or deferred speculum screening — the group with the highest cervical cancer risk is people who are underscreened, not people screened at the wrong interval. A positive hrHPV result still requires clinician follow-up with cytology or colposcopy depending on genotype and history, so self-collection changes the sampling step, not the pathway after it.
