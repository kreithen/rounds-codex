### 1. AATS Expert Consensus: Surgical Management of Esophageal and GEJ Cancer

*The Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The American Association for Thoracic Surgery convened an international, multidisciplinary panel — gastroenterologists, medical oncologists, radiation oncologists, surgical oncologists and thoracic surgeons — to produce consensus recommendations on treating esophageal and gastroesophageal junction cancer at every stage, including the difficult scenarios. Developed by modified Delphi method with expert opinion where evidence was thin, it addresses the role of surgery, endoscopic therapy and surveillance in early-stage adenocarcinoma; how to combine surgery with multimodality therapy in locally advanced adenocarcinoma; and when to operate for squamous cell carcinoma.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Esophagectomy's place has shifted underneath the specialty as endoscopic resection, systemic therapy and radiation have all improved. A consensus that treats each histology and stage separately, rather than issuing one pathway, matches how these decisions are actually made.

**Practical implications.** The document's value is in the grey zones — margin requirements, extent of two-field lymphadenectomy, and what surveillance looks like after complete endoscopic mucosal resection when no esophagectomy has been done. Note the method: modified Delphi with expert opinion means these are consensus statements, not trial results, and where the panel could not agree the honest reading is that the question is open. Squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma are handled as different diseases here, which is the right instinct and worth carrying into the tumour board.

[The 2025 AATS expert consensus document: surgical management of esophageal and gastroesophageal junction cancer](https://www.jtcvs.org/article/S0022-5223(25)00298-3/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — document confirmed, recommendation count unverified.** Confirmed: 'The 2025 American Association for Thoracic Surgery (AATS) expert consensus document: Surgical management of esophageal and gastroesophageal junction cancer', JTCVS 2025, volume 170 issue 3, pages 884–901, international multidisciplinary panel, modified Delphi. The submission's description of the scope — early-stage, locally advanced adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, endoscopic resection versus radical esophagectomy, margins, two-field lymphadenectomy, post-EMR surveillance — matches the published abstract closely. Two adjustments: the '23 evidence-based recommendations' figure could not be verified in accessible sources and has been dropped rather than asserted; and the submission calls the recommendations 'evidence-based' without qualification, where the document itself states they rest on modified Delphi consensus and expert opinion — that distinction has been restored because it changes how much weight a reader should give a statement in a grey zone. Also removed the submission's claim that the document 'standardizes surgical pathways', which overstates a consensus statement.

### 2. CHEST Clinical Practice Guideline: Early-Stage Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer

*CHEST · July 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The American College of Chest Physicians issued a new clinical practice guideline for stage I and II NSCLC. For clinical stage I, it makes a STRONG recommendation for minimally invasive approaches over thoracotomy — on LOW certainty evidence, supported by the first meta-analysis to show an overall survival association favouring minimally invasive surgery. For clinical stage II, minimally invasive surgery is a CONDITIONAL recommendation. New evidence is cited showing equivalent overall survival for peripheral NSCLC of 2 cm or less treated by sublobar resection versus lobectomy. On systemic therapy: adjuvant chemotherapy plus a checkpoint inhibitor improves overall survival in resected stage II including node-negative tumours 4 cm or larger, and adjuvant targeted therapy improves overall survival in resected stage IB (3 cm or more) and stage II EGFR-mutant disease.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It settles the open-versus-minimally-invasive question for stage I with the strongest recommendation the evidence will bear, and it folds the adjuvant immunotherapy and targeted-therapy results into a surgical guideline — which is where the surgeon's referral obligation now sits.

**Practical implications.** Read the two recommendation strengths as different instructions: stage I is strong, stage II is conditional, and the certainty of evidence behind the stage I recommendation is LOW — so a thoracotomy in a difficult stage II case is not a guideline deviation. Systematic intraoperative mediastinal and hilar nodal sampling or dissection is required in both stages; without it the pathological stage is unreliable and the adjuvant decision is made on bad information. The sublobar equivalence applies to PERIPHERAL tumours 2 cm or less, which is the population the randomised trials studied — do not extend it upward on the strength of this guideline.

[Management of patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer: an American College of Chest Physicians clinical practice guideline](https://journal.chestnet.org/article/S0012-3692(25)00737-8/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — recommendation strength overstated, and the guideline's key surgical finding was omitted.** The guideline is real: CHEST, published July 2025 (PubMed 40562304). Corrections. (1) The submission says it 'formally establishes minimally invasive surgical resection (VATS or RATS) as the IMPERATIVE standard over open thoracotomy for clinical Stage I NSCLC'. The published recommendation is STRONG but on LOW CERTAINTY evidence for stage I, and for stage II it is only CONDITIONAL — the submission collapses two different strengths into one absolute. (2) The submission omits the guideline's most surgically consequential new statement: equivalent overall survival for peripheral ≤2 cm NSCLC treated by SUBLOBAR resection versus lobectomy. Restored, with the size limit stated, because the adjacent entry in this same list extends sublobar resection to 2–3 cm on weaker evidence. (3) 'Confirms routine adjuvant systemic therapy for resected Stage II disease' is imprecise — the guideline specifies chemotherapy PLUS checkpoint inhibitor, and separately adjuvant targeted therapy for EGFR-mutant stage IB(≥3 cm)/II. (4) The '17 evidence-based recommendations' count could not be verified and has been dropped. Added the systematic nodal sampling requirement, which the guideline states and the submission reduced to 'consistent post-resection pathological staging'.

### 3. NCCN Lung Cancer Screening v1.2025: No Limit on Years Since Quitting

*Journal of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** NCCN removed the years-since-quitting restriction from its lung cancer screening criteria. Screening is now recommended for anyone aged 50 or older with a 20 pack-year or greater smoking history, with NO limit on how long ago they stopped. The rationale is that people who formerly smoked carry a substantially higher lung cancer risk than those who never smoked, and there is no substantive drop-off in that risk beyond 15 years since quitting — the cut-off the USPSTF adopted in 2021 and NCCN has now abandoned.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It expands eligibility to a group that had been aging out of screening precisely as their incidence peaked. For thoracic surgery the consequence is downstream: more screen-detected stage I nodules in older former smokers, and therefore more candidates for anatomical sublobar resection.

**Practical implications.** The practical friction is that NCCN and the USPSTF now disagree, and in the United States insurance coverage follows the USPSTF and CMS, not NCCN — so a patient 18 years past quitting may meet the NCCN criterion and still face a coverage problem. Check before promising a scan. Clinically, the expanded group is older and more comorbid, which raises the value of pulmonary function and cardiac assessment before a nodule becomes a resection discussion, and makes sublobar resection more often the right operation rather than a compromise.

[NCCN Guidelines Insights: Lung Cancer Screening, Version 1.2025](https://jnccn.org/view/journals/jnccn/23/1/article-e250002.xml)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched — verified, with the coverage mismatch added.** Fully verified. NCCN Guidelines Insights: Lung Cancer Screening, Version 1.2025 (JNCCN 23(1)): screening recommended at age ≥50 with ≥20 pack-years and NO limit on years since quitting; the stated rationale is that risk in people who formerly smoked remains substantially elevated with no substantive drop-off after 15 years since quitting. The submission's description is accurate, including the point that the 15-year restriction was the one removed and that the effect is to expand the pool of screen-detected stage I candidates. One addition of my own, not a correction: NCCN and USPSTF now differ on this criterion and US coverage tracks the USPSTF, so eligibility by NCCN does not guarantee a covered scan — a reader acting on this entry needs to know that before telling a patient they qualify.

### 4. Robotic Versus VATS Resection After Neoadjuvant Chemoimmunotherapy

*Journal of Thoracic Disease / Journal of Robotic Surgery (meta-analysis) · 2025–2026*

**The breakthrough.** Comparative series and a meta-analysis address a problem neoadjuvant immunotherapy created: dense hilar and mediastinal fibrosis that makes a planned minimally invasive resection difficult. In a comparative cohort, conversion to thoracotomy occurred in 7.5% of robotic cases against 28.2% of VATS cases (P<0.001); the meta-analysis found robotic surgery associated with lower conversion (OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.13–0.65; P=0.003), more lymph nodes dissected (MD 4.04) and more nodal stations explored (MD 1.22). Nodal assessment differed accordingly — yp-N0 in 88.5% versus 67.6%, yp-N2 in 3.8% versus 19.7%. Rates of complete resection, operative duration, blood loss and transfusion were not meaningfully different.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The clinical question is whether a minimally invasive approach survives contact with a post-immunotherapy hilum, and on these data the robotic platform converts less often and samples more nodes. Both approaches showed equivalent perioperative and oncological safety.

**Practical implications.** Interpret the nodal numbers carefully. More nodes and stations retrieved is a real advantage, but the accompanying yp-N0 versus yp-N2 difference reflects the populations being compared as much as the instrument — a retrospective series where more nodes are found in one arm and more N2 disease in the other is describing two different case mixes, not proving a staging superiority. The defensible statement is that robotic dissection maintains a minimally invasive approach through post-treatment fibrosis with more thorough nodal sampling. Have a plan for conversion regardless of platform: 7.5% is not zero, and a fibrotic hilum is the situation where an unplanned conversion goes badly.

[Robotic-assisted versus video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery for NSCLC after neoadjuvant immunotherapy: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11701-026-03364-y)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the conversion figures are wrong, and no guideline update exists.** The clinical claim is supported but the numbers are not. The submission states conversion of '3.1% vs. 8.9%' for RATS versus VATS; the published comparative figures are 7.5% versus 28.2% (P<0.001), with a meta-analysis OR of 0.28 — both arms are substantially higher than submitted, and the gap is much wider. Corrected. The submission also claims the finding 'updates thoracic surgical society guidelines to recommend robotic platforms for complex post-immunotherapy hilar dissections and high-station N2 lymphadenectomy'; no such society guideline could be found, and that sentence has been removed. 'Multi-center trial analyses' overstates the evidence, which is retrospective comparative series plus a meta-analysis of them — restated. Retained and verified: lower conversion, higher node and station counts, no difference in complete resection/operative time/blood loss, and equivalent perioperative and oncological safety. Added a caution on the yp-N0/yp-N2 contrast, which the submission did not report and which is more likely case-mix than staging superiority.

### 5. Segmentectomy Versus Lobectomy in 2–3 cm (T1c) NSCLC

*CHEST · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A national propensity-matched analysis compared anatomical segmentectomy with lobectomy in NSCLC of 2–3 cm — T1c, above the size range the randomised sublobar trials studied. No difference in overall survival was found between segmentectomy and lobectomy in either multivariable-adjusted or propensity score-matched analysis for clinical T1c disease; in the pathological T1cN0M0 subset, five-year survival was 74.1% versus 74.0%. The conclusion was that segmentectomy is non-inferior to lobectomy for clinical or pathological T1c NSCLC in a national dataset.

**Clinical guideline impact.** JCOG0802 and CALGB140503 established sublobar resection for peripheral tumours of 2 cm or less. This extends the question upward, where no randomised answer exists, and finds no survival penalty in a matched observational comparison.

**Practical implications.** This is propensity-matched registry data, not a randomised trial, and observational matching cannot remove the selection that determines who gets a segmentectomy for a 2.5 cm tumour in the first place. It is a reason to consider segmentectomy in a patient with limited pulmonary reserve where lobectomy is a real functional cost — not a reason to prefer it. Whatever the resection, the nodal dissection has to be complete: sublobar resection with inadequate nodal sampling is how an occult N1 goes unfound and adjuvant therapy is never offered. One claim to discard: matched analysis in patients WITH lymphovascular invasion showed no significant difference between segmentectomy and lobectomy either, so LVI is not the switch that makes lobectomy mandatory.

[Segmentectomy vs lobectomy for patients with 2- to 3-cm non-small cell lung cancer](https://journal.chestnet.org/article/S0012-3692(25)00812-8/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED on the stratification claim — LVI does not make lobectomy mandatory.** The study is real (CHEST 2025, S0012-3692(25)00812-8) and its headline finding — segmentectomy non-inferior to lobectomy in T1c, five-year survival 74.1% vs 74.0% in pathological T1cN0M0 — is confirmed. The submission's clinical rule is not. It states that segmentectomy achieves equivalent recurrence-free survival 'in T1c tumors LACKING VPI/LVI, while lobectomy remains MANDATORY if microvascular/pleural invasion is present'. The published propensity-matched analysis found NO significant difference between segmentectomy and lobectomy in patients WITH lymphovascular invasion — the opposite of a mandate. No source supporting a VPI/LVI-conditional rule could be found, and it is the kind of invented decision rule that reads authoritative and changes an operation. Also corrected: 'propensity-matched trial data' — propensity matching is what you do in the ABSENCE of a trial, and calling it trial data hides the confounding that matters most here; and the submission omits that the randomised evidence for sublobar resection stops at 2 cm, which is the single most important caveat for a 2–3 cm tumour.

### 6. Postoperative ctDNA as a Prognostic Marker After NSCLC Resection

*Multiple (meta-analyses) · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Meta-analyses of postoperative circulating tumour DNA in resected NSCLC establish it as a strong prognostic marker. In one synthesis of 13 studies and 1,309 patients, immediate postoperative ctDNA detection (4 studies, n=556) carried a hazard ratio of 6.41 (95% CI 4.18–9.83) for recurrence-free survival; a second, of 7 studies and 1,138 patients, found increased risk of both recurrence and death with ctDNA positivity. ctDNA positivity immediately after surgery or after adjuvant chemotherapy is more strongly associated with worse recurrence-free and overall survival than negativity, and clearance after radical therapy is associated with prolonged survival. Tumour-informed assays are the more specific; tumour-agnostic approaches are more sensitive in some settings.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A hazard ratio above 6 for recurrence identifies a group that will relapse, months before imaging shows it. As a prognostic marker in resected NSCLC, ctDNA is now well evidenced.

**Practical implications.** Prognostic is not the same as actionable, and this is where the field currently is. No randomised trial has shown that changing adjuvant treatment on the basis of a ctDNA result improves outcomes in NSCLC, and neither NCCN nor ASCO recommends ctDNA to guide adjuvant decisions or routine post-resection surveillance outside a trial. A positive result identifies risk without providing an intervention proven to modify it; a negative result does not license omitting adjuvant therapy that stage-based evidence supports. Enrol these patients in trials rather than escalating or de-escalating on the number — and know which assay type was used, because specificity and sensitivity differ between tumour-informed and tumour-agnostic platforms.

[Prognostic value of postoperative circulating tumour DNA for recurrence-free survival in resected NSCLC: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12094-025-04182-8)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — cohort size unverified, and guideline integration REVERSED.** The prognostic evidence is real and has been retained with actual figures (HR 6.41 for RFS on immediate postoperative detection; 13 studies/1,309 patients and 7 studies/1,138 patients). Three corrections. (1) The submission's '3,280+ resected NSCLC patients' could not be verified; the locatable meta-analyses are roughly a third of that, and the figure has been replaced with what is sourced. (2) The submission states this 'integrates post-resection ctDNA clearance as a high-confidence prognostic biomarker in NCCN and ASCO POST-SURGICAL SURVEILLANCE PROTOCOLS'. Neither society recommends ctDNA for routine post-resection surveillance in NSCLC; that sentence is removed and the actual position stated. (3) The submission says the result guides 'the escalation or de-escalation of adjuvant chemotherapy'. No randomised trial has shown ctDNA-guided adjuvant decisions improve NSCLC outcomes — and de-escalation on a negative ctDNA means withholding therapy with a proven stage-based survival benefit, which is the direction with real potential for harm. Restated explicitly. Retained from the submission and verified: lead time over radiological relapse, and the tumour-informed versus tumour-agnostic distinction.

### 7. Biomarker Selection for Perioperative Immunotherapy in Resectable NSCLC

*Multiple (phase III trial programmes) · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The perioperative immunotherapy trials define their own boundaries by whom they enrolled. CheckMate 816 EXCLUDED patients with known EGFR or ALK alterations; KEYNOTE-671 permitted them but did not mandate testing. Both trials significantly improved event-free survival and pathological complete response with chemoimmunotherapy — KEYNOTE-671 in resectable stage II–IIIB with perioperative pembrolizumab. On PD-L1, higher expression was generally associated with greater benefit, but significant improvements were seen even in patients with low or negative PD-L1.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The practical instruction is a testing one: molecular profiling has to happen BEFORE the perioperative treatment decision, not after resection, because an EGFR or ALK alteration changes the pathway and the pivotal trials provide no evidence for chemoimmunotherapy in that group.

**Practical implications.** Be precise about why EGFR/ALK patients are steered elsewhere. It is not that a subgroup analysis showed immunotherapy fails them — it is that the trial that defined the standard EXCLUDED them, so there is no evidence either way from that programme, and separate experience of immune-related toxicity when checkpoint inhibitors and EGFR TKIs are given in sequence argues for caution. Absence of evidence is the honest reason, and it still supports the same decision. On PD-L1: do not use a low or negative result to withhold perioperative chemoimmunotherapy, because benefit was demonstrated in those patients too. Get tissue adequate for full molecular testing at diagnosis; a small biopsy that cannot be profiled is what forces this decision to be made blind.

[Perioperative immunotherapy in resectable NSCLC — CheckMate 816 and KEYNOTE-671](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/breathe/20/3/240044)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the reasoning is exclusion, not subgroup analysis, and the PD-L1 claim is overstated.** The clinical bottom line the submission reaches — profile for EGFR/ALK before committing to perioperative immunotherapy — is right, and both trials are real. Two corrections to how it gets there. (1) The submission says 'Phase III trial subgroup ANALYSES CONFIRMED that PD-L1 expression levels and targetable driver mutations (EGFR/ALK) dictate the efficacy of perioperative immune checkpoint blockade.' For EGFR/ALK there was no such subgroup analysis in the defining trial: CheckMate 816 EXCLUDED those patients, and KEYNOTE-671 allowed them without mandating testing. The recommendation rests on absence of evidence plus toxicity concerns, not on a demonstrated lack of benefit — a distinction that matters if a reader is ever asked to justify it. (2) 'maximizing pathological complete response in high-PD-L1 candidates' implies PD-L1 selects who should be treated; the trials showed significant benefit even at low or negative PD-L1, so a low value must not be used to withhold treatment. Restated. Removed the submission's unsourced claim about 'surgical delays' as a documented harm, and its assertion that guidelines now formally 'advise against' perioperative immunotherapy in this group, which could not be verified as written.

### 8. DECIPHER-NODL: Imaging Plus cfDNA Fragmentomics for Pulmonary Nodules

*The Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** DECIPHER-NODL is the first model to combine deep-learning low-dose CT radiomics with a cell-free DNA model built on four genome-wide fragmentation features — copy number variation, fragment size ratio, fragment-based methylation and motif signatures — through a stacked ensemble algorithm. The integrated imaging-plus-cfDNA model outperformed either component alone, with an AUC of 0.950 (95% CI 0.926–0.975) in the internal test set and 0.966 (0.940–0.991) on external validation. A companion invasion-prediction model stratified aggressiveness, separating adenocarcinoma in situ from minimally invasive and invasive adenocarcinoma.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The indeterminate nodule is the commonest unresolved problem in thoracic surgery, and the two available signals — how it looks and what is circulating — have been read separately. Combining structural imaging with molecular fragmentation is where the complementary information is, particularly for small or radiologically ambiguous lesions.

**Practical implications.** This is a model development and validation study. An AUC of 0.95 on a curated test set is not the same as prospective performance in a screening clinic, where prevalence is lower and the model will be applied to nodules unlike those it was trained on. Nothing here is in any guideline, and no assay is available to order. The right use of it is to know the direction the field is moving and to read the eventual prospective work critically — specifically, whether the model beats a validated clinical risk calculator in the same patients, which is the comparison that decides whether it changes anything.

[Risk-stratified classification of pulmonary nodule malignancy via a machine learning model integrating imaging and cell-free DNA (DECIPHER-NODL)](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanwpc/article/PIIS2666-6065(25)00269-X/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — model real and strong, but it is not prospective and not in any guideline.** The study is real and better than its odd name suggests — Lancet Regional Health Western Pacific 2025, deep-learning LDCT radiomics plus a four-feature cfDNA fragmentomics model, stacked ensemble, AUC 0.950 internal and 0.966 external validation, with an additional invasion-prediction model. This is the fifth time in this review that a claim reading as fabricated has turned out to be real; the failure mode remains overstated sourcing, not invented science. Three corrections. (1) The submission calls it a 'prospective AI framework' validated 'across 1,350+ indeterminate pulmonary nodules'; the publication is a model DEVELOPMENT AND VALIDATION study, and the 1,350 figure could not be verified. (2) 'diagnostic accuracy superior to traditional Brock and Mayo clinical risk models' could not be verified from accessible sources — the documented comparison is against the individual imaging and cfDNA models, not against Brock or Mayo — so that claim has been reframed as the comparison the field still needs. (3) The submission says it 'integrates multi-modal AI nodule risk stratification INTO CLINICAL GUIDELINES'; it is in none, and no assay is orderable. Removed the claim that it 'significantly reduces unnecessary diagnostic resections', which is an aspiration of the framework rather than a measured outcome.

### 9. RAMIE and Left Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Nodal Yield

*Multiple (meta-analyses) · 2024–2025*

**The breakthrough.** Meta-analyses of robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy against conventional minimally invasive esophagectomy consistently find a higher nodal yield, with the advantage concentrated in the left recurrent laryngeal nerve station. Reported effects: left RLN nodes 1.74 versus 1.34 (mean difference 0.22, P<0.001) in one synthesis; a mean difference of 0.60 (95% CI 0.13–1.08, P=0.01) for left RLN nodes and 2.01 (1.05–2.96, P<0.001) for total nodes in another. The technical explanation is the exposure the robot gives in the upper mediastinum, where the left RLN dissection is the hardest part of a two-field lymphadenectomy to do well thoracoscopically.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Left RLN nodal dissection is both the highest-yield and the highest-risk part of the operation — it is where nodal staging is won and where vocal cord palsy is caused. A platform that improves access to that station addresses the specific technical bottleneck.

**Practical implications.** Look at the effect sizes before quoting this. A difference of 0.22 to 0.60 nodes at the left RLN station is statistically robust across meta-analyses and clinically small — it is a fraction of one node per patient, not a transformed lymphadenectomy. It may still matter, because a single positive station changes stage, but 'significantly higher' should not be heard as 'substantially higher'. What is worth taking seriously is the mechanism: whatever platform is used, the left RLN station needs deliberate, unhurried dissection with nerve preservation as an explicit goal, and intraoperative nerve monitoring is a reasonable adjunct. Vocal cord function should be assessed after the operation, not assumed.

[Short-term outcomes of robot-assisted versus conventional minimally invasive esophagectomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38492067/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — direction confirmed, magnitude and guideline status overstated.** The finding is real and reproducible across several meta-analyses: RAMIE yields more total nodes and more left recurrent laryngeal nerve nodes than conventional minimally invasive esophagectomy, and the left RLN station is where the technical advantage lies. Corrections. (1) MAGNITUDE: the submission says RAMIE 'yields SIGNIFICANTLY HIGHER lymph node harvest in the left RLN station', which is true statistically and misleading practically — the published differences are 1.74 versus 1.34 nodes (MD 0.22) and MD 0.60. Stated numerically so the reader can judge. (2) The submission claims this 'establishes RAMIE as a PRIMARY RECOMMENDED SURGICAL MODALITY in ISDE and ESMO practice guidelines for locally advanced esophageal carcinoma'. Neither guideline could be verified as making that recommendation; removed. (3) The submission asserts RAMIE 'mitigat[es] postoperative vocal cord paralysis' — the fewer pulmonary complications finding is supported, but a reduction in RLN palsy specifically could not be verified and would be counterintuitive given that the advantage is MORE aggressive dissection at that nerve. Replaced with the practical instruction (deliberate dissection, consider nerve monitoring, assess cord function postoperatively) rather than a reassurance.

### 1. STS 2026 Guidelines: Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation After Cardiac Surgery

*The Annals of Thoracic Surgery · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The Society of Thoracic Surgeons published clinical practice guidelines for the prevention and treatment of new-onset postoperative atrial fibrillation after CARDIAC surgery, developed by the STS Workforce on Evidence-Based Surgery. Fifteen recommendations are organised into three domains: perioperative interventions to reduce POAF, intraoperative strategies to prevent it, and postoperative treatment. The guidance is notably more assertive about amiodarone and posterior pericardiotomy than prior practice. POAF is the most frequent complication after cardiac surgery and carries greater morbidity, mortality, length of stay and cost.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A phase-based framework replaces the piecemeal habit of treating POAF as a postoperative surprise. Naming preoperative and intraoperative prevention as separate domains puts the decision before the arrhythmia rather than after it.

**Practical implications.** Note the scope: these are cardiac surgery guidelines. POAF after lung resection or esophagectomy is a related but distinct problem with its own incidence and its own evidence base, and the recommendations here should not be transplanted wholesale to a thoracic service. Posterior pericardiotomy is the intervention most likely to be new to a unit — it is an intraoperative decision, so it has to be discussed before the case, not on the ward round afterwards. The anticoagulation question for POAF persisting beyond 48 hours is genuinely unsettled and is treated separately in this list.

[The Society of Thoracic Surgeons 2026 clinical practice guidelines for the prevention and treatment of new-onset postoperative atrial fibrillation after cardiac surgery](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(26)00344-9/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — verified, and moved here from the 2025 list where it was submitted twice.** Verified: Annals of Thoracic Surgery 2026, S0003-4975(26)00344-9, co-led by Chatterjee and Schena, STS Workforce on Evidence-Based Surgery, 15 recommendations across preoperative/intraoperative/postoperative domains, with more assertive guidance on amiodarone and posterior pericardiotomy. Two structural corrections. (1) DEDUPLICATION: the SAME document was submitted as entry 7 of the Thoracic Surgery 2025 list ('STS Clinical Practice Guidelines for Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation'). It is a 2026 publication, so it is kept here on 2026 and removed from 2025 — leaving that year with nine entries, per the project rule that a development appears under one year only. (2) SCOPE: both submissions describe it as covering POAF 'following thoracic AND cardiac surgery'. The published title and content are specific to CARDIAC surgery; the thoracic extrapolation has been flagged rather than carried, since post-resection POAF has a different incidence and evidence base. The submission's Class I/IIa attributions (Class I for perioperative amiodarone and cardioversion, Class IIa for posterior pericardiotomy and beta-blockers) could not be individually verified against the published tables and have been stated as emphasis rather than as class assignments.

### 2. 2026 Multisociety Guideline for Acute Pulmonary Embolism

*Circulation / Journal of the American College of Cardiology · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The first ACC/AHA clinical practice guideline on acute pulmonary embolism, written jointly with ACCP, ACEP, CHEST, SCAI, SHM, SIR, SVM and SVN. Its central innovation is a new classification, the Acute Pulmonary Embolism Clinical Categories, with five categories A through E plus subcategories, spanning low to high risk of adverse outcomes, intended to sharpen severity classification, prognosis and treatment selection. At the low end, patients in Category A can be discharged home from the emergency department without hospitalisation.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Ten societies signing one document ends a long period in which emergency physicians, cardiologists, interventional radiologists and surgeons each risk-stratified PE differently. A shared category is what makes a PE response team conversation coherent.

**Practical implications.** For a thoracic surgeon the relevant end is the high-severity one, where surgical embolectomy competes with catheter-directed therapy and systemic thrombolysis. The categories are a common language for that discussion, not an algorithm that selects the intervention — the choice still turns on haemodynamics, right ventricular function, bleeding risk, clot location and what the institution can actually deliver at 3am. Learn the category definitions from the guideline itself rather than from a summary: the whole benefit is that everyone in the room means the same thing by 'Category C'.

[2026 AHA/ACC/ACCP/ACEP/CHEST/SCAI/SHM/SIR/SVM/SVN guideline for the evaluation and management of acute pulmonary embolism in adults](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIR.0000000000001415)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — guideline and categories verified, journals and specifics adjusted.** Verified: the 2026 AHA/ACC/ACCP/ACEP/CHEST/SCAI/SHM/SIR/SVM/SVN guideline, published in Circulation (CIR.0000000000001415) and JACC, is the first ACC/AHA guideline on acute PE and does introduce 'Acute Pulmonary Embolism Clinical Categories' with five categories A–E plus subcategories, ranging low to high risk. The submission's URL is correct. Corrections. (1) The submission says it was 'published across Circulation, CHEST, and JAMA'; it appears in Circulation and JACC — CHEST is one of the ten writing societies, not a journal of publication, and JAMA is not involved. (2) The submission's specific operational claims — 'fast-track home discharge for low-risk presentations' and 'expediting thoracic surgical embolectomy consultation for category D/E incipient cardiopulmonary collapse' — are directionally plausible and the Category A home-discharge point is verifiable, but the D/E-to-embolectomy mapping could not be confirmed and is now stated as the discussion the categories enable rather than as a guideline instruction. (3) 'Replaces rigid risk scores' overstates it; the categories are a new scheme, and existing scores are not withdrawn.

### 3. STS 2026: Nodal Assessment, Upstaging and Survival in Node-Negative NSCLC

*Society of Thoracic Surgeons Annual Meeting (J. Maxwell Chamberlain Memorial Paper) · January 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Christopher Seder presented the J. ### 4. Resection Without Preoperative Tissue Diagnosis in Indeterminate Nodules

*Multiple (surgical series and CHEST nodule guidance) · established practice*

**The breakthrough.** Preoperative tissue diagnosis is not always obtainable, and operating without it is accepted practice within limits. For indeterminate solid lesions of 8–30 mm, management follows the pretest probability of malignancy, with surgical resection recommended for high-probability lesions — above roughly 65% — ideally as thoracoscopic wedge resection with intraoperative frozen section. The benign yield in published series quantifies the cost: of 500 consecutive resections without preoperative tissue diagnosis, 336 (67.2%) were lung cancer, 93 (18.6%) metastatic cancer and 71 (14.2%) benign. In a series of 199 patients resected without a definitive preoperative diagnosis, 98 (49%) went to upfront lobectomy without frozen section because wedge resection was not feasible, and 6% of those proved benign. Male sex, increasing age, smoking history and prior lung cancer predict malignancy.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The framing that matters is that proceeding without tissue is a probability decision with a quantified benign rate, not a shortcut. Roughly one in seven resections done this way removes something benign.

**Practical implications.** The rule that protects patients is about the magnitude of what is being committed: a diagnostic thoracoscopic wedge with frozen section is a reasonable way to resolve a high-probability nodule, and an anatomical resection should follow only once malignancy is confirmed. A lobectomy or pneumonectomy without a tissue diagnosis is a different proposition — the 6% benign rate in the upfront-lobectomy series is the price, and for a pneumonectomy it is not an acceptable one. Where the nodule is deep and transthoracic needle aspiration carries a high pneumothorax risk, navigational bronchoscopy or an intraoperative approach is the better route to tissue, not abandoning the tissue requirement.

[Lung resection without tissue diagnosis: a pragmatic perspective on the indeterminate pulmonary nodule](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33773938/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND as cited — no STS 2026 guideline on this exists; rebuilt from the real evidence.** The submission attributes this to 'STS 2026 guidelines' that 'established clear benchmarks permitting surgical resection without a preoperative tissue biopsy in select high-risk indeterminate pulmonary nodules', with a link to a generic STS topic page. No such 2026 guideline could be found. The underlying clinical position IS well established but comes from surgical series and pulmonary nodule guidance, not a new document: pretest probability above roughly 65% supports resection, ideally thoracoscopic wedge; benign rates in resection-without-diagnosis series are 14.2% (500 patients) and 6% in an upfront-lobectomy subset. The submission's own instinct — that high-risk diagnostic manoeuvres in deep lesions and pneumonectomy without tissue should be rejected — is sound and has been kept and sharpened, because the graded rule (wedge with frozen section yes, anatomical resection only after confirmation, pneumonectomy never) is the part that actually protects a patient and the submission stated it only loosely.

### 5. Reoperative Surgical Mitral Valve Replacement Versus Transcatheter Valve-in-Valve

*The Annals of Thoracic Surgery · April 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A retrospective cohort at two quaternary care centres compared transcatheter mitral valve-in-valve (mViV) against reoperative surgical mitral valve replacement (rSMVR) for failing bioprosthetic mitral valves without active endocarditis, 2004 to 2023. Of 229 patients — 90 rSMVR, 139 mViV, mean age 66.0 versus 68.7 years — 30-day Mitral Valve Academic Research Consortium outcomes were similar between groups except for more prolonged ventilation after surgery. Endpoints were 30-day MVARC procedural success and five-year cumulative incidence of death, freedom from reintervention and heart failure. The reported long-term signal favours reoperative surgery.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Transcatheter valve-in-valve is established as safe in the short term, and the open question has been what happens over years. A cohort with follow-up long enough to reach five years begins to answer it, and the answer favours the open operation in patients who can tolerate one.

**Practical implications.** This is 229 patients at two high-volume centres, retrospectively compared, over a nineteen-year period during which both technologies changed substantially — so it identifies a direction, not an effect size to quote. The clinically defensible reading is the one the numbers support: mViV has the gentler early course, rSMVR the better long-term trajectory, so age and operative risk should drive the choice. A younger low-to-intermediate-risk patient with a failed bioprosthesis is the one for whom accepting a redo sternotomy plausibly buys durability; a frail high-risk patient is who valve-in-valve exists for. Beware the selection built into the comparison: the patients who were offered surgery in this cohort were offered it because someone judged they would survive it.

[Long-term survival advantage of reoperative surgical mitral valve replacement over transcatheter mitral valve-in-valve: a multicenter cohort](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(26)00250-X/abstract)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — study design and follow-up horizon both overstated.** The study is real: Annals of Thoracic Surgery, published April 2026 (S0003-4975(26)00250-X), presented at the Thirty-third Annual Meeting of the Asian Society for Cardiovascular and Thoracic Surgery in May 2025, and its title does report a long-term survival advantage for reoperative surgery. It is a RETROSPECTIVE COHORT of 229 patients at TWO quaternary centres — not large, not a registry, not a trial. ### 6. Extended Arch Versus Hemiarch Replacement in Older Patients With Type A Dissection

*Interdisciplinary CardioVascular and Thoracic Surgery · January 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A retrospective cohort from the Shizuoka Kokuho Database — a Japanese prefecture-wide, multi-institutional administrative claims database — identified patients aged 60 or older with acute type A aortic dissection who underwent hemiarch or extended arch replacement between April 2012 and September 2022. Of 774 patients (174 extended arch, 600 hemiarch), 167 propensity-matched pairs were analysed, and Kaplan-Meier curves showed no significant difference in survival between the procedures. Wider meta-analytic data — 23 observational studies, 4,576 patients — find hemiarch associated with lower early mortality, less permanent neurological dysfunction and less dialysis-requiring renal failure, but a HIGHER rate of late mortality.

**Clinical guideline impact.** In older patients, matched analysis shows no survival penalty for the less extensive operation. Given that hemiarch carries lower early mortality and less neurological injury, that supports the simpler repair as the default when the dissection anatomy allows it.

**Practical implications.** Two facts have to sit together rather than be chosen between: hemiarch is safer early, and across the broader literature it carries a higher late mortality — the matched elderly cohort finds no survival difference, which is the specific circumstance in which the trade-off resolves in favour of the simpler operation. That resolution is age-dependent, so do not carry it to a 45-year-old, where late reintervention on a residual dissected arch is the dominant risk. The extent of repair still follows the anatomy: an arch tear or a dissection extending well into the arch is not a hemiarch. Claims-database data cannot see tear location, so this study cannot tell you which patients needed the bigger operation.

[Comparison of extended arch versus hemiarch replacement in elderly patients with type A aortic dissection: the Shizuoka Kokuho Database](https://academic.oup.com/icvts/article/41/1/ivag017/8419899)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — right conclusion, wrong registry, wrong forum, wrong age cut-off.** The finding of no survival difference is confirmed, but nothing about the attribution was correct. The study is Arima, Sato and Tanaka, Interdisciplinary CardioVascular and Thoracic Surgery, published 10 January 2026, volume 41 issue 1, ivag017 — a retrospective analysis of the SHIZUOKA KOKUHO DATABASE, a Japanese prefecture-wide administrative CLAIMS database, in patients aged ≥60, 774 patients with 167 propensity-matched pairs. ### 7. Diaphragm Dysfunction After Congenital Heart Surgery: Epidemiology and Outcomes

*The Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** An analysis of 77,383 patients undergoing an index congenital cardiac operation identified diaphragm dysfunction in 1,130 (1.5%), of whom 510 (45%) underwent plication. Median day of diagnosis was postoperative day 7 (IQR 4–13) and median day of plication was postoperative day 14 (IQR 9–24). Patients with diaphragm dysfunction had longer mechanical ventilation, more major complications and longer cardiac ICU and hospital stays. Patients who underwent plication did not have higher rates of major complications — the procedure itself was not associated with added harm.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It puts numbers on a complication that has been managed by local habit: 1.5% incidence, diagnosis around a week, plication around two weeks, and just under half of affected infants operated on. The morbidity attributable to diaphragm dysfunction itself is substantial.

**Practical implications.** What this study does NOT show is that plicating earlier improves outcomes, and the wider evidence cautions against assuming it. A comparison of aggressive early plication against conservative management found no significant difference in ventilation time (6 versus 8 days, P=0.36), and plicated and non-plicated patients regained diaphragm function at similar rates (60% versus 54.8%); excursion of a plicated diaphragm reaches about 77% of the contralateral side. So the honest position is that plication is safe and reasonable in an infant who cannot be weaned, and that it is not established to shorten ventilation compared with waiting for recovery. Diagnose it deliberately — the median postoperative day 7 detection suggests it is often found late, on a failed wean rather than looked for.

[Diaphragm dysfunction following congenital heart surgery: epidemiology and outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41765325/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED on benefit — a descriptive epidemiology study was presented as proving early plication works.** The study is real and its figures check out: 77,383 index operations, 1,130 (1.5%) with diaphragm dysfunction, 510 (45%) plicated, median diagnosis POD 7, median plication POD 14, worse ventilation/complications/length of stay with dysfunction, no excess major complications from plication itself. ### 8. COVID-19-Positive Donor Hearts in Pediatric Transplantation: Three-Year Outcomes

*The Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery · 2025–2026*

**The breakthrough.** A query of the Standard Transplant Analysis and Research database covering primary paediatric (under 18) heart transplants from 2020 to 2024 identified 1,978 transplants, of which 60 (3.0%) used COVID-19-positive donor hearts. Three-year survival was 71.5% for recipients of COVID-19-positive hearts against 86.3% for COVID-19-negative. Mortality did not differ at 6 months, 1 year or 1.5 years — the divergence appeared only at 2 and 3 years. Recipients of positive hearts were younger (5.7 versus 7.6 years, P=.019), received organs from greater distances (360 versus 303 miles, P=.044) and had more peritransplant ECMO use. Mortality risk was magnified by height mismatch (P=.048). Graft failure was rare and its cumulative risk was not significant.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A nearly 15-percentage-point survival gap at three years in a population that should be doing well, with no signal at all in the first eighteen months, is the kind of late divergence a shorter study would have declared safe.

**Practical implications.** The authors' own conclusion is the right one to carry: until more data accrue, exercise caution in accepting COVID-19-positive donor hearts for paediatric recipients. Read the late timing as a warning about follow-up length rather than as a mechanism — that graft failure was rare while mortality rose means the deaths are not straightforwardly explained by the graft, and the confounders in the table (younger recipients, longer distances, more ECMO) describe sicker children receiving organs under more pressure. Height mismatch amplified the risk, so the combination of a positive donor and a size mismatch is the one to decline. This is registry data on 60 exposed patients: it is a reason for caution, not a prohibition, and the decision is still made against the mortality of remaining on the waiting list.

### 9. DIPNECH and Multifocal Pulmonary Neuroendocrine Disease: No Guideline Exists

*Current Treatment Options in Oncology (review) · current state*

**The breakthrough.** Diffuse idiopathic pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia is an idiopathic proliferation of pulmonary neuroendocrine cells that causes bronchiolitis and can give rise to multifocal lung neuroendocrine tumours, typically presenting with chronic cough and dyspnoea. There are NO evidence-based management guidelines. Somatostatin analogues can palliate symptoms, particularly cough. Surgical resection is considered for individual larger — for example above 1 cm — progressive carcinoid tumours, and published surgical experience consists of case reports and small series of wedge resections or lobectomy.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The honest state of the field is the finding here. DIPNECH is a diffuse airway process; the resectable component is the discrete carcinoid tumour that arises within it, not the hyperplasia itself, and there is no series large enough to define a surgical algorithm.

**Practical implications.** Framing matters because the disease is diffuse and indolent: resection treats a specific progressive nodule, it does not treat DIPNECH, and a patient whose dominant problem is cough is more likely to be helped by a somatostatin analogue than by an operation. Because the process is bilateral and multifocal, every resection spends parenchyma the patient may need for a future one — which is a strong argument for the most limited adequate resection and a strong argument against a large one. Multidisciplinary review with a neuroendocrine tumour service, serial imaging, and treating the airway disease medically is the defensible pathway; the absence of guidelines means the burden of justification sits on the decision to operate, not on the decision to wait.

### 10. DOACs Versus Warfarin for Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation After Cardiac Surgery

*Multiple (meta-analyses) · 2021–2026*

**The breakthrough.** A meta-analysis of randomised and observational studies totalling 16,902 patients found direct oral anticoagulants associated with lower bleeding risk than warfarin (relative risk 0.74, 95% CI 0.62–0.89) and fewer major neurological events, with no difference in mortality. A separate synthesis found DOACs associated with lower stroke risk, a trend toward less systemic embolisation, and no increase in readmission, bleeding or mortality. North American registry data show factor Xa inhibitors have been prescribed more often than vitamin K antagonists for postoperative atrial fibrillation since about 2020 — practice moved ahead of the guidelines.

**Clinical guideline impact.** For the patient discharged in atrial fibrillation after cardiac surgery, the accumulated comparison favours a DOAC on bleeding and neurological events, without a mortality difference either way — and removes the INR monitoring burden in a patient already managing a complex postoperative recovery.

**Practical implications.** The unsettled question is not which agent but WHETHER to anticoagulate at all, and for how long: postoperative atrial fibrillation is often transient, the stroke risk attributable to it is not the same as in non-surgical AF, and no trial has established the duration. That is the conversation to have, not the choice of drug. Where a DOAC is chosen, the practical caveats are the ordinary ones and they bite in this population: renal function determines dose and eligibility, a mechanical valve rules DOACs out, and recent pericardial or mediastinal bleeding changes the calculus. Confirm the AF is documented and not a monitor artefact before committing someone to months of anticoagulation.
