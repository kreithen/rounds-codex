### 1. Randomized Trial of Ureteral Stent Omission After Ureteroscopy for Renal Stones

*Canadian Urological Association Journal / AUA 2025 · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre randomised controlled trial assigned 74 patients undergoing ureteroscopy and laser lithotripsy for non-obstructing renal stones up to 1.5 cm total diameter to stent placement or stent omission. The primary outcome — 30-day emergency department visits, unanticipated provider visits or hospitalisation — was not increased by omitting the stent. Secondary outcomes covered WISQOL and PROMIS 6a scores, return to work, symptom-driven phone calls, postoperative narcotic use, unintentional stent dislodgement, UTI and hydronephrosis at 4–8 weeks. Quality of life was better without a stent, and patients who had no stent were far more likely to say they would definitely undergo the procedure again.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Stent discomfort is the part of stone surgery patients remember. A randomised comparison showing no safety penalty from omission, with markedly better patient-reported outcomes, shifts the default question from whether to stent to whether there is a reason to.

**Practical implications.** Note the size: 74 randomised patients. That is enough to detect a large difference in a common outcome and not enough to exclude an uncommon serious one, so this supports omission in the population studied rather than a blanket policy. The eligibility criteria are the whole entry — UNCOMPLICATED ureteroscopy, NON-OBSTRUCTING renal stones, up to 1.5 cm total burden. Ureteral stones, obstruction, ureteral injury, significant oedema after a difficult access, residual fragments, solitary kidney and infection are all outside it and still warrant a stent. Have a clear route back for the patient who obstructs after a stentless case.

[Randomized, multicenter trial of ureteral stent placement vs. stent omission after ureteroscopy for renal stones](https://cuaj.ca/index.php/journal/article/view/9761)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — trial confirmed, but it is small and no guideline change has occurred.** Verified: multicentre randomised trial presented at AUA 2025 and published in the Canadian Urological Association Journal; stent omission after uncomplicated ureteroscopy for non-obstructing renal stones ≤1.5 cm was safe with no increase in 30-day complications or unplanned healthcare use, and with better quality-of-life and satisfaction measures. The submission's clinical claim is accurate. Two corrections. (1) SIZE: the submission omits that only 74 patients were randomised, which is the fact that determines how far the conclusion travels — restored, with what it can and cannot exclude. (2) The submission states the trial 'PROMPTS MAJOR UPDATES to AUA and EAU Endourology practice guidelines to establish stent omission as a PRIMARY RECOMMENDATION'. No such guideline change could be verified, and a 74-patient trial would not support a primary recommendation; removed. Added the exclusion criteria explicitly — the submission said 'uncomplicated' once and then generalised to 'uncomplicated stone procedures', which would sweep in ureteral stones and obstructed systems that this trial did not study.

### 2. PSMAfore: 177Lu-PSMA-617 Before Chemotherapy in mCRPC

*Annals of Oncology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** PSMAfore randomised 468 patients with PSMA-positive metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer who had progressed on a second-generation androgen receptor pathway inhibitor and had not received a taxane, to 177Lu-PSMA-617 or a change of ARPI. Median radiographic progression-free survival was 9.3 months with radioligand therapy against 5.6 months with the ARPI switch (HR 0.41), meeting the primary endpoint. On 28 March 2025 the FDA expanded the label to adults with PSMA-positive mCRPC previously treated with an ARPI in whom it is appropriate to delay taxane chemotherapy.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It moves radioligand therapy ahead of chemotherapy in the sequence, for men who have exhausted one androgen receptor pathway inhibitor and for whom the alternative was switching to another with modest expected benefit.

**Practical implications.** The overall survival analysis favoured 177Lu-PSMA-617 but did NOT reach statistical significance, which the final analysis attributes to the high rate of crossover from the control arm — so what is established is a delay in radiographic progression, not a demonstrated survival gain, and the consent conversation should say so. The comparator matters too: the control was another ARPI switch, a treatment of limited efficacy, so this is not a comparison against taxane chemotherapy. Selection requires PSMA-positive imaging, and the practical constraints are access to a radiopharmacy, salivary and marrow toxicity, and the radiation-safety instructions the patient and household have to follow.

[Final overall survival and safety analyses of the phase III PSMAfore trial](https://www.annalsofoncology.org/article/S0923-7534(25)00852-X/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — rPFS accurate, non-significant overall survival omitted.** Verified: PSMAfore, 468 patients, taxane-naive PSMA-positive mCRPC progressing on a second-generation ARPI, median rPFS 9.3 versus 5.6 months, HR 0.41; FDA label expansion 28 March 2025. The submission is accurate in confining its efficacy claim to radiographic progression-free survival, which is more careful than most entries in this review. The correction is an omission: the final overall survival analysis favoured 177Lu-PSMA-617 but was NOT statistically significant, attributed to high crossover. The submission's practical section asserts the approach 'preserv[es] bone marrow function and quality of life' — marrow preservation relative to taxane is a reasonable rationale but was not an established endpoint here, and radioligand therapy has its own marrow and salivary toxicity, so that has been reframed. Added that the comparator was an ARPI switch rather than chemotherapy, which bounds how the 9.3-month figure should be read, and the radiopharmacy/radiation-safety logistics the submission did not mention.

### 3. IMvigor011: ctDNA-Guided Adjuvant Atezolizumab After Cystectomy

*New England Journal of Medicine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** IMvigor011 enrolled 756 patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer into surveillance 6 to 24 weeks after cystectomy, with serial circulating tumour DNA testing for molecular residual disease for up to a year. Of 379 who became ctDNA-positive at any point, 250 were evaluable and randomised to atezolizumab (n=167) or placebo. Adjuvant atezolizumab significantly improved both disease-free and overall survival, with a 36% lower risk of recurrence — whether ctDNA positivity was present immediately after surgery or emerged later during screening. Patients who remained persistently ctDNA-negative and received no adjuvant therapy had a low risk of recurrence or death. Grade 3–4 adverse events occurred in 28% on atezolizumab and 22% on placebo.

**Clinical guideline impact.** This is the first randomised evidence that a molecular residual disease biomarker can direct adjuvant immunotherapy — treat the patients whose blood says they have residual disease, and spare the rest. Presented at ESMO 2025 and published in the NEJM.

**Practical implications.** The two halves of the result carry different weights. That ctDNA-positive patients benefit from atezolizumab is randomised evidence. That ctDNA-negative patients can safely be observed is an observational finding within the trial — they were not randomised to treatment versus observation — so 'low risk' is not 'no risk', and surveillance has to continue. The design detail that makes it work is SERIAL testing for up to a year: a single negative postoperative sample is not what was studied, and positivity that emerged later still identified patients who benefited. Implementation needs a defined testing schedule and someone owning the results, or a conversion is missed.

[ctDNA-guided adjuvant atezolizumab in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (IMvigor011)](https://www.urotoday.com/conference-highlights/esmo-2025/esmo-2025-bladder-cancer/164149-esmo-2025-imvigor011-a-phase-3-trial-of-ctdna-guided-adjuvant-atezolizumab-versus-placebo-in-mibc.html)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched — verified, one of the accurate submissions in this batch.** Verified in full: IMvigor011, phase 3, 756 patients into post-cystectomy surveillance with serial ctDNA for up to 1 year, 379 ctDNA-positive at any time, 250 evaluable and randomised (atezolizumab n=167), significant improvement in both disease-free and overall survival, 36% lower recurrence risk, low recurrence risk in persistently ctDNA-negative patients, grade 3–4 AEs 28% versus 22%; presented ESMO 2025 (LBA8) and published in the New England Journal of Medicine. The submission's description — including that ctDNA-positive patients derived a major overall survival benefit and ctDNA-negative patients maintained high disease-free survival without adjuvant therapy — matches the published trial. Additions rather than corrections: the exact figures, the grade 3–4 toxicity rate, and the point that the ctDNA-negative arm was NOT randomised, so 'eliminates overtreatment' (the submission's phrasing) rests on within-trial observation rather than randomised comparison. Also made explicit that serial testing over a year, not a single postoperative sample, is what was validated.

### 4. SunRISe-1: TAR-200 Intravesical Gemcitabine System in BCG-Unresponsive NMIBC

*Journal of Clinical Oncology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** SunRISe-1 is a phase 2b study of TAR-200, an intravesical device that releases gemcitabine continuously into the bladder. In cohort 2 — 85 patients with BCG-unresponsive high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer with carcinoma in situ, with or without papillary disease — TAR-200 monotherapy produced a complete response rate of 82.4%, the highest single-agent complete response rate reported in this population. Responses were rapid, at a median 2.8 months to onset, and durable: median duration of response 25.8 months, with 51% maintaining complete response for at least a year. The FDA has approved TAR-200 for BCG-unresponsive NMIBC with CIS.

**Clinical guideline impact.** BCG-unresponsive CIS has meant radical cystectomy for most patients. A complete response rate above 80% with a median response duration over two years is a genuine bladder-sparing option rather than a delaying tactic.

**Practical implications.** This is a phase 2b single-arm study — there is no randomised comparison against cystectomy, and cystectomy remains the treatment with known long-term cancer-specific survival. The trade being offered is a high chance of a durable complete response against the risk that a non-responder or a late relapse progresses to muscle-invasive disease while the bladder is being preserved, which is the outcome that cannot be undone. That makes surveillance intensity, and a pre-agreed threshold for abandoning bladder preservation, part of the treatment plan from the start. The elderly or frail patient for whom cystectomy and diversion carry high operative risk is the clearest beneficiary; a fit 60-year-old is a genuine shared decision.

[TAR-200 for BCG-unresponsive high-risk non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer: results from the phase IIb SunRISe-1 study](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO-25-01651)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — response rate verified, trial phase and guideline claim adjusted.** Verified: SunRISe-1, published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology, cohort 2 of 85 patients with BCG-unresponsive high-risk NMIBC with CIS, complete response 82.4% (reported as 82.8% in some coverage), median time to response 2.8 months, median duration of response 25.8 months, 51% maintaining CR at ≥1 year, FDA approved. The submission's 'exceeding 80%' is right. Corrections. (1) PHASE: the submission calls it 'registration trial data' for 'SunRISe-1 / SunRISe-5'; SunRISe-1 is a PHASE 2B SINGLE-ARM study, and SunRISe-5 is a separate trial in a different population — conflating them overstates the evidence, so the entry is confined to SunRISe-1. (2) The submission says it 'updates AUA and EAU NMIBC guidelines to position device-assisted intravesical delivery systems as a primary bladder-preserving alternative to radical cystectomy'; FDA approval is verified but the guideline repositioning is not, so the approval is stated instead. Added the durability figures the submission omitted and the substantive caveat it skipped: no randomised comparison against cystectomy exists, and progression to muscle-invasive disease during bladder preservation is the irreversible outcome that has to be planned for.

### 5. BOND-003: Intravesical Cretostimogene Grenadenorepvec in BCG-Unresponsive NMIBC

*Phase 3 (EAU 2025 / published) · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** BOND-003 is a phase 3 single-arm trial of intravesical cretostimogene grenadenorepvec — an oncolytic adenovirus that selectively replicates in and lyses cells with Rb-E2F pathway alterations, releasing viral and tumour antigens to trigger antitumour immunity, potentiated by a GM-CSF transgene. In 112 patients with high-risk BCG-unresponsive NMIBC with carcinoma in situ, the complete response rate at any time was 75.5%. The 24-month complete response rate was 41.8%, with 90% of 12-month responders maintaining response, and the cystectomy-free rate was 84.5% at 24 months. There were no grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Two bladder-sparing options for BCG-unresponsive CIS now have phase 3 or registrational data — this and TAR-200 — where recently there were none. The tolerability here is the standout: no grade 3 or worse treatment-related events in 112 patients.

**Practical implications.** Read the two response numbers together. 75.5% complete response at any time and 41.8% at 24 months describe the same cohort at different points, and the second is the one that answers what a patient wants to know. Even so, an 84.5% cystectomy-free rate at two years is the number that captures the clinical value — most patients still have their bladder. The mechanism requires an intact Rb-E2F alteration for selectivity, and administration is intravesical and office-based, so the practical demands are low. As with any bladder-preserving strategy in CIS, the surveillance schedule and the pre-agreed point at which cystectomy is triggered belong in the initial plan, not in the discussion after a relapse.

[BOND-003 cohort C: phase 3 study of intravesical cretostimogene grenadenorepvec for high-risk BCG-unresponsive NMIBC with CIS](https://www.urotoday.com/conference-highlights/eau-2025/eau-2025-bladder-cancer/159231-eau-2025-updated-clinical-translational-results-bond-003-cohort-c-a-phase-3-single-arm-study-of-intravesical-cretostimogene-grenadenorepvec-for-high-risk-bcg-unresponsive-nmibc-with-cis.html)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched — verified, with durability figures added.** Verified: BOND-003, phase 3 single-arm, 112 patients with high-risk BCG-unresponsive NMIBC with CIS, complete response 75.5% at any time — the submission's figure is exactly right. Added from the source and missing from the submission: 24-month CR 41.8%, 90% of 12-month responders maintaining response, cystectomy-free rate 84.5% at 24 months, no grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events, and the Rb-E2F selectivity mechanism with the GM-CSF transgene. The 41.8% at 24 months matters because the submission quoted only the any-time 75.5%, which reads as sustained control. Two adjustments: the submission describes it as 'Final results' — the located data are updated results including 24-month follow-up, so 'final' is not asserted; and 'Integrates intravesical oncolytic virus immunotherapy into AUA and EAU guidelines' could not be verified and has been replaced with the factual state (phase 3 data available, bladder-sparing option). The submission's 'induces systemic antitumor immunity' is retained since the mechanism is documented, but is described as the mechanism rather than a measured clinical effect.

### 6. PROBE (SWOG S1931): Cytoreductive Nephrectomy in the Immunotherapy Era — Ongoing

*SWOG (trial in progress) · opened 2021, recruiting*

**The breakthrough.** PROBE (SWOG S1931) is a phase 3 randomised trial asking whether cytoreductive nephrectomy adds benefit to modern immune checkpoint inhibitor-based systemic therapy in metastatic renal cell carcinoma. Patients receive systemic therapy first and are then randomised 1:1, after a 12-week disease response assessment, either to cytoreductive nephrectomy followed by continued systemic therapy or to systemic therapy alone — with randomisation stratified by pre-randomisation immunotherapy status, performance status, systemic regimen and 12-week response. It opened on 8 March 2021 and is RECRUITING. No results have been reported.

**Clinical guideline impact.** This is the question the specialty needs answered. CARMENA and SURTIME addressed cytoreductive nephrectomy in the targeted-therapy era; no trial has assessed it against checkpoint inhibitor-based combinations, which is what patients now receive.

**Practical implications.** Because there is no result, current practice has to rest on what is already known: CARMENA showed sunitinib alone was non-inferior to nephrectomy followed by sunitinib in intermediate- and poor-risk disease, and cytoreductive nephrectomy is now generally reserved for selected patients — good performance status, low metastatic burden, a large symptomatic primary, or a good response to systemic therapy that leaves the kidney as the dominant residual site. The design contains its own clinical lesson: PROBE randomises AFTER an initial systemic therapy response assessment, which is how the decision is increasingly made in practice — treat first, then decide about the kidney. Refer eligible patients to the trial rather than deciding on inference.

[PROBE (SWOG S1931): immunotherapy-based combination therapy with or without cytoreductive nephrectomy](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04510597)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND — the trial is real but has NO RESULTS; the submission reports findings that do not exist.** PROBE / SWOG S1931 is real (NCT04510597, opened 8 March 2021) and its design is as the submission describes, but the trial status as of September 2025 is RECRUITING and no results have been reported. The submission is written in the past tense — 'The randomized Phase III PROBE trial EVALUATED whether upfront cytoreductive nephrectomy... improves survival' — and then states outcomes: that it 'refines NCCN and EAU renal cell carcinoma practice guidelines, ESTABLISHING clear surgical selection criteria' and 'IDENTIFYING specific patient subgroups (such as those with low metastatic burden) who GAIN A SURVIVAL ADVANTAGE from tumor debulking'. None of that has happened. This is the second entry in the 2025 urology list describing results from a trial that has not reported (see WATER IV), and reporting a subgroup survival advantage from an unreported trial is the most consequential form of the error, because it would justify operating on a patient. Entry rewritten as an ongoing trial, with current practice stated from CARMENA and the existing selection criteria so the entry is still useful, and with the design's own implicit lesson — randomise after the systemic therapy response — kept because it is real and it is how the decision is made.

### 7. CAPTAIN: MRI-Guided TULSA Versus Robotic Radical Prostatectomy

*Randomized trial (perioperative and early functional results) · 2025–2026*

**The breakthrough.** CAPTAIN randomised men with organ-confined, intermediate-risk Gleason 7 (Grade Group 2 and 3) prostate cancer to MRI-guided transurethral ultrasound ablation or robotic radical prostatectomy, treating 212 men at 23 centres between 2022 and 2025 and exceeding its enrolment target. Reported results are perioperative and early functional: less blood loss, same-day discharge for most, less pain and faster return to normal activities at one month. On penile length there was NO median change at one month after TULSA against a median 0.65 cm reduction after robotic prostatectomy. Early continence and erectile function measures favoured TULSA.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A randomised comparison of a whole-gland ablative technique against the standard operation, in a defined intermediate-risk population, is what this field has lacked. The early functional and recovery advantages are consistent and measured, not inferred.

**Practical implications.** LONG-TERM ONCOLOGIC EFFICACY DATA REMAIN PENDING — that is the trial's own position, and it is the only thing a patient choosing between these two treatments needs to weigh against better early function. Prostatectomy has decades of cancer-specific survival data; TULSA has one month of quality-of-life advantage and no mature oncologic comparison. Do not present them as equivalent on cancer control. Note also what prostatectomy provides that ablation cannot: whole-gland pathology, which upgrades or upstages a meaningful minority and changes adjuvant decisions. The eligible population is narrow — organ-confined, intermediate-risk, Grade Group 2–3 — and it excludes high-risk disease entirely.

[CAPTAIN trial — early advantages of TULSA versus prostatectomy](https://www.urologytimes.com/view/captain-trial-highlights-early-advantages-of-tulsa-vs-prostatectomy)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the submission asserts equal 2-year oncologic control; oncologic data are not yet reported.** The trial is real: CAPTAIN, 212 men treated at 23 centres 2022–2025, organ-confined intermediate-risk Gleason 7 (GG2–3), randomised against robotic radical prostatectomy. The reported results are PERIOPERATIVE and EARLY FUNCTIONAL — less blood loss, same-day discharge, less pain, faster return to activity at one month, no median penile length change versus a 0.65 cm median reduction after prostatectomy — and the sources state explicitly that LONG-TERM ONCOLOGIC EFFICACY DATA REMAIN PENDING. The submission claims 'MRI-guided TULSA achieved EQUAL 2-YEAR ONCOLOGIC CONTROL compared to robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy'. That result has not been reported, and it is the single claim on which a man's decision between ablation and surgery turns. The submission's '(98%)' continence figure also could not be verified and has been removed; the verified statement is that early continence and erectile function measures favoured TULSA. 'Evaluated by AUA/EAU panels to establish TULSA as a primary organ-preserving alternative' is not a guideline position and has been dropped. Added the loss of whole-gland pathology, which is a real cost of any ablative approach and was absent from the submission.

### 8. EMBARK and PRESTO: ARSI Plus ADT in High-Risk Biochemical Recurrence

*New England Journal of Medicine · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** EMBARK randomised men with non-metastatic biochemical recurrence and a PSA doubling time under nine months to enzalutamide plus leuprolide, leuprolide alone, or enzalutamide monotherapy, with treatment suspension if PSA fell below 0.2. At eight years, overall survival was 78.9% with enzalutamide plus leuprolide against 69.5% with leuprolide alone (HR 0.597, 95% CI 0.444–0.804, P=.0006) — a 40% reduction in risk of death, on top of the previously reported metastasis-free survival benefit. Enzalutamide monotherapy gave a hazard ratio of 0.83 that did not reach significance. PRESTO separately found ADT plus apalutamide improved metastasis-free survival and delayed castration resistance in the same clinical setting.

**Clinical guideline impact.** An overall survival benefit in non-metastatic biochemical recurrence establishes enzalutamide plus ADT as a standard of care in high-risk relapse, where the previous choice was ADT alone or observation.

**Practical implications.** The two trials support different claims and should not be merged. EMBARK has an overall survival benefit for the enzalutamide–ADT doublet; PRESTO's apalutamide result is metastasis-free survival and delayed castration resistance, not overall survival. Enzalutamide monotherapy was not significant, so the doublet is the regimen with the evidence. The finite duration and the planned treatment suspension when PSA falls below 0.2 are part of the design, not an informal concession — that is what makes the toxicity acceptable in men who are asymptomatic and may have years of life ahead. Selection is defined by PSA doubling time under nine months and non-metastatic conventional imaging; PSMA-PET will find disease that conventional imaging does not, which does not automatically move a patient out of this evidence base but does need a considered decision.

[Improved survival with enzalutamide in biochemically recurrent prostate cancer](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMoa2510310)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — EMBARK verified, but the apalutamide claim is a different endpoint.** Verified: EMBARK, NEJM 2025 (NEJMoa2510310), presented at ESMO 2025 — 8-year overall survival 78.9% versus 69.5% for enzalutamide plus leuprolide against leuprolide alone, HR 0.597 (0.444–0.804), P=.0006; enzalutamide monotherapy HR 0.83, not significant; entry criteria of PSA doubling time under nine months and non-metastatic disease on conventional imaging; treatment suspension if PSA fell below 0.2. PRESTO's final results support ADT plus apalutamide for metastasis-free survival and delayed castration resistance. The correction is precision, not direction: the submission writes 'finite-duration Enzalutamide (OR APALUTAMIDE) combined with ADT significantly extends SURVIVAL', attributing an overall survival benefit to both drugs. Only EMBARK demonstrated overall survival; PRESTO's endpoint is metastasis-free survival. Also corrected: 'post-surgery or radiation' is right but the submission omits that enzalutamide MONOTHERAPY failed to reach significance, which is what makes the doublet — not the drug — the standard. Added the PSMA-PET tension, since a patient staged with PSMA-PET no longer matches the trial's conventional-imaging entry criteria and clinicians face that mismatch daily.

### 9. WATER IV: Aquablation Versus Radical Prostatectomy — Trial in Progress

*Investigational device exemption trial (no results) · enrolment complete, completion expected July 2027*

**The breakthrough.** WATER IV PCa (NCT06651632) is a global, multicentre, prospective investigational device exemption trial randomising patients with Grade Group 1 to 3 localised prostate cancer between first-line Aquablation therapy — robotically controlled waterjet resection of the diagnosed cancer and most of the prostate — and radical prostatectomy, with follow-up to 10 years. Enrolment of 280 patients is complete. The PRIMARY ENDPOINTS ARE URINARY INCONTINENCE AND ERECTILE DYSFUNCTION RATES, not oncologic control. Trial completion is anticipated in July 2027, and no results have been reported.

**Clinical guideline impact.** It is the first randomised test of a waterjet resection platform, established in benign prostatic hyperplasia, against radical prostatectomy for cancer. What it will answer is a functional question; what it will take a decade to answer is the oncologic one.

**Practical implications.** Nothing here supports offering Aquablation for prostate cancer outside the trial. Aquablation's approved indication is BPH, and the primary endpoints of WATER IV are functional — so even a positive result will describe continence and potency, not cancer control, and the 10-year follow-up is what would eventually address survival. The design point worth understanding is that this is not focal therapy: the protocol resects the cancer and most of the prostate, which is a different proposition from HIFU or cryotherapy applied to a single lesion, and it should not be grouped with them. Refer interested patients to the trial.

[WATER IV PCa — Aquablation versus radical prostatectomy in localized prostate cancer](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06651632)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND — the trial has NO RESULTS and does not measure what the submission claims.** WATER IV is real (NCT06651632, IDE trial, up to 280 patients with Grade Group 1–3 localised prostate cancer randomised to Aquablation or radical prostatectomy, follow-up to 10 years) but enrolment completion is the only milestone reached and COMPLETION IS ANTICIPATED IN JULY 2027 — no results exist. The submission presents it as a completed 'Multi-center Phase III trial evaluating' the comparison and then makes efficacy claims: that it 'prompts guidelines to expand Aquablation indications beyond BPH into targeted focal therapy for localized prostate cancer' and 'DELIVERS rapid, heat-free tissue resection that ELIMINATES localized cancerous lesions while avoiding thermal injury to surrounding erectile nerves and the urinary sphincter'. Two further errors compound it. (1) The trial's PRIMARY ENDPOINTS are urinary incontinence and erectile dysfunction — functional, not oncologic — so even on reporting it will not establish that the technique eliminates cancer. (2) The submission calls it 'targeted FOCAL therapy'; the protocol resects the cancer and most of the prostate, so grouping it with focal HIFU or cryotherapy is wrong. Entry rewritten as a trial in progress with the endpoints stated plainly.

### 10. Thulium Fiber Laser Versus Holmium:YAG for Renal Stone Lithotripsy

*Journal of Urology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A prospective randomised controlled trial compared SuperPulse thulium fiber laser against pulse-modulated high-power holmium:YAG for retrograde intrarenal surgery, and a multicentre randomised trial at four institutions compared the two for dusting renal stones of 5–20 mm during flexible ureteroscopy. The randomised finding was no discernible difference between the platforms in CT-assessed stone-free rate, laser efficiency or complications. Meta-analytic data — drawn largely from non-randomised comparisons — point the other way, reporting a higher stone-free rate with thulium fiber laser (OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.20–2.06, P=0.001) along with shorter operating time and less stone retropulsion.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Where randomised trials and meta-analysis of observational studies disagree, the randomised answer governs: on current evidence the two lasers are equivalent for stone clearance. That frees a department to choose on cost, fibre consumables, ergonomics and generator footprint rather than on a claimed efficacy advantage.

**Practical implications.** The honest summary for a purchasing conversation is that no randomised trial has shown one laser clears stones better than the other, and that the meta-analytic signal favouring thulium comes from studies with the selection problems that come with non-randomised comparison. Retropulsion and fibre burn-back are real practical differences between the platforms and worth weighing on their own terms. Note that this evidence is about stone DUSTING of 5–20 mm renal stones — it does not settle the separate question of dusting versus basketing fragments, which has a larger effect on residual fragment burden than the choice of laser.

[Prospective randomized controlled trial comparing SuperPulse thulium fiber laser vs pulse-modulated high-power holmium:YAG for retrograde intrarenal surgery](https://www.auajournals.org/doi/10.1097/JU.0000000000004310)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT FOUND for the fluid-protocol half — no PUSH trial exists; the laser half is real.** Two claims were bundled in this entry. (1) The laser comparison is real and the submission's conclusion is supported by the randomised evidence: a prospective RCT (J Urol, PubMed 39918120) and a multicentre randomised trial across four institutions found no discernible difference between SuperPulse thulium fiber laser and pulse-modulated high-power holmium:YAG in stone-free rate, laser efficiency or complications for 5–20 mm renal stones. Added the countervailing meta-analytic finding (OR 1.57 favouring TFL) with the reason the randomised result should govern — the submission presented equivalence as settled without acknowledging the disagreement. (2) The 'PUSH trial' proving 'forced fluid hyper-hydration did not improve stone-free rates' could NOT be found under that or any related name, and no trial of a postoperative forced-hydration protocol and stone-free rate could be located. The submission's practical claim that it 'relieves patients from burdensome post-procedure fluid protocols' therefore rests on nothing, and has been removed rather than shipped — advising against a fluid protocol on the authority of a non-existent trial is a live clinical instruction. Entry retitled to what is verifiable. Added the dusting-versus-basketing point, which affects residual fragments more than the laser choice does.

### 1. AUA 2026 Guideline on BPH/LUTS: Expanded Procedural Options

*The Journal of Urology · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** The American Urological Association published a multi-part guideline on management of lower urinary tract symptoms attributed to benign prostatic hyperplasia, with Part III covering procedural and surgical management. Prostatic artery embolisation was UPGRADED from Grade C to Grade B evidence and established as a legitimate option for prostates of 50 cc or more, including patients on anticoagulation at higher bleeding risk from conventional surgery. Aquablation evidence was extended into the 80–150 mL range. Intraprostatic drug-coated balloon may be offered for prostates of 20–80 cc with a prostatic urethral length of 32–55 mm and no obstructing median lobe. Separately, the EAU upgraded Aquablation to a strong recommendation as an alternative to TURP in moderate-to-severe BPH symptoms in March 2026.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The large prostate has been the limiting case for minimally invasive options, pushing patients toward open or endoscopic enucleation. Extending Aquablation to 150 mL and legitimising embolisation above 50 cc changes what can be offered before that point.

The submission states that 'Results from the prospective multi-center CURATE-UTUC trial PRESENTED AT EAU 2026 SHOWED that longitudinal monitoring of paired urine tumor DNA and circulating tumor DNA RELIABLY IDENTIFIES minimal residual disease and PREDICTS RECURRENCE following radical nephroureterectomy.' No such presentation or result could be found — this is the third entry across the two urology lists reporting results from a trial that has not reported (with PROBE and WATER IV). Entry rebuilt on what is published: a cohort study reporting baseline ctDNA positivity 69.2% and utDNA 88.9% falling to 46.2% and 21.4% one month postoperatively, with combined detection described as PRELIMINARY predictive biomarkers; and a separate finding that PREOPERATIVE plasma ctDNA predicts muscle-invasive/non-organ-confined disease and is prognostic for progression-free and cancer-specific survival. The preoperative application has been foregrounded because it is better evidenced than the postoperative MRD use the submission described, and because it addresses the specific clinical trap in this disease — that nephroureterectomy removes the renal function cisplatin requires, so neoadjuvant decisions cannot be deferred. The submission's claim that this 'facilitates timely delivery of post-operative adjuvant therapies' is not supported by any reported trial.
