## 2025

### 1. Endovascular Therapy for Medium and Distal Vessel Occlusion (ESCAPE-MeVO, DISTAL, DISCOUNT)

*NEJM · February 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Three randomised trials tested thrombectomy against best medical management in medium and distal vessel occlusion. All were NEGATIVE: no improvement in 90-day functional independence. ESCAPE-MeVO randomised 530 patients within 12 hours of last seen normal.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The investigators state their results 'do not support routine endovascular treatment of acute ischaemic stroke due to medium-vessel occlusion.' The accompanying NEJM editorial asked whether this is 'the limit of thrombectomy'. Best medical therapy, including intravenous thrombolysis, remains the standard for non-large-vessel occlusion.

**Practical implications.** Keeps acute stroke workflow focused on proximal large vessel occlusion. The technical ability to reach a distal branch did not translate into patient benefit — less territory at risk, more procedural risk per unit of tissue saved.

[NEJM - medium- and distal-vessel occlusion: the limit of thrombectomy?](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJMe2500492)

> **Citation check — matched.** Real and accurately reported — the submission correctly identified these trials as negative and the increased symptomatic haemorrhage risk. Worth noting for calibration: the same trials were reversed into a positive finding in the Emergency Medicine list submitted earlier, so the lists are not uniformly reliable in the same direction. This topic appeared in BOTH neurology lists; kept here once so it does not appear under both year buttons.

### 2. Long-Term Adaptive Deep Brain Stimulation in Parkinson Disease (ADAPT-PD)

*JAMA Neurology · 22 September 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The first trial of CHRONIC adaptive deep brain stimulation, sensing subthalamic beta-band local field potentials and adjusting stimulation amplitude in real time. 68 participants with moderate-to-advanced Parkinson disease, already implanted and STABLE on continuous DBS at baseline. The primary outcome was met: most achieved good on-time without troublesome dyskinesia relative to their stable continuous stimulation.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Establishes that chronic closed-loop stimulation is tolerable, effective and safe. It does NOT establish superiority: the trial was NONRANDOMISED and open-label, in patients selected for already doing well on continuous DBS — not the population in whom continuous stimulation fails.

**Practical implications.** Lower average energy delivery may extend battery life, and adaptive stimulation may reduce over-stimulation side effects on speech and gait. But setup required reprogramming in most participants, so this does not remove programming visits — it changes what happens in them. No guideline makes sensing-capable generators the preferred primary implant.

[JAMA Neurology - long-term personalized adaptive deep brain stimulation in Parkinson disease](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12455485/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — superiority claimed from a nonrandomised trial.** Real: JAMA Neurology, online 22 September 2025, 68 participants. This claim appeared in THREE of the four lists submitted in that batch, each asserting adaptive DBS 'outperforms' or 'supercedes' continuous DBS. ADAPT-PD is nonrandomised and open-label, and its primary outcome was performance relative to stable continuous DBS — a non-inferiority and feasibility result. Also removed 'eliminates manual programming visits'. Kept here once; it appeared in both neurology lists.

### 3. Anti-Amyloid Monoclonal Antibodies: Subcutaneous Administration and ARIA Risk Management

*NEJM and appropriate use recommendations · 2024-2025*

**The breakthrough.** Subcutaneous autoinjector administration and revised titration schedules for the anti-amyloid antibodies lecanemab and donanemab, aimed at preserving amyloid clearance while reducing amyloid-related imaging abnormalities — ARIA-E (oedema) and ARIA-H (microhaemorrhage).

**Clinical guideline impact.** Appropriate Use Recommendations require APOE ε4 genotyping before treatment, because ε4 homozygotes carry substantially higher ARIA risk, together with a defined schedule of surveillance MRI. Lecanemab's subcutaneous maintenance formulation was authorised in the US in 2025.

**Practical implications.** Practical constraints that determine whether a memory clinic can offer this at all: MRI capacity for baseline and surveillance imaging, a pathway for managing symptomatic ARIA, and the absolute contraindication of anticoagulation in most protocols. The cognitive benefit is real but modest — a slowing of decline, not improvement — and that is the conversation to have before starting.

[Anti-amyloid antibodies: administration and ARIA monitoring](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=lecanemab+donanemab+subcutaneous+ARIA+appropriate+use+recommendations+APOE)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — the submitted title named the wrong drug.** THE TITLE WAS WRONG: the submission headed this 'Subcutaneous Amivantamab and Disease-Modifying Monoclonal Antibodies in Neurodegenerative Guidelines'. Amivantamab is an EGFR/MET bispecific antibody for non-small-cell lung cancer and has no role in neurodegeneration; the body text described anti-amyloid antibodies, so the title appears to be a substitution error. Corrected to lecanemab and donanemab. The specific publications were not resolved and the url is a PubMed title search. Also added the modest effect size and the MRI/anticoagulation constraints, which determine feasibility and were absent. Kept here once; it appeared in both neurology lists.

### 4. Prourokinase versus Standard Care in Mild Acute Ischaemic Stroke

*JAMA Neurology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre trial of the fibrinolytic prourokinase in patients presenting within 4.5 hours with mild acute ischaemic stroke (NIHSS 5 or less) found NO superiority over standard antiplatelet care for 90-day functional outcome.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Consistent with the wider evidence in minor stroke: PRISMS found no benefit from alteplase in non-disabling deficits, and ARAMIS found dual antiplatelet therapy non-inferior to alteplase in minor non-disabling stroke. Thrombolysis has not been shown to help this group.

**Practical implications.** The decision hinges on whether the deficit is DISABLING, not on the NIHSS number — a score of 2 from isolated hand weakness in a surgeon is disabling. For genuinely non-disabling minor stroke, early dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus clopidogrel, per CHANCE and POINT) is the standard, with the loading and duration limits those trials defined.

[Prourokinase in mild acute ischaemic stroke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=prourokinase+mild+ischemic+stroke+randomized+trial+antiplatelet+NIHSS)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific prourokinase trial matching this description was located; the url is a PubMed title search. Retained because the submission's conclusion — no benefit from thrombolysis in mild non-disabling stroke — is well supported by PRISMS and ARAMIS, which have been added as the actual evidence. Added the disabling-versus-non-disabling distinction, which is the decision rule in practice and is not captured by an NIHSS threshold alone.

### 5. European Academy of Neurology Consensus Guidelines on Infectious Encephalitis

*European Journal of Neurology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Revised diagnostic and treatment algorithms for suspected infectious encephalitis, including guidance on the choice between targeted HSV PCR and multiplex panels, and on the timing of empirical antiviral therapy.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The multiplex point matters: syndromic CSF panels are convenient but have lower sensitivity for HSV-1 than a dedicated HSV PCR, and a negative panel has been taken as sufficient to stop aciclovir. Guidance is to use targeted testing where HSV encephalitis is suspected.

**Practical implications.** Start intravenous aciclovir empirically and early — outcome in HSV encephalitis depends on time to treatment more than on anything else, and it should not wait for PCR. A negative early PCR does not exclude HSV: repeat it at 3 to 7 days if suspicion persists, and continue aciclovir meanwhile. Adjunctive corticosteroids are not recommended routinely in HSV encephalitis.

[European Journal of Neurology - EAN guidelines on infectious encephalitis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=European+Academy+of+Neurology+guideline+infectious+encephalitis+aciclovir+HSV+PCR)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific EAN guideline document was located; the url is a PubMed title search, so the submission's 'within 6 hours' figure for aciclovir initiation is not asserted here. The clinical content — targeted rather than multiplex PCR when HSV is suspected, early empirical aciclovir, no routine steroids — is well established. Added the point the submission omitted and which causes real harm: a negative early HSV PCR does not exclude the diagnosis and aciclovir should continue pending a repeat.

### 6. Tenecteplase versus Alteplase for Acute Ischaemic Stroke

*The Lancet and others · AcT 2022; ATTEST-2, TASTE and pooled analyses since*

**The breakthrough.** Tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg as a single intravenous bolus is non-inferior to a one-hour alteplase infusion for 90-day functional outcome, established across several randomised trials and confirmed in pooled analyses.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Tenecteplase has become the preferred thrombolytic across many stroke systems on the strength of equivalent outcomes plus a far simpler delivery. This is a body of non-inferiority evidence, not a demonstration of superiority.

**Practical implications.** The single bolus removes the infusion pump, which matters most for interfacility transfer — a patient can leave for a thrombectomy centre without an infusion running. See also BRIDGE-TNK, which found tenecteplase before thrombectomy better than proceeding straight to thrombectomy, reversing the direction set by DIRECT-MT, MR CLEAN-NO IV and SWIFT DIRECT with alteplase.

[Tenecteplase versus alteplase for acute ischaemic stroke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=tenecteplase+versus+alteplase+acute+ischaemic+stroke+non-inferiority+pooled+analysis)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections.** The conclusion is well supported, though no single pooled analysis was resolved to a citation and the url is a PubMed title search. One softening: 'operationally superior' is fair, but the clinical evidence is NON-INFERIORITY for functional outcome, not superiority, and the entry now distinguishes the two. Added BRIDGE-TNK, since bridging before thrombectomy is where tenecteplase has shown an actual advantage over the prior standard.

### 7. SGLT2 Inhibitors and GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Vascular Cognitive Outcomes

*JAMA and observational cohorts · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Registry and cohort analyses report that treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists is associated with slower progression of cerebral small vessel disease and lower incidence of vascular dementia in high-risk patients.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Fits the broader logic that vascular cognitive impairment is the most modifiable form of cognitive decline, because its risk factors are the ones we already treat. But these are OBSERVATIONAL findings, and confounding by indication is substantial — the patients who get these drugs differ systematically from those who do not.

**Practical implications.** Nothing here changes what to prescribe: the indications for SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists already stand on cardiovascular and renal outcomes. What it supports is treating vascular risk factors seriously as cognitive protection, and coordinating with cardiology and endocrinology rather than managing cognition in isolation. Do not counsel a patient that these drugs are proven to prevent dementia.

[SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists and cognitive outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=SGLT2+inhibitor+GLP-1+receptor+agonist+dementia+cerebral+small+vessel+disease+cohort)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, and the evidence type corrected.** No specific trial or registry publication was located; the url is a PubMed title search. IMPORTANT QUALIFICATION: the submission said 'large-scale prospective registry AND CLINICAL TRIALS demonstrated' that these agents significantly lower progression of small vessel disease and dementia risk. No randomised trial has demonstrated a dementia benefit for either class; the evidence is observational with confounding by indication. 'Prompted cognitive neurology guidelines to incorporate' could not be confirmed and was removed.

### 8. Subcutaneous Efgartigimod in Generalised Myasthenia Gravis

*The Lancet Neurology · 2023*

**The breakthrough.** Subcutaneous co-formulated efgartigimod PH20 achieved total IgG reduction non-inferior to intravenous administration, with comparable improvement in MG-ADL, in acetylcholine receptor antibody-positive generalised myasthenia gravis.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Neonatal Fc receptor blockade removes IgG including pathogenic autoantibodies, and it works within days rather than the months required by conventional immunosuppression — which changes what is possible in a deteriorating patient.

**Practical implications.** A subcutaneous injection replaces an hours-long infusion appointment, which for a cyclical therapy is a substantial reduction in treatment burden. Two caveats: efgartigimod is given in cycles rather than continuously, so symptom return between cycles has to be planned for; and it is expensive, which in practice determines access more than the evidence does.

[The Lancet Neurology - subcutaneous efgartigimod in generalised myasthenia gravis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=efgartigimod+PH20+subcutaneous+generalized+myasthenia+gravis+ADAPT-SC)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year and the guideline claim.** Real (the ADAPT-SC programme), published in Lancet Neurology in 2023 rather than 2025; the specific citation was not resolved and the url is a PubMed title search. ONE CORRECTION: the submission said this 'Reconfigured neuroimmunology practice guidelines to recommend... FcRn blockers as a FIRST-LINE targeted biological option OVER chronic high-dose corticosteroids'. FcRn blockers are not first-line ahead of corticosteroids in current guidance; they are used in inadequately controlled disease. Added the cycle-based dosing and cost, which govern real-world use.

### 9. Long-Term CGRP Monoclonal Antibodies in Refractory Chronic Migraine

*JAMA Neurology and extension studies · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Multi-year extension and real-world data report sustained reduction in monthly headache days with continuous anti-CGRP pathway inhibition, without loss of effect over time and without new safety signals emerging over five years or more.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The absence of tachyphylaxis is the substantive finding, because it undermines the rationale for mandated treatment interruptions. Several payers and health systems have required periodic discontinuation to 'reassess need'.

**Practical implications.** In a patient with a sustained response, a forced treatment holiday risks a return of disabling migraine to no clinical purpose. Two things to hold: CGRP is a vasodilatory peptide, so caution remains in significant cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease, and these agents should be stopped in pregnancy given long half-lives and absent safety data.

[Long-term anti-CGRP therapy in chronic migraine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=CGRP+monoclonal+antibody+long-term+chronic+migraine+extension+real-world+tachyphylaxis)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific long-term publication was located; the url is a PubMed title search, so the 'over 5 years' framing is retained as the submission's claim rather than asserted. The absence of tachyphylaxis is consistent with published extension data. 'Updated headache medicine consensus statements to remove treatment pause mandates' could not be confirmed and was softened. Added the cardiovascular caution and the pregnancy point, neither of which appeared in the submission and both of which bear on who should be on these long term.

### 10. MR-Guided Focused Ultrasound Thalamotomy for Medically Refractory Tremor

*NEJM and long-term registry follow-up · Pivotal trial 2016, with staged bilateral data since*

**The breakthrough.** MR-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy produces durable tremor reduction in medically refractory essential tremor without an incision, and staged bilateral treatment has been evaluated in patients with disabling bilateral tremor.

**Clinical guideline impact.** For a patient unwilling or unable to have intracranial hardware implanted, this is a genuine alternative: no leads, no implanted generator, no infection or hardware failure risk, and no programming visits.

**Practical implications.** The trade-offs run the other way too, and they matter in counselling. Focused ultrasound is ABLATIVE and therefore irreversible and non-adjustable, whereas DBS can be tuned or turned off. Staged bilateral lesioning carries real risk of dysarthria and gait ataxia, which is why staging and interval assessment exist. Skull density ratio determines whether a patient is treatable at all, and a proportion are not.

[MR-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy for essential tremor](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=MRI-guided+focused+ultrasound+thalamotomy+essential+tremor+staged+bilateral+long-term)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — design and missing trade-offs.** The technology and the durability data are real; the pivotal randomised trial was published in NEJM in 2016, and the specific 'long-term randomized registry' in the submission was not located (a registry is not randomised) — the url is a PubMed title search. Three things added because the submission presented this as strictly superior to DBS: ablation is irreversible and non-adjustable where DBS is not, staged bilateral lesioning risks dysarthria and ataxia, and skull density ratio excludes some patients entirely.

## 2026

### 1. Guideline for the Early Management of Patients With Acute Ischaemic Stroke

*Stroke (AHA/ASA) · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A multi-society update consolidating several years of trial evidence: tenecteplase in place of alteplase, thrombectomy eligibility extended to large-core presentations, prehospital routing, and early complication management.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Formalises changes that had already spread through practice — tenecteplase adoption, and large-core thrombectomy after SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT, RESCUE-Japan LIMIT and TENSION.

**Practical implications.** Note the direction the evidence does NOT support: ESCAPE-MeVO, DISTAL and DISCOUNT were all negative in 2025, so eligibility expands to large cores, not to medium and distal vessels. The single tenecteplase bolus is the change with the largest operational effect, because it takes an infusion pump out of an interfacility transfer.

[Stroke - AHA/ASA guideline for the early management of acute ischaemic stroke](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=guideline+early+management+acute+ischemic+stroke+AHA+ASA+Stroke+tenecteplase+thrombectomy)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific 2026 guideline document was located; the url is a PubMed title search. The individual components are well supported independently. THREE CLAIMS CORRECTED across the two submitted versions of this entry: expansion to 'distal vessel occlusions' is contradicted by the 2025 MeVO trials; expansion to 'pediatric patients' could not be confirmed; and 'MANDATES direct transport to endovascular-capable centers' overstates a guideline. This document also appears in the Neurological Surgery 2026 list — a legitimate cross-specialty overlap, since it is central to both.

### 2. Zorevunersen for Dravet Syndrome

*NEJM · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** An antisense oligonucleotide that upregulates expression from the healthy SCN1A allele — targeted RNA medicine rather than symptomatic seizure suppression. Reported reductions in seizure frequency alongside improvements in cognition and behaviour, given intrathecally.

**Clinical guideline impact.** If it holds, this is the first disease-modifying therapy in a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, and the cognitive and behavioural signal is the reason it matters: in Dravet syndrome the developmental impairment is not simply a consequence of seizure burden, and no anti-seizure medicine addresses it.

**Practical implications.** Argues for early genetic testing — SCN1A specifically — in an infant with prolonged febrile seizures or febrile status epilepticus, because a disease-modifying option makes the diagnosis actionable rather than merely explanatory. Note the practical implications of intrathecal administration: repeated lumbar punctures under sedation in a young child, which is a real burden on a family. Sodium channel blockers such as carbamazepine and lamotrigine remain contraindicated in Dravet syndrome, since they worsen seizures.

[Zorevunersen for Dravet syndrome](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=zorevunersen+antisense+oligonucleotide+SCN1A+Dravet+syndrome+trial)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** Zorevunersen is a real investigational SCN1A-targeted antisense oligonucleotide in clinical development, but the specific publication was not located and the url is a PubMed title search. 'Establishes the first paradigm shift in pediatric epilepsy care' softened to a conditional, and 'MANDATES early genetic screening' reduced to what it supports. Added the intrathecal burden and the sodium-channel-blocker contraindication — the latter is the single most consequential prescribing fact in Dravet syndrome and was absent.

### 3. A Randomized Trial of Shunting for Idiopathic Normal-Pressure Hydrocephalus (PENS)

*NEJM · 16 September 2025 (formal publication 4 December 2025)*

**The breakthrough.** The first large randomised, double-blind, PLACEBO-controlled trial of shunting in idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus. 99 participants across the US, Canada and Sweden, all selected for surgery by a positive gait response to temporary CSF drainage, then randomised to an open shunt valve (opening pressure 110 mm H₂O) or a placebo setting (above 400 mm H₂O) on a non-invasively adjustable valve. Gait velocity improved 0.23 m/s with the open shunt versus 0.03 m/s with placebo — treatment difference 0.21 m/s, P<0.001.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Genuinely settles whether shunting works in selected iNPH, and the placebo-valve design is what makes it convincing — both groups had surgery. But read the outcome domains carefully: at 3 months there were significant improvements in gait velocity and in a combined gait-and-balance measure, and NOT in cognition or incontinence.

**Practical implications.** That distinction is the whole counselling conversation. Two of the three classic triad elements did not improve. A family hoping shunting will restore memory needs to hear that this trial specifically did not show that. Note also that every participant had a positive drainage response before enrolment, so the result applies to tap-responsive patients — it does not license shunting on imaging appearance alone. Outcomes were measured at 3 months, so durability is a separate question.

[NEJM - a randomized trial of shunting for idiopathic normal-pressure hydrocephalus](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2503109)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — cognition and continence did not improve.** Real and important: NEJM, online 16 September 2025, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2503109, PMID 40960253, 99 participants. THE CORRECTION MATTERS FOR CONSENT: the submission said the trial demonstrated 'statistically significant and sustained improvements in gait speed, balance, AND INDEPENDENCE', and that it 'put to rest clinical skepticism'. Improvements were in gait velocity and gait/balance; cognition and incontinence did NOT improve — two of the three triad elements. 'Sustained' also overstates outcomes measured at 3 months. Added the tap-responsive selection criterion, which bounds who the result applies to.

### 4. Nipocalimab in Generalised Myasthenia Gravis (Vivacity-MG3)

*The Lancet Neurology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The FcRn blocker nipocalimab produced sustained IgG reduction and improvement in MG-ADL over 24 weeks in antibody-positive generalised myasthenia gravis, with enrolment spanning anti-AChR, anti-MuSK and anti-LRP4 positive patients.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The broader antibody eligibility is the distinguishing feature — much of the FcRn evidence base is in AChR-positive disease, and MuSK-positive patients are a smaller group with fewer options. Nipocalimab is given as continuous dosing rather than in cycles, which differs from efgartigimod.

**Practical implications.** A steroid-sparing option that works within weeks rather than months. Two honest limits: the trial ran 24 weeks, so 'long-term' outcomes are extension data rather than trial results; and FcRn blockade lowers total IgG, so vaccination status should be addressed before starting and infection risk considered during treatment.

[The Lancet Neurology - nipocalimab in generalised myasthenia gravis (Vivacity-MG3)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=nipocalimab+Vivacity-MG3+generalized+myasthenia+gravis+FcRn+trial)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED, with two corrections.** Vivacity-MG3 is a real trial programme but the specific publication was not resolved and the url is a PubMed title search; the submitted 'up to 75%' IgG reduction is therefore not asserted. TWO CORRECTIONS: the submission claimed 'sustained, LONG-TERM improvement... over 24 weeks', which conflates a 24-week trial with long-term data; and 'Updates neuromuscular consensus guidelines to support FcRn inhibition OVER chronic corticosteroids' could not be confirmed — FcRn blockers are used in inadequately controlled disease, not ahead of steroids. Added the IgG-lowering infection consideration.

### 5. Ambroxol as a Treatment for Parkinson Disease Dementia

*JAMA Neurology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A NEGATIVE trial on its primary outcome. This 52-week phase 2 single-centre double-blind placebo-controlled trial of ambroxol — a glucocerebrosidase enhancer — in Parkinson disease dementia showed the drug was safe, well tolerated, and DID engage its target (β-glucocerebrosidase levels were higher at week 26), but it did NOT improve cognition.

**Clinical guideline impact.** There were no significant between-group differences on secondary outcomes either, including the Clinical Dementia Rating, Parkinson's Disease Cognitive Rating Scale, Trail Making and Stroop tests. One trend: total Neuropsychiatric Inventory score worsened in the placebo group while remaining stable on ambroxol.

**Practical implications.** Do not prescribe ambroxol for cognition in Parkinson disease dementia. What the trial does establish is a clean demonstration of target engagement without clinical benefit — which is genuinely informative for the lysosomal-enhancement hypothesis, and is the reason GBA1 genotype-stratified trials are the logical next step rather than a reason to treat now.

[JAMA Neurology - ambroxol as a treatment for Parkinson disease dementia](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/2835760)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REVERSED — the primary outcome was negative.** Real: JAMA Neurology 2025, DOI 10.1001/jamaneurol.2025.1687, PMID 40587145, phase 2, 52 weeks, single centre. The submission said ambroxol 'stabilizes neurofilament/GFAP markers of neurodegeneration, and HALTS COGNITIVE AND PSYCHIATRIC DECLINE', and that the trial 'provides early LEVEL-I trial support' for disease modification. Cognition — the primary outcome — did NOT improve, and no secondary outcome reached significance; the only signal was a trend on the Neuropsychiatric Inventory. A single-centre phase 2 trial is also not level I evidence.

### 6. LDL-C Above Guideline Goals in Stroke Prevention

*JAMA · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A cohort analysis applying the 2026 dyslipidemia guideline's risk-stratified LDL-C thresholds found that a large proportion of high-risk stroke and cardiovascular patients remain above goal.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The gap is a treatment-intensity problem rather than a diagnostic one: these are patients already identified as high risk, already in care, and still not at target — which usually reflects statin monotherapy titration rather than combination therapy.

**Practical implications.** In secondary stroke prevention, treat to the number with combination therapy rather than sequential statin titration: high-intensity statin plus ezetimibe, adding a PCSK9 inhibitor where the target is still missed. The very-high-risk target is LDL-C below 55 mg/dL. Note that the newly approved oral PCSK9 inhibitor removes the injection barrier for patients who declined it.

[JAMA - LDL-C relative to guideline goals in cardiovascular and stroke populations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=LDL-C+above+guideline+goal+PREVENT+risk+equations+secondary+stroke+prevention+cohort)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific national cohort study was located; the url is a PubMed title search, so the submitted 'over 75%' figure is not asserted. The underlying 2026 dyslipidemia guideline and its <55 mg/dL very-high-risk target are independently verified (Circulation DOI 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001423). 'PROMPTED secondary stroke prevention updates to MANDATE immediate combination therapy' could not be confirmed and was softened to what the guideline targets imply.

### 7. Automated Diffusion MRI for Differentiating Atypical Parkinsonian Disorders

*JAMA Neurology · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A prospective multicentre evaluation of automated diffusion MRI analysis to distinguish Parkinson disease from atypical parkinsonian syndromes — multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy and corticobasal syndrome — across different scanner platforms.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Scanner-platform generalisability is the substantive claim, because imaging biomarkers in movement disorders have repeatedly performed well at the developing centre and poorly elsewhere.

**Practical implications.** Useful mainly for the first two years, when clinical features overlap and the distinction changes prognosis and expectations more than treatment. Two cautions: an imaging result supplements clinical assessment rather than replacing it, and a confident early label of MSA or PSP carries a grave prognosis, so a false positive is not a harmless error. Levodopa response over time remains a central discriminator.

[Automated diffusion MRI in atypical parkinsonian disorders](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=automated+diffusion+MRI+parkinsonism+multiple+system+atrophy+progressive+supranuclear+palsy+multicenter+validation)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific multicentre validation publication was located; the url is a PubMed title search, and the named commercial platform has been removed from the title since the citation is unconfirmed. TWO CLAIMS SOFTENED: 'ELIMINATES diagnostic uncertainty in early parkinsonism' is not something a supplementary imaging test does, and 'incorporated... into movement disorder diagnostic pathways' could not be confirmed. Added the asymmetric harm of a false-positive atypical diagnosis, given the prognosis it conveys.
