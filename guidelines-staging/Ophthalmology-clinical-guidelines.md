## 2025

10 entries · **10 with a citation problem — see the checks below**

### 1. Faricimab and Extended Dosing in Neovascular AMD (TENAYA, LUCERNE and Extension)

*The Lancet · 2022, with extension data since*

**The breakthrough.** Dual inhibition of angiopoietin-2 and VEGF-A with faricimab achieved vision outcomes non-inferior to aflibercept in neovascular AMD, with a large majority of patients maintained on 12- or 16-week dosing intervals in the second year.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The durability rather than the potency is the advance. Anti-VEGF therapy works; what limits real-world outcomes is that patients cannot sustain injections every 4 to 8 weeks indefinitely, and undertreatment is the commonest cause of vision loss in nAMD outside a trial.

**Practical implications.** Fewer injections means fewer visits, less transport burden for an elderly patient and less cumulative endophthalmitis risk. Note this is a NON-INFERIORITY result on visual acuity — the gain is in interval length, not in letters read. Extension data (AVONELLE-X) address longer-term durability.

[The Lancet - faricimab in neovascular age-related macular degeneration](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=faricimab+TENAYA+LUCERNE+neovascular+age-related+macular+degeneration+durability)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year and the nature of the result.** Real; TENAYA and LUCERNE were published in The Lancet in 2022, not 2025, and the url is a PubMed title search since the specific extension publication was not resolved — the submitted 'over 80%' figure is therefore not asserted. TWO CORRECTIONS: the trials were designed as NON-INFERIORITY for visual acuity, so 'without loss of visual acuity' is right but 'dual-pathway agents as primary first-line therapies' overstates a guideline position that was not confirmed. Added that the benefit is interval length rather than better vision, which is what a patient should be told.

### 2. Complement Inhibitors for Geographic Atrophy: Real-World Safety

*Ophthalmology / American Journal of Ophthalmology · 2023-2025*

**The breakthrough.** Pegcetacoplan (C3 inhibitor, FDA-approved February 2023) and avacincaptad pegol (C5 inhibitor) slow geographic atrophy lesion growth. Post-marketing surveillance has defined the safety profile that the pivotal trials did not capture.

**Clinical guideline impact.** THE SIGNAL THE PIVOTAL TRIALS MISSED: no cases of retinal vasculitis or occlusive retinal vasculitis occurred in DERBY or OAKS, but the ASRS Research and Safety in Therapeutics Committee reported occlusive retinal vasculitis after intravitreal pegcetacoplan in 2023. Pegcetacoplan now carries labelled risks of retinal vasculitis, retinal vascular occlusion, intraocular inflammation and endophthalmitis, plus a higher rate of neovascular AMD than sham. Pharmacovigilance analysis suggests a narrower risk profile for avacincaptad pegol.

**Practical implications.** Two things this changes in clinic. First, OCT at each visit to detect occult conversion to exudative AMD, which is the expected and manageable risk. Second, and more urgent, a patient with sudden vision loss after a complement inhibitor injection needs same-day assessment for occlusive vasculitis — this is vision-threatening and is not the same as the conversion risk. Counsel on both before the first injection.

[American Journal of Ophthalmology - ocular adverse events with pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol](https://www.ajo.com/article/S0002-9394(25)00199-0/fulltext)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — a vision-threatening risk was omitted.** The submission correctly identified exudative conversion and intraocular inflammation as real-world risks, which is more than most entries manage. THE OMISSION MATTERS: occlusive retinal vasculitis after pegcetacoplan, reported by the ASRS ReST Committee in 2023 and absent from DERBY and OAKS, is the vision-threatening complication that prompted a safety alert — and it is now labelled. Also added the differential risk profile between the two agents. 'AAO Preferred Practice Patterns were updated to MANDATE structured OCT monitoring' could not be confirmed; OCT surveillance is retained as practice rather than mandate.

### 3. Gene Therapy for Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (rAAV2/2-ND4)

*NEJM / ophthalmology trials · RESCUE, REVERSE and REFLECT*

**The breakthrough.** Intravitreal lenadogene nolparvovec, an AAV2 vector carrying wild-type ND4, improved visual acuity in patients with the m.11778G>A ND4 mutation. The striking and unexpected finding was BILATERAL improvement after UNILATERAL injection — a contralateral effect attributed to interocular vector transfer, demonstrated in non-human primates.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The contralateral effect is what makes the trial results difficult to interpret, because the untreated eye was the intended control. It also complicates any claim of a placebo-controlled demonstration of efficacy.

**Practical implications.** Confirmatory mitochondrial genetic testing in subacute bilateral optic neuropathy is worth doing regardless — LHON is frequently misdiagnosed as optic neuritis and treated with steroids to no effect, and the three primary mutations (ND4, ND1, ND6) are straightforward to test for. Note that this therapy is not FDA-approved, and idebenone is the agent with a European approval in LHON.

[Lenadogene nolparvovec gene therapy for ND4 Leber hereditary optic neuropathy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=lenadogene+nolparvovec+rAAV2+ND4+Leber+hereditary+optic+neuropathy+RESCUE+REVERSE+REFLECT)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — approval status and the control problem.** The trial programme is real, but the specific publication was not resolved and the url is a PubMed title search. TWO CORRECTIONS: the submission said phase 3 results 'CONFIRMED... sustained, bilateral visual acuity restoration' and framed this as a disease-modifying intervention now available — the therapy is NOT FDA-approved, and the bilateral improvement after unilateral injection undermined the intended within-patient control, which is central to interpreting the results. 'Restoration' also overstates partial improvement from a very low baseline. Added the misdiagnosis-as-optic-neuritis point, which is the actionable part.

### 4. Sustained-Release Bimatoprost Implant versus Topical Therapy in Open-Angle Glaucoma

*JAMA Ophthalmology / glaucoma trials · ARTEMIS programme; FDA approval 2020*

**The breakthrough.** An intracameral biodegradable bimatoprost implant (Durysta) lowers intraocular pressure without daily drops, addressing the central problem in glaucoma care — that adherence to topical therapy is poor and unmeasurable.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Removes the drop entirely for a period, along with preservative-related ocular surface disease. Sustained-release delivery is a genuine direction of travel in glaucoma, alongside selective laser trabeculoplasty as a first-line alternative to drops (the LiGHT trial).

**Practical implications.** Read the label carefully: Durysta is approved for a SINGLE administration per eye, not repeat dosing, because of corneal endothelial cell loss with repeat implantation — which is the constraint that limits its role. Duration of effect is months rather than the two years the submission claimed. SLT, by contrast, is repeatable and has randomised first-line evidence behind it.

[Bimatoprost intracameral implant for open-angle glaucoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=bimatoprost+implant+Durysta+intracameral+intraocular+pressure+ARTEMIS+corneal+endothelial)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — duration and a labelled restriction.** The implant is real (FDA-approved 2020) but two claims were wrong. The submission said it 'maintain[s] target IOP control for up to two years following a single administration' — the duration of effect is months, and the product is labelled for a SINGLE administration per eye because of endothelial cell loss on repeat dosing. That restriction is the single most important fact about the drug and was absent. 'Glaucoma society guidelines integrated sustained-release systems... as primary initial therapy options' could not be confirmed; SLT's first-line evidence (LiGHT) was added instead.

### 5. Netarsudil–Latanoprost Fixed Combination for Open-Angle Glaucoma and Ocular Hypertension

*American Journal of Ophthalmology · MERCURY trials; FDA approval 2019*

**The breakthrough.** A fixed combination of the Rho-kinase inhibitor netarsudil with latanoprost lowers IOP more than either component alone, by combining increased trabecular outflow (and reduced episcleral venous pressure) with increased uveoscleral outflow.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The mechanistic appeal is real: netarsudil is the only class that targets the trabecular meshwork pharmacologically, which is where the pathology in primary open-angle glaucoma sits.

**Practical implications.** One evening drop replaces two, which helps adherence. The limiting factor in practice is tolerability rather than efficacy: conjunctival hyperaemia occurs in a substantial minority with netarsudil, along with cornea verticillata and subconjunctival haemorrhage, and hyperaemia is the commonest reason patients stop it. Warn the patient at the point of prescribing.

[Netarsudil-latanoprost fixed-dose combination for glaucoma and ocular hypertension](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=netarsudil+latanoprost+fixed+combination+MERCURY+intraocular+pressure+hyperemia)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year.** Real (the MERCURY programme; FDA-approved 2019), not a 2025 trial; the url is a PubMed title search. 'AAO Glaucoma Preferred Practice Patterns incorporated dual-action fixed combinations into step-up algorithms' could not be confirmed and was softened. Added the tolerability profile — hyperaemia, cornea verticillata, subconjunctival haemorrhage — because the submission described only benefits, and hyperaemia is what actually determines whether a patient stays on this drug.

### 6. Low-Concentration Atropine for Childhood Myopia Control (LAMP Study)

*Ophthalmology · LAMP and LAMP2, with extended follow-up*

**The breakthrough.** The Low-Concentration Atropine for Myopia Progression study compared 0.05%, 0.025% and 0.01% atropine against placebo, and found a concentration-dependent effect, with 0.05% most effective at slowing both spherical equivalent progression and axial elongation.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Established 0.05% rather than 0.01% as the better-supported concentration in this population, which reversed a widely adopted practice based on earlier ATOM2 data.

**Practical implications.** Photophobia and near-blur increase with concentration, so 0.05% is a trade-off rather than a free improvement, and children need cycloplegic refraction and axial length monitoring rather than uncorrected acuity checks. Two important caveats: the LAMP population was East Asian children in Hong Kong, and trials in other populations — notably the US MOSAIC and CHAMP studies — have produced less consistent results, with some finding no benefit at 0.01%. Rebound on cessation is real and argues for tapering.

[Ophthalmology - LAMP study, low-concentration atropine for myopia progression](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=LAMP+study+low-concentration+atropine+myopia+progression+0.05%25+axial+elongation)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — generalisability.** LAMP is real and the 0.05% conclusion is accurately reported; the specific 5-year publication was not resolved and the url is a PubMed title search. TWO ADDITIONS the submission omitted, both of which bear on whether to prescribe: the LAMP cohort was East Asian children in Hong Kong, and trials in other populations have been less consistent — so 'shifted baseline pharmacotherapy recommendations' overstates a globally settled position. Also 'minimal rebound effect upon cessation' is contestable; rebound is described and tapering is usual.

### 7. Autonomous AI Screening for Diabetic Retinopathy in Primary Care

*JAMA Network Open and validation studies · FDA clearances from 2018*

**The breakthrough.** Autonomous AI fundus-image analysis detects more-than-mild and vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy without a physician reading the image. IDx-DR (now LumineticsCore) received FDA clearance in 2018 as the first autonomous diagnostic AI in any field of medicine, followed by EyeArt and others.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Screening can happen where the patient already is — the primary care or endocrinology visit — rather than requiring a separate ophthalmology appointment that a large proportion of diabetic patients never attend. That access gap, not diagnostic accuracy, is what causes preventable blindness from diabetes.

**Practical implications.** Two operational realities. A positive result generates a referral, so deploying screening without ophthalmology capacity to absorb it moves the bottleneck rather than removing it. And ungradable images are common — media opacity, small pupils, poor fixation — so a proportion of patients still need dilation or conventional examination. AI screening detects retinopathy; it does not detect the glaucoma or cataract a dilated examination would find.

[Autonomous AI for diabetic retinopathy screening in primary care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=autonomous+artificial+intelligence+diabetic+retinopathy+screening+primary+care+validation+sensitivity)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year.** Real and well established, with FDA clearances from 2018 rather than a 2025 validation trial; the url is a PubMed title search and the submitted 'over 95% sensitivity' figure is not asserted. 'ADA and AAO JOINT guidelines formally endorsed autonomous AI screening as an EQUIVALENT diagnostic pathway to dilated eye exams' overstates — the ADA supports AI screening as an option, and it is not equivalent to a dilated examination, which is the point of the added note about what AI screening does not detect.

### 8. Standalone Trabecular Micro-Bypass Surgery in Open-Angle Glaucoma

*Ophthalmology · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Prospective data on trabecular meshwork micro-stents implanted as a standalone procedure — independent of cataract surgery — reporting durable IOP reduction with endothelial cell loss rates comparable to physiological ageing.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Most MIGS evidence comes from procedures combined with phacoemulsification, where separating the effect of the stent from the effect of lens removal is genuinely difficult. Standalone data address that confound directly, which is why they matter.

**Practical implications.** Expands options for a phakic or pseudophakic patient failing drops but not yet needing trabeculectomy — the group with the fewest good choices. Keep the ceiling in mind: trabecular bypass lowers IOP to episcleral venous pressure at best, so it does not reach the low targets achievable with a filtering procedure, and a patient with advanced disease needing a pressure in the low teens is not a MIGS candidate.

[Ophthalmology - standalone trabecular micro-bypass in open-angle glaucoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=standalone+trabecular+micro-bypass+stent+glaucoma+five-year+endothelial+cell+loss)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** No specific five-year standalone MIGS publication was located; the url is a PubMed title search. 'Reconfigured surgical glaucoma practice standards' could not be confirmed and was softened. Added the physiological ceiling — trabecular bypass cannot lower pressure below episcleral venous pressure — because the submission implied MIGS could substitute for trabeculectomy generally, and for advanced disease requiring low targets it cannot.

### 9. Corneal Cross-Linking Protocols for Progressive Keratoconus

*Cornea and consensus statements · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Comparison of accelerated epithelium-off cross-linking against transepithelial (epi-on) protocols, with attention to UV fluence, riboflavin formulation and oxygen availability — oxygen being the rate-limiting factor that makes higher-irradiance protocols less effective per unit of energy delivered.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Cross-linking halts keratoconus progression, which is a genuine change in the natural history of a disease that used to end in transplantation for a significant minority. The protocol question is about how to deliver it, not whether it works.

**Practical implications.** Know the regulatory position where you practise: in the United States, only the epithelium-off Dresden protocol with Photrexa is FDA-approved, and accelerated and epi-on protocols are used off-label. Accelerated protocols shorten the procedure substantially and are standard in much of Europe. Epi-on is more comfortable and lower-risk for infection but achieves less stromal demarcation depth, so it is generally reserved for thinner corneas.

[Corneal cross-linking protocols for progressive keratoconus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=corneal+cross-linking+accelerated+epithelium-off+transepithelial+keratoconus+consensus+protocol)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — regulatory status.** No specific consensus document was located; the url is a PubMed title search. IMPORTANT CORRECTION: the submission said accelerated cross-linking has 'REPLACED legacy Dresden protocols... as the evidence-based GLOBAL STANDARD of care'. In the US only the epi-off Dresden protocol is FDA-approved, and accelerated and epi-on protocols are off-label — which materially affects what a US surgeon can offer and bill. Added the oxygen-limitation mechanism and the epi-on trade-off, since 'reduces procedure times from 60 to under 20 minutes' presented only the upside.

### 10. Topical Chemotherapy for Ocular Surface Squamous Neoplasia

*American Journal of Ophthalmology and systematic reviews · Established practice*

**The breakthrough.** Topical chemotherapy can achieve complete resolution of ocular surface squamous neoplasia without surgical excision. The three agents in use are MITOMYCIN C, 5-FLUOROURACIL and INTERFERON ALFA-2B.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Topical therapy treats the whole ocular surface rather than a visible lesion with margins, which suits diffuse or limbal disease where excision would sacrifice limbal stem cells. In comparative series, resolution was achieved in about 96% with 5-FU and 81% with interferon, though treatment modality was not an independent predictor on multivariable analysis.

**Practical implications.** Choose on tolerability, since efficacy is broadly comparable. Interferon alfa-2b is the best tolerated but requires refrigeration and is expensive. Mitomycin C is effective but causes conjunctival hyperaemia, punctate epitheliopathy, punctal stenosis and — importantly — limbal stem cell deficiency and scleral thinning, so it needs punctal occlusion and limited cycles. 5-FU sits between the two.

[American Academy of Ophthalmology - topical treatments for ocular surface squamous neoplasia](https://www.aao.org/eyenet/article/treatments-ocular-surface-squamous-neoplasia)

> **Citation check — ⚠ REPLACED — the submitted title named the wrong drugs.** THE TITLE WAS WRONG: the submission headed this 'Trifluridine/Tipiracil and Anti-VEGF Combinations in Ocular Surface Squamous Neoplasia'. Trifluridine/tipiracil is Lonsurf, an ORAL agent for metastatic colorectal and gastric cancer, with no role in OSSN; anti-VEGF is not a standard OSSN therapy either. The established topical agents are mitomycin C, 5-fluorouracil and interferon alfa-2b, and the entry has been rewritten accordingly. This is the SECOND wrong-drug title in these submissions — 'amivantamab' appeared in the neurology list where anti-amyloid antibodies were meant. Also corrected: the submission said topical therapy avoids limbal stem cell deficiency, when mitomycin C is a recognised CAUSE of it.

## 2026

10 entries · **10 with a citation problem — see the checks below**

### 1. GALE Long-Term Extension of Pegcetacoplan in Geographic Atrophy

*Extension of the OAKS and DERBY phase 3 trials · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Long-term continuous treatment data from the GALE extension of OAKS and DERBY, reporting sustained slowing of geographic atrophy lesion growth with C3 inhibition, with effect size increasing over time.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The effect is on ANATOMY — lesion growth rate — not on visual acuity, which is the central limitation of every complement inhibitor to date. Neither OAKS nor DERBY met a functional vision endpoint, and that has not changed.

**Practical implications.** Counsel on what the treatment does and does not do: it slows the enlargement of atrophy, it does not restore or preserve measured acuity in the trials, and it requires injections indefinitely. Weigh that against the labelled risks — retinal vasculitis, retinal vascular occlusion, intraocular inflammation, endophthalmitis, and a higher rate of conversion to neovascular AMD than sham. For a patient with extrafoveal atrophy and years of useful vision to protect, the calculus differs from one with established foveal involvement.

[Pegcetacoplan long-term extension in geographic atrophy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=pegcetacoplan+GALE+extension+geographic+atrophy+OAKS+DERBY+lesion+growth)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — an anatomical endpoint was described as visual benefit.** The GALE extension is real but the specific 5-year publication was not located; the url is a PubMed title search, and the submitted 'reduced lesion growth by approximately 1.5 years' is an unusual formulation that could not be verified. THE CORRECTION: the submission said patients 'experience sustained slowing of VISUAL FIELD LOSS and photoreceptor degradation'. The demonstrated effect is on lesion growth rate; the pivotal trials did not show a functional vision benefit. Added the labelled safety risks, absent here though the 2025 list acknowledged some of them.

### 2. Photobiomodulation in Intermediate Dry AMD (LIGHTSITE III)

*Retina · 2024, with 24-month data*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre double-masked sham-controlled trial of multiwavelength photobiomodulation — 590, 660 and 850 nm light delivered in a series of office sessions — in intermediate dry AMD, reporting improved best-corrected visual acuity and less progression to geographic atrophy in treated eyes.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Non-invasive, no injection, and aimed at the stage before atrophy develops — which is the gap in dry AMD care, since complement inhibitors only address established geographic atrophy.

**Practical implications.** Treat the evidence as promising rather than established. This is a single trial programme from a device manufacturer, the acuity gains are small in absolute terms, and photobiomodulation has a long history of positive small trials in other conditions that did not replicate. It is not a substitute for AREDS2 supplementation, smoking cessation and home Amsler monitoring, which remain the evidence-based core of intermediate dry AMD management.

[Retina - LIGHTSITE III photobiomodulation in dry age-related macular degeneration](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=LIGHTSITE+III+photobiomodulation+dry+age-related+macular+degeneration+multiwavelength)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** LIGHTSITE III is a real trial published in Retina, but this specific 24-month report was not located; the url is a PubMed title search, so the submitted '+6.2 letters at 21 months' figure is not asserted. 'ESTABLISHES non-invasive office-based light therapy as an early-stage option' overstates a single sponsored trial programme, and has been reframed. Added AREDS2 and smoking cessation, which remain the standard of care and which the submission's framing risks displacing.

### 3. High-Dose Aflibercept 8 mg in Neovascular AMD and Diabetic Macular Edema (PULSAR, PHOTON)

*The Lancet / NEJM · 2024; FDA approval August 2023*

**The breakthrough.** Aflibercept 8 mg achieved visual acuity outcomes non-inferior to 2 mg dosing while maintaining most patients on 12- to 16-week intervals — PULSAR in neovascular AMD, PHOTON in diabetic macular edema.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Together with faricimab, shifts the competitive question in retina from potency to durability. Both approaches reduce injection frequency without improving letters read.

**Practical implications.** Fewer visits for the patient and less clinic congestion, which in a real practice translates into fewer patients lost to follow-up — the commonest route to avoidable vision loss in nAMD. Note an important safety point the submission got wrong: intraocular pressure is not the relevant concern, but intraocular INFLAMMATION and the rare risk of retinal vasculitis with any intravitreal agent are, and higher-dose formulations warrant attention to that rather than to IOP.

[High-dose aflibercept 8 mg in neovascular AMD and diabetic macular edema](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=aflibercept+8+mg+PULSAR+PHOTON+neovascular+AMD+diabetic+macular+edema+extended+dosing)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year and a misplaced safety concern.** Real (FDA-approved August 2023, trials published 2024), not a 2026 result; the url is a PubMed title search. TWO CORRECTIONS: 'clinical guidelines now recommend high-dose anti-VEGF agents as FIRST-LINE initial therapy' could not be confirmed and overstates a non-inferiority result. And the submission said extended dosing works 'without sacrificing INTRAOCULAR PRESSURE control' — IOP is not the safety issue with intravitreal anti-VEGF; intraocular inflammation is, and that has been corrected.

### 4. Faricimab Long-Term Extension Data (AVONELLE-X, RHONE-X)

*Extension trials of TENAYA/LUCERNE and YOSEMITE/RHINE · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Multi-year extension outcomes for faricimab in neovascular AMD (AVONELLE-X) and diabetic macular edema (RHONE-X), reporting maintained anatomical drying and vision with continued dual Ang-2/VEGF-A inhibition.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Extension data matter in retina specifically because durability claims made at year 2 have not always survived to year 4, and because patients on these drugs stay on them for the rest of their lives.

**Practical implications.** Switching a patient with persistent subretinal fluid on aflibercept to faricimab is a common and reasonable manoeuvre, and observational data support interval extension after switching. Bear in mind that switch cohorts are selected and unblinded, so reported gains overstate what a randomised comparison would show — regression to the mean does much of the work when you switch a patient at their worst.

[Faricimab long-term extension outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=faricimab+AVONELLE-X+RHONE-X+extension+neovascular+AMD+diabetic+macular+edema)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** AVONELLE-X and RHONE-X are real extension trials but this specific 4-year report was not located; the url is a PubMed title search. 'Dual-pathway inhibition is ENDORSED for treatment-resistant retinal fluid... providing GREATER VASCULAR STABILITY than anti-VEGF monotherapy' could not be confirmed and overstates non-inferiority data. Added the selection-bias caveat on switch studies, which is why real-world switching data read better than randomised comparisons.

### 5. Subretinal Gene Therapy for Neovascular AMD (ABBV-RGX-314: ATMOSPHERE, ASCENT)

*Phase 3 programme · Ongoing*

**The breakthrough.** An AAV8 vector delivering a gene encoding an anti-VEGF Fab fragment, administered subretinally in a single procedure, producing continuous intraocular anti-VEGF protein expression — the aim being to replace repeated injections with one intervention.

**Clinical guideline impact.** If it works, it changes the shape of the disease: nAMD becomes a treated condition rather than a lifelong injection schedule. That is a large if, and the trials are still running.

**Practical implications.** Two things to be clear about with a patient who asks. This is not approved and not available outside trials. And it is not a cure — the vector produces an anti-VEGF protein, so it suppresses neovascular activity for as long as expression continues; it does not repair the macula or reverse existing atrophy or scarring. Subretinal delivery is a vitreoretinal surgical procedure with its own risks, which is a different proposition from an office injection.

[ABBV-RGX-314 subretinal gene therapy for neovascular AMD](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=RGX-314+subretinal+gene+therapy+neovascular+AMD+ATMOSPHERE+ASCENT+anti-VEGF)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — 'cure' language and approval status.** The programme is real and in phase 3, but no results publication was located; the url is a PubMed title search. THE CORRECTION: the submission described this as 'a one-time interventional CURE for neovascular age-related macular degeneration' and said the trials 'VALIDATE gene therapy as a paradigm shift'. The trials are ongoing, the therapy is not approved, and continuous anti-VEGF expression is suppression rather than cure — it does not reverse existing damage. Added that subretinal delivery is a surgical procedure, which the submission mentioned only as a delivery route.

### 6. Travoprost Intracameral Implant (iDose TR) in Open-Angle Glaucoma

*Glaucoma trials; FDA approval December 2023 · 2023-2026*

**The breakthrough.** A titanium intracameral implant releasing travoprost continuously within the anterior chamber over an extended period, anchored at the trabecular meshwork — sustained-release prostaglandin delivery without daily drops.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Part of the same shift as the bimatoprost implant and selective laser trabeculoplasty: moving glaucoma therapy away from a daily action the patient has to remember and toward interventions delivered by the clinician.

**Practical implications.** Addresses non-adherence and preservative-related ocular surface disease, both of which are underestimated causes of progression. Note the constraints that determine who is a candidate: it requires an anterior chamber procedure, is currently indicated for a single administration, and long-term data on repeat dosing and on corneal endothelial safety are limited — the same question that restricted the bimatoprost implant to one administration per eye.

[Travoprost intracameral implant for open-angle glaucoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=travoprost+intracameral+implant+iDose+glaucoma+sustained+release+intraocular+pressure)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year and duration claim.** Real; FDA-approved December 2023 rather than a 2026 development, and the url is a PubMed title search since no specific long-term study was located. TWO CORRECTIONS: 'releasing travoprost continuously... over MULTIPLE YEARS' and 'providing 24/7 IOP control without patient adherence barriers' overstate what is established, and the submission omitted that this is a single-administration product with limited repeat-dosing data. The endothelial-safety question is the same one that limits the bimatoprost implant and belongs here.

### 7. Direct Selective Laser Trabeculoplasty

*Glaucoma trials and AAO guidance · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Automated non-contact direct selective laser trabeculoplasty delivers laser energy through the limbus without a gonioscopy contact lens, in a treatment lasting seconds and requiring less operator skill than conventional SLT.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The relevant background is LiGHT, which established conventional SLT as a reasonable FIRST-LINE alternative to drops in open-angle glaucoma, with better outcomes at 6 years and lower cost. DSLT aims to make that intervention deliverable by more clinicians in more settings.

**Practical implications.** The value proposition is access rather than efficacy — a laser that does not need a skilled gonioscopist widens who can deliver first-line laser therapy. Short-term IOP spikes occur after any laser trabeculoplasty and need prophylaxis and a same-day pressure check. Note that DSLT is a newer technology than conventional SLT and has correspondingly less long-term data behind it.

[Direct selective laser trabeculoplasty in open-angle glaucoma](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=direct+selective+laser+trabeculoplasty+DSLT+glaucoma+non-contact+intraocular+pressure)

> **Citation check — ⚠ NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.** DSLT is a real technology but the specific trials and the AAO Preferred Practice Pattern update were not located; the url is a PubMed title search. Added the LiGHT trial, which is the evidence that makes laser-first glaucoma therapy defensible at all and which the submission did not mention — without it, the entry describes a delivery improvement to an intervention whose rationale is left unstated.

### 8. Point-of-Care Autonomous AI for Diabetic Retinopathy Screening

*British Journal of Ophthalmology · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** Real-world deployment of a fully autonomous AI diabetic retinopathy screening platform with portable cameras and EHR integration, reporting a high diagnostic completion rate without pharmacological pupil dilation in non-ophthalmic settings.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Undilated point-of-care screening integrated into the primary care visit is the configuration that closes the access gap, because it removes both the separate appointment and the hours of blurred vision that follow dilation.

**Practical implications.** A medical assistant can capture the images and the result returns within the visit, so a referral can be made while the patient is still present. Two limits worth stating: a positive result still needs ophthalmology capacity to absorb it, and AI retinopathy screening does not assess for the glaucoma, cataract or macular pathology that a dilated examination would find — so it substitutes for retinopathy screening, not for an eye examination.

### 9. Port Delivery System with Ranibizumab (Susvimo): Real-World Safety

*Registry and post-marketing data · 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A refillable surgically implanted ocular reservoir delivering ranibizumab continuously into the vitreous, refilled in clinic roughly every 6 months — continuous therapy in place of intermittent injections.

**Clinical guideline impact.** THE HISTORY MATTERS AND THE SUBMISSION OMITTED IT: Susvimo was VOLUNTARILY RECALLED from the market in October 2022 after septum dislodgement was identified during refill-exchange procedures, and was reintroduced after implant and procedure changes. Endophthalmitis rates were also higher than with intravitreal injection in the pivotal programme.

**Practical implications.** A genuine option for a patient with high treatment burden who is reliably responsive to ranibizumab. But it is a surgical implant with a specific and non-trivial complication profile — endophthalmitis, conjunctival erosion or retraction, implant-related vitreous haemorrhage — and it requires a surgeon trained in the insertion and refill technique. That combination is why uptake has been limited, and the recall is why patients ask about it.

### 10. Early Lens Extraction versus Laser Peripheral Iridotomy in Primary Angle Closure (EAGLE)

*The Lancet · 2016, with long-term follow-up since*

**The breakthrough.** The EAGLE randomised trial found that clear lens extraction gave better quality-of-life and intraocular pressure outcomes, and was more cost-effective, than laser peripheral iridotomy in patients aged 50 or over with primary angle closure and raised IOP, or primary angle-closure glaucoma.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Reframed primary angle closure as a lens-driven disease in this age group: removing the crystalline lens addresses the anatomical mechanism, where iridotomy addresses only the pupillary-block component.

**Practical implications.** The distinction the submission blurred: EAGLE enrolled patients aged 50 and over with primary angle closure and RAISED IOP, or established angle-closure glaucoma — not everyone with a shallow anterior chamber. An asymptomatic narrow angle without raised pressure is not an EAGLE population, and iridotomy remains appropriate there. Clear lens extraction in a phakic patient with good accommodation also means losing it, which matters more at 51 than at 71.

[The Lancet - EAGLE trial, lens extraction versus laser peripheral iridotomy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=EAGLE+trial+clear+lens+extraction+laser+peripheral+iridotomy+primary+angle+closure+Lancet)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — year and the enrolled population.** EAGLE is real and was published in The Lancet in 2016, not a 2026 update; the url is a PubMed title search since no specific 5-year update was located. THE IMPORTANT CORRECTION: the submission recommended early lens extraction 'for patients over age 50 presenting with primary angle-closure glaucoma OR HIGH INTRAOCULAR PRESSURE WITH SHALLOW ANTERIOR CHAMBERS' — EAGLE required raised IOP or established glaucoma, and extending it to shallow chambers without raised pressure would mean operating on eyes the trial did not study. Added the loss of accommodation, which is the trade-off for a younger patient.
