### 1. Intensity-Modulated Proton Therapy versus IMRT for Oropharyngeal Cancer: Phase III Randomized Trial

*The Lancet · Presented ASCO 2024; published 2025 to 2026*

**The breakthrough.** A US open-label multicentre non-inferiority trial led from MD Anderson randomised 440 patients with stage III or IV oropharyngeal cancer between October 2013 and May 2022 to intensity-modulated proton therapy (n=221) or IMRT (n=219), with concurrent systemic therapy at the tumour board's discretion. IMPT met the primary endpoint of non-inferior progression-free survival. Five-year overall survival was 90.9% with protons against 81.0% with photons — the first randomised evidence of a survival benefit for proton therapy in any disease. Gastrostomy-tube dependence fell by 33% relative and 13% absolute.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Proton therapy has been argued for two decades on dosimetry and toxicity. This is the first trial to attach a survival difference to it, which changes the nature of the coverage argument entirely.

**Practical implications.** Refer to a proton centre for stage III-IV oropharyngeal carcinoma where access allows, and use the gastrostomy figure in the discussion — a 13-point absolute reduction in feeding-tube dependence is what the patient will experience. Read the design honestly: the primary endpoint was PFS non-inferiority, which was met, and the overall survival difference is a secondary finding from an open-label trial. That is not a reason to dismiss it, but it is the reason the result will be debated. Note this is a different trial from TORPEdO, the UK phase 3 whose primary endpoint was toxicity — the two are complementary, not the same study.

[The ASCO Post — IMPT versus IMRT in oropharyngeal cancer](https://ascopost.com/news/january-2026/impt-vs-imrt-in-oropharyngeal-cancer/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — verified, and I was wrong to doubt it.** VERIFIED, INCLUDING THE SURVIVAL CLAIM I EXPECTED TO FAIL. Having established for the otolaryngology list that TORPEdO's result was toxicity rather than survival, I expected a 10% absolute OS gain for protons to be an overstatement. It is not: this is a separate MD Anderson-led phase III trial, 440 patients (221 IMPT / 219 IMRT), randomised October 2013 to May 2022, published in The Lancet, with 5-year overall survival 90.9% versus 81.0% and a 33% relative / 13% absolute reduction in gastrostomy-tube dependence. It is described as the first trial to demonstrate a survival benefit for proton therapy. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The primary endpoint was PFS NON-INFERIORITY, which the trial met; overall survival is a secondary finding in an open-label design, and the entry says so because that is how the result will be challenged. (2) The submitted link is to proton-therapy.org, a proton therapy advocacy site, not The Lancet — replaced with The ASCO Post's report. Added the distinction from TORPEdO so the two trials are not conflated across the app.

### 2. Omitting Regional Nodal Irradiation After Nodal Conversion to ypN0 (NSABP B-51 / RTOG 1304)

*New England Journal of Medicine · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** 1,556 patients whose clinically node-positive breast cancer converted to pathologically node-negative (ypN0) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy were randomised to regional nodal irradiation (n=772) or none (n=784). At a median 59.5 months, the invasive breast cancer recurrence-free interval was 92.7% with irradiation and 91.8% without — hazard ratio 0.88, P = 0.51. Five-year overall survival was 93.6% versus 94.0%.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A de-escalation result with an unusually large denominator and an unambiguous answer. Nodal clearance after chemotherapy is itself the prognostic information; adding radiotherapy to that group buys nothing measurable.

**Practical implications.** In a patient who converts to ypN0, regional nodal irradiation can be omitted — sparing lymphoedema, chest wall fibrosis, and cardiac and pulmonary dose. The eligibility detail that matters is the conversion: this applies to patients who were clinically node-positive before chemotherapy and are pathologically node-negative after it, not to patients node-negative from the outset or with residual nodal disease. Note the follow-up is about five years, so very late recurrence is not yet addressed.

[NEJM — omitting regional nodal irradiation after response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2414859)

> **Citation check — ⚠ matched with corrections — verified, guideline attribution softened.** Verified: NSABP B-51 / RTOG 1304, NEJMoa2414859, 1,556 patients with ypN0 after neoadjuvant chemotherapy randomised to regional nodal irradiation (772) or none (784), median follow-up 59.5 months, invasive breast cancer recurrence-free interval 92.7% versus 91.8% (HR 0.88, P = 0.51), 5-year overall survival 93.6% versus 94.0%. The submission's direction is exactly right and the figures have been added. TWO CORRECTIONS. (1) The submission titles this as 'ASTRO-ASCO-SSO Joint Postmastectomy Radiation Therapy Guidelines' and says it 'formally establishes' the omission; the trial is the evidence, and I could not confirm a 2025 joint guideline update incorporating it, so the trial is the anchor. (2) Added the eligibility boundary — clinically node-positive before chemotherapy, pathologically node-negative after — because that is the population, and the five-year follow-up limit, since a de-escalation decision turns on late as well as early recurrence.

### 3. Neoadjuvant 177Lu-PSMA Before Ablative Radiotherapy in Oligorecurrent Prostate Cancer (LUNAR)

*Journal of Clinical Oncology · ASTRO 2025; published 2025*

**The breakthrough.** An open-label randomised phase II trial enrolled 92 men with hormone-sensitive oligorecurrent prostate cancer and one to five distant lesions on PSMA PET/CT, assigning them to stereotactic ablative radiotherapy alone (n=47) or to two cycles of 177Lu-PNT2002 followed by SBRT (n=45). Median progression-free survival rose from 7.4 months (95% CI 6.0-13.5) to 17.6 months (95% CI 15.0-not reached), hazard ratio 0.37 (0.22-0.61), P < 0.0001, without a significant increase in toxicity. The benefit persisted after adjustment for PSA, prior hormonal therapy and lesion number.

**Clinical guideline impact.** A hazard ratio of 0.37 in a randomised comparison is a large effect, and the logic is clean: SBRT treats what the scan shows, the radioligand treats what it does not.

**Practical implications.** Get the sequence right — the radioligand is given NEOADJUVANTLY, two cycles before SBRT, not concurrently. And get the evidence level right: this is phase II with 92 patients, so it justifies a phase III rather than a change in standard care, and PNT2002 is not approved for this indication. Be careful with the deferral claim: the trial's endpoint is progression-free survival, and a roughly ten-month PFS gain is not the same as ten months of demonstrated ADT deferral unless time to ADT was itself measured.

[JCO — 177Lu-PSMA neoadjuvant to stereotactic ablative radiotherapy for oligorecurrent prostate cancer (LUNAR)](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO-25-01553)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — phase II not III, and one figure wrong.** Verified and the effect is real: the LUNAR trial, JCO 10.1200/JCO-25-01553, presented at ASTRO 2025, 92 patients, SBRT alone (47) versus two cycles of 177Lu-PNT2002 then SBRT (45), median PFS 7.4 to 17.6 months, HR 0.37 (0.22-0.61), P<.0001, no significant added toxicity. FOUR CORRECTIONS. (1) PHASE: submitted as 'A Phase III trial'; LUNAR is phase II, which is the difference between justifying a confirmatory trial and changing practice. (2) FIGURE: 17.6 months, not the 17.3 submitted — and the submission writes '7.4 vs 17.3', which reads as though 7.4 were the treated arm. (3) SEQUENCE: the radioligand is neoadjuvant, given before SBRT, not combined with it. (4) The claim that it 'delays the need for androgen deprivation therapy by a median of 10 additional months' appears to restate the PFS difference as an ADT-deferral result; time to ADT was not confirmed as an endpoint, so it is flagged rather than asserted. Also removed 'establishes a new paradigm in... treatment guidelines' — no guideline follows from a 92-patient phase II.

### 4. Stereotactic Radiosurgery versus Hippocampal-Avoidance Whole-Brain Radiotherapy for 5 to 20 Brain Metastases

*Randomised trial reporting · 2024 to 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A multicentre randomised trial compared stereotactic radiosurgery against hippocampal-avoidance whole-brain radiotherapy in patients with 5 to 20 intact brain metastases — a population historically treated with whole-brain radiotherapy because SRS was reserved for one to four lesions.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The lesion-count threshold for SRS has been a practical convention rather than a biological boundary, and testing it in the 5-to-20 range is where the question actually lives.

**Practical implications.** If SRS is non-inferior for intracranial control in this range, the argument for it is neurocognitive preservation and avoiding whole-brain fatigue. Two things to weigh in a real decision: SRS in a patient with many lesions demands a higher rate of surveillance imaging and salvage treatment for new lesions appearing outside treated sites, and the planning burden scales with lesion number. I could not confirm this specific trial, its journal or its results, so treat the direction as promising rather than settled, and confirm the primary report before changing practice.

[Stereotactic radiosurgery versus hippocampal-avoidance WBRT for multiple brain metastases](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=stereotactic+radiosurgery+versus+hippocampal+avoidance+whole+brain+radiotherapy+5-20+brain+metastases+randomized)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified.** The clinical question is real and actively studied — randomised comparisons of SRS against hippocampal-avoidance WBRT for patients with more than four brain metastases exist, and expanding the SRS indication beyond one to four lesions is a genuine direction of travel. But I did not verify this specific multicentre trial, its publication (the submission says 'ASCO/JAMA', which are different things), or its outcomes, so no result is asserted. The claim that it redefines ASTRO and NCCN brain metastasis protocols is likewise unconfirmed. Added the two practical costs the submission omits — higher surveillance imaging and salvage burden for new lesions outside treated sites, and planning workload scaling with lesion count — since those are what determine whether SRS is deliverable for a patient with 15 metastases.

### 5. Long-Term Outcomes of SBRT versus Surgery for Operable Early-Stage NSCLC

*Long-term follow-up of randomised cohorts · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Extended follow-up of randomised data comparing stereotactic body radiotherapy against lobectomy in operable stage I non-small cell lung cancer addresses the durability question: whether the equivalence seen at three to five years holds at ten.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Durability is the whole argument. SBRT's early equivalence has never been seriously disputed; the objection has always been that surgery wins over a decade, and only long follow-up can answer it.

**Practical implications.** SBRT in one to five fractions with no perioperative mortality is a genuine option for an operable patient who declines surgery or is marginal for it, and it is reasonable to present it that way rather than as a fallback. Two caveats matter in a real conversation: the randomised trials in operable patients closed early with small numbers, so much of the comparative evidence is propensity-matched rather than randomised, and SBRT forgoes pathological nodal staging, which changes what adjuvant therapy decisions can be based on. I could not confirm the specific ten-year dataset the submission cites.

[SBRT versus surgery in operable early-stage NSCLC: long-term outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=SBRT+versus+lobectomy+operable+stage+I+NSCLC+long-term+overall+survival+randomized)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — one structural limitation added.** The comparison is real and long-standing, and SBRT's position as a radical option rather than only a salvage for medically inoperable patients is well argued in the literature. But I did not verify the specific ten-year randomised follow-up the submission describes, so no survival figures are quoted. ADDED, because the submission omits it and it is the standing objection: the randomised trials of SBRT versus surgery in OPERABLE patients closed early with small samples, so much comparative evidence is propensity-matched, and SBRT provides no pathological nodal staging — which affects adjuvant decision-making regardless of local control. Flagged for the physician to supply the primary citation.

### 6. Single-Fraction versus Conventional Postoperative Radiotherapy for Recurrent NSCLC

*Institutional randomised study · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A randomised study reported from Roswell Park compared a single stereotactic fraction after surgery against a conventional multi-week course of postoperative radiotherapy for recurrent non-small cell lung cancer, reporting comparable local control.

**Clinical guideline impact.** The dose-per-fraction question is downstream of a patient question: a single visit versus five weeks of daily attendance is often what decides whether treatment is completed at all.

**Practical implications.** Attractive where travel or performance status is the limiting factor. The caution with extreme hypofractionation is anatomical rather than statistical: single-fraction lung SBRT is least forgiving near central structures — proximal airway, oesophagus, brachial plexus, chest wall — and the toxicity that matters may appear well after the local-control endpoint is read. I could not confirm the specific trial, its size or its follow-up duration, so treat this as a single-institution result rather than a basis for changing protocol.

[Single-fraction stereotactic radiotherapy after surgery for recurrent NSCLC](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=single-fraction+stereotactic+body+radiotherapy+postoperative+recurrent+non-small+cell+lung+cancer+randomized)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified.** Extreme hypofractionation in lung SBRT is a real direction and single-institution randomised work in this area is plausible, but I did not verify this specific Roswell Park study, its sample size, its follow-up or its outcomes, so nothing is asserted beyond the comparison it describes. The submission's claim that it 'accelerates hypofractionation guidelines' is not supported by an institutional study. ADDED: the central-structure caution, because single-fraction delivery near proximal airway, oesophagus, brachial plexus or chest wall is where dose-per-fraction escalation causes harm, and late toxicity typically emerges after the local-control endpoint is reported — a point the submission's 'fewer acute toxicities' framing obscures.

### 7. Adjuvant Pelvic Radiotherapy After Radical Cystectomy in High-Risk Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer (BART)

*Trial reporting, ASTRO 2025 · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** The Bladder Adjuvant RadioTherapy trial tested whether adding postoperative pelvic radiotherapy after radical cystectomy and chemotherapy reduces locoregional recurrence in high-risk locally advanced bladder cancer — a setting where adjuvant local treatment had largely been abandoned.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Pelvic recurrence after cystectomy is morbid and hard to salvage, and adjuvant radiotherapy fell out of use partly on toxicity concerns from older techniques that modern planning has changed.

**Practical implications.** If confirmed, the candidate group is the patient with adverse pathology after cystectomy — positive margins, extravesical or nodal disease — where local relapse risk is concentrated. Weigh it against what a previously irradiated pelvis means for a urinary diversion and for any future treatment in that field. I could not verify the trial's results, its primary endpoint or whether the locoregional benefit translated into a survival difference, and the submitted source is a conference-highlights blog post rather than a primary report.

[ASTRO 2025 highlights — BART adjuvant bladder radiotherapy trial](https://outcomes4me.com/article/cancer-breakthroughs-top-takeaways-from-astro-2025/)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — source is a conference-highlights blog.** The BART trial is a real trial name in this space and the question — adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy after cystectomy in high-risk muscle-invasive bladder cancer — is genuine. I did not verify the results, the primary endpoint, the sample size, or whether reduced locoregional recurrence came with any survival benefit, so the submission's 'proved that... significantly lowers local pelvic recurrence rates' is not asserted. The submitted link is a cancer-news roundup rather than a primary publication or ASTRO abstract. ADDED: the trade-off the submission omits, namely what an irradiated pelvis means for a urinary diversion and for future treatment options in that field, which is the reason adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy fell out of favour in the first place.

### 8. Genomic Classifier-Guided Use of ADT with Salvage Radiotherapy After Prostatectomy

*Biomarker analyses of randomised salvage radiotherapy trials · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Analyses of genomic classifiers and molecular subtype — Decipher score and luminal versus basal subtyping — in men receiving salvage radiotherapy after radical prostatectomy examine whether the benefit of adding androgen deprivation therapy is confined to a biologically identifiable subgroup.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Salvage radiotherapy plus ADT is a decision with real cost — hot flushes, sexual dysfunction, bone and metabolic effects — imposed on many men to benefit some. A test that identifies which is exactly the right target for biomarker work.

**Practical implications.** The concept to hold onto is that these are prognostic-and-predictive analyses of randomised trial cohorts, not a randomised test of biomarker-directed treatment: no trial has yet randomised men to receive ADT according to a classifier result versus standard criteria. So a low-risk classifier result is a contribution to shared decision-making rather than a licence to withhold ADT from a man with adverse clinical features. I could not confirm the specific validation the submission describes or the claim that guidelines now recommend genomic profiling before prescribing ADT with salvage radiotherapy.

[Genomic classifiers in salvage radiotherapy after prostatectomy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Decipher+genomic+classifier+luminal+basal+subtype+salvage+radiotherapy+androgen+deprivation+prostatectomy)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified — study design distinction added.** The field is real: genomic classifier and luminal/basal subtype analyses of randomised salvage radiotherapy cohorts have been published and are the basis for interest in biomarker-guided ADT. I did not verify the specific validation study, so no result is asserted, and the claim that clinical practice guidelines now recommend genomic profiling before prescribing hormone therapy with salvage radiation is unconfirmed. THE IMPORTANT ADDITION is a design distinction the submission elides: these are biomarker analyses OF randomised trials, not randomised trials OF biomarker-directed treatment. Nobody has randomised men to classifier-guided versus standard ADT decisions. The submission's 'allows doctors to avoid unnecessary hormone therapy' presents the stronger claim, and withholding ADT from a man with adverse clinical features on the strength of a subtype is not yet supported.

### 9. Stereotactic Arrhythmia Radioablation for Refractory Ventricular Arrhythmia

*Early-phase and single-centre studies · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** Stereotactic arrhythmia radioablation delivers a single high dose of radiation to an arrhythmogenic substrate identified by electroanatomic mapping and imaging, as a non-invasive alternative to catheter ablation in refractory ventricular arrhythmia.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Extends radiation oncology into a non-malignant indication, and does so for patients who have genuinely run out of options — recurrent ventricular tachycardia after failed catheter ablation, often in advanced heart failure.

**Practical implications.** The population is refractory and high-risk, not routine. Two things bound the enthusiasm: efficacy data come predominantly from small single-centre series and early-phase trials rather than randomised comparison against catheter ablation, and the late-toxicity question is unresolved — the heart, pericardium, coronary vessels, oesophagus and lung all sit in or near the field, and late effects of a single high dose to myocardium accumulate over years in patients now surviving long enough to see them. I could not verify a trial showing equivalent control with fewer adverse events than ablation, nor consensus guideline incorporation.

[Stereotactic arrhythmia radioablation for ventricular tachycardia](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=stereotactic+arrhythmia+radioablation+STAR+ventricular+tachycardia+catheter+ablation)

> **Citation check — ⚠ corrected — evidence level and guideline status overstated.** STAR is real and the Washington University work the submission alludes to is genuine and important. But the submission calls it 'a landmark non-oncologic trial published in major medical literature' showing 'equivalent anti-arrhythmic control with far fewer treatment-related adverse events compared to catheter ablation', and says it is incorporated 'into multidisciplinary electrophysiology and radiation oncology consensus guidelines as a preferred non-invasive option'. I could not verify a randomised comparison against catheter ablation, and no consensus guideline designating STAR preferred was found — the published evidence is dominated by small single-centre series and early-phase studies in patients refractory to ablation. Rewritten to that evidence level. ADDED: the unresolved late-toxicity question, since the submission's '15-minute non-invasive intervention that avoids invasive catheter complications' frames a high single dose to the myocardium as though it carried no risk of its own.

### 10. Olanzapine for Prevention of Radiotherapy-Induced Nausea and Vomiting

*Randomised trial reporting, ASCO 2025 · 2025*

**The breakthrough.** A randomised placebo-controlled trial tested low-dose olanzapine for preventing nausea and vomiting during radiotherapy at moderate-to-high emetic risk, such as upper abdominal and craniospinal irradiation — extending to radiotherapy an agent already established in chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.

**Clinical guideline impact.** Radiotherapy-induced nausea is under-treated relative to the chemotherapy equivalent, and it causes the practical harm of treatment interruption in a course where gaps compromise outcome.

**Practical implications.** Low-dose olanzapine is inexpensive, oral and widely available, which is what makes this worth adopting if confirmed. Dose and sedation are the practical variables — the low doses used for nausea (2.5 to 5 mg at night) are chosen to limit sedation, and getting that wrong is the usual reason patients stop it. Watch for additive sedation with opioids or benzodiazepines. I could not confirm the trial's results or that ASCO and MASCC/ESMO antiemetic guidelines have been updated to incorporate olanzapine for radiotherapy specifically.

[ASCO 2025 — additional noteworthy studies](https://www.asco.org/about-asco/press-center/news-releases/additional-noteworthy-studies-2025-asco-annual-meeting)

> **Citation check — ⚠ citation not independently verified.** The premise is well founded: olanzapine is established for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting and is recommended in ASCO and MASCC/ESMO antiemetic guidance in that setting, so extending it to radiotherapy-induced nausea is a natural and plausible trial. I did not verify the specific phase III trial, its results, or that the guidelines have been updated to cover radiotherapy specifically — the submitted link is an ASCO press release listing noteworthy studies rather than the abstract or paper, so the effect size could not be checked. Rewritten to describe the intervention and the practical dosing point, which is the part a clinician needs and the submission omits: low doses of 2.5 to 5 mg are used to limit sedation, and additive sedation with opioids or benzodiazepines is the common problem.
